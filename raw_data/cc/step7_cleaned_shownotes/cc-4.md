## Summary

Procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) is a critical practice in emergency medicine aimed at reducing patient discomfort and anxiety during procedures while maintaining airway reflexes and hemodynamic stability. The document outlines the continuum of sedation from minimal sedation to general anesthesia, emphasizing the importance of appropriate personnel, monitoring, and equipment to manage potential complications such as respiratory depression and allergic reactions. Key agents used in PSA include fentanyl, midazolam, ketamine, propofol, and etomidate, each with specific indications and side effects. Ketamine, for instance, can cause hypersalivation, laryngospasm, and emergence reactions, while propofol may lead to respiratory depression and hypotension. The document also highlights the importance of patient assessment using the ASA classification and provides guidelines for safe discharge post-sedation. Special considerations are noted for pediatric patients, including the use of distraction techniques and careful dosing.

## Overview

### Key Concepts

- Anxiolysis: Decreased apprehension about a procedure where their level of awareness doesn't change.
- Analgesia: Pain going bye-bye.
- Dissociation: Trance-like state where protective reflexes are maintained such as respiration.
- Sedation: Controlled reduction of environmental awareness.
- Procedural sedation and analgesia: Idea is to decrease LOC without inhibiting oxygenation, airway control.

### Rosens in Perspective

- There are five traditional groups on the continuum of procedural sedation:
  - Minimal sedation = Anxiolysis
  - Moderate sedation "conscious sedation": Drug-induced depression where people are purposefully responding to commands when stimulated. All reflexes and hemodynamics are preserved.
  - Dissociative sedation: Profound analgesia, amnesia, with protective airway and respiratory reflexes maintained. CV stability maintained.
  - Deep sedation: Depression of LOC where patients cannot be roused unless by very painful stimuli. Usually require assistance to maintain an airway and help with ventilation, but airway reflexes would kick in with intubation.
  - General anesthesia: LOC with no rousability even despite painful stimuli. Requires full support with ventilation and cardiovascular support usually needed. No airway reflexes.

## Core Questions

### 1. What are the depths of procedural sedation?

- Minimal sedation = Anxiolysis
- Moderate sedation "conscious sedation": Drug-induced depression where people are purposefully responding to commands when stimulated. All reflexes and hemodynamics are preserved.
- Dissociative sedation: Profound analgesia, amnesia, with protective airway and respiratory reflexes maintained. CV stability maintained.
- Deep sedation: Depression of LOC where patients cannot be roused unless by very painful stimuli. Usually require assistance to maintain an airway and help with ventilation, but airway reflexes would kick in with intubation.
- General anesthesia: LOC with no rousability even despite painful stimuli. Requires full support with ventilation and cardiovascular support usually needed. No airway reflexes.

### 2. Describe the recommended personnel and monitoring during PSA.

- Minimum: ERP & nurse/RT as a support person.
- Supplies ready for allergic reaction, oversedation, respiratory depression, arrest, airway emergency.
- Oxygen, suction, patient monitoring (SpO2, ECG, BP, CO2 detector, vascular access, resuscitation drugs), airway equipment, monitor, ACLS supplies.
- Visual observation of the patient is the best monitoring (i.e., their color, breathing, response to pain, etc.).
- Vitals, ETCO2 (but no evidence showing affected clinical outcomes).

### 3. What are contraindications for PSA in the ED?

- No absolute contraindications: Rosen’s includes a discussion of avoiding it in high-risk patients.
- Use the airway mnemonics to help risk stratify your patients.

### 4. What agents can be used for procedural sedation and analgesia?

- Fentanyl
- Midazolam
- Ketamine
- Propofol
- Etomidate

### 5. Describe ASA classification system.

- A full set of vital signs; mental status assessment; Airway and CV assessment.
- Use LEMON/MOANS.
- A focused anesthetic history with ASA grading is adequate.
- ASA III/IV may need an anesthetic consult.

### 6. Describe appropriate discharge instructions for patients post-procedural sedation.

- Must be back to baseline.
- Should not drive for 12-24 hours.
- Should be supervised for at least 4-8 hours post-procedure.

### 7. List 8 complications / side effects of Ketamine and how some can be treated.

1. Hypersalivation / increased secretions
2. Emesis
3. Laryngospasm
   - Treatment: PPV with BVM PEEP, Jaw thrust, Paralysis and intubation
4. Emergence reactions
   - Treatment: Giving ketamine at a slow rate helps limit the transient apnea, laryngospasm, and emesis/nausea
5. Transient apnea (if given quickly)
6. Increased IOP
7. Worsening psychosis (hallucinogenic effects)
8. Catecholamine surge phenomenon

### 8. List 4 situations when ketamine may be contraindicated.

1. Infants < 3 months
2. Children with ongoing URTIs
3. Psychosis (acute or even well-controlled)
4. Globe rupture (increasing IOP)

### 9. List 4 side effects of propofol in procedural sedation.

1. Respiratory depression
2. Hypotension
3. Injection pain
4. Loss of airway reflexes

## Wisecracks

### What are CAEP, ACEP guidelines for PSAA?

- CAEP has no official policy on PSAA.
- ACEP: Do not delay procedural sedation in adults or pediatrics in the ED based on fasting time.
- Capnography may be used as an adjunct to pulse oximetry and clinical assessment.
- The provider performing the procedure, a nurse or other qualified individual should be present for continuous monitoring.

### How to choose the right drug?

- Questions to Point:
  - Painful or NOT (e.g., fentanyl vs midazolam)?
  - Length of procedure (e.g., IM ketamine for a long peds. facial lac vs. IV propofol for a short reduction)?
  - Anxiolysis vs. immobility (e.g., NOT etomidate)?

### Touch on various reversal agents

- Reversal of opiates and benzos are not recommended as a routine but if you absolutely must, here are your options: Narcan and Flumazenil.

### Mention some pearls about PSAA in kids

- Use distraction as much as possible (child life, parents telling stories).
- Ketamine and propofol are both very safe.
- Use of EMLA or LET when possible.
- Calculate the drug doses carefully and make sure backup equipment is handy.
- Don’t use Chloral hydrate: it has a very poor safety record.
