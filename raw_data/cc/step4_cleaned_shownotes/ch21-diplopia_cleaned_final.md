  Diplopia, though uncommon in emergency settings, can significantly impact patients and is categorized into monocular and binocular types, with the latter being more prevalent. Monocular diplopia often indicates an ocular issue, while binocular diplopia can arise from various pathologies, including ocular muscle dysfunction, cranial nerve palsies (notably CN VI), and central nervous system disorders. Critical causes of diplopia include basilar artery thrombosis and botulism, while emergent conditions encompass vertebral dissection and myasthenia gravis. Urgent causes include brainstem tumors and multiple sclerosis. Diagnosis involves assessing whether diplopia is monocular or binocular and identifying any associated cranial nerve or neuromuscular disorders. Management requires addressing the underlying cause, with particular attention to life-threatening conditions.

## Overview

### Rosens in Perspective

Diplopia is an uncommon presentation to the ED, representing 1.4% of eye emergencies; however, it can be extremely debilitating for patients when it does occur. Diplopia can be:

- Monocular (15%) – persists in one eye even if the other eye is closed
- Binocular (85%) – resolves when either eye is closed

Monocular Diplopia = distortion with the light path through the eye (typically an eye issue)  
Binocular Diplopia = pathology can exist in multiple locations, including:

- Ocular
- Ocular muscle
- Cranial nerves (CN VI palsy a very common cause of diplopia)
- Upper versus lower neuron disease
- Ocular centre dysfunction in CNS
- Brainstem
