  A retrospective observational study conducted across 140 non-specialist acute hospital trusts in England examined whether higher mortality rates for emergency patients admitted on weekends compared to weekdays are due to the admission of fewer and/or sicker patients. The study analyzed 12,670,788 A&E attendances and 4,656,586 emergency admissions, finding no significant difference in risk-adjusted 30-day mortality for weekend A&E attendances. However, weekend admissions were associated with higher risk-adjusted mortality, particularly for direct admissions, with an odds ratio of 1.212. The study highlights that differences in patient demographics and conditions likely contribute to these outcome differences, though it cannot establish causality. The findings suggest that while attending A&E on weekends does not increase mortality risk, being admitted does, emphasizing the need for careful consideration of weekend hospital service provision.

# Higher Mortality Rates Amongst Emergency Patients Admitted to Hospital at Weekends Reflect a Lower Probability of Admission

## Clinical Point
Do higher mortality rates for emergency patients admitted at weekends compared to weekdays result from the admission of fewer and/or sicker patients at weekends?

## Design
- Retrospective observational study
- Patients followed up for 30 days
- Data from Hospital Episode Statistics
- Statistical analysis:
  - T-test for attendances and deaths by day of the week
  - Logistic regression for 30-day mortality
  - C statistic for model fit

## Setting
- All 140 non-specialist acute hospital trusts in England
- Study period: April 2013 to February 2014

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- All admissions

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- None

**Enrollment:**
- 12,670,788 A&E attendances
- 4,656,586 emergency admissions
- 772 records excluded due to missing risk-adjusted variables

## Cases
- Patients admitted at the weekend

## Control
- Patients admitted on a Wednesday

## Outcomes

### A&E Attendances
- Highest on Monday, lowest on Friday (41,416 vs. 36,426)
- No significant difference in weekend vs. weekday attendances (38,254 vs. 37,812)
- 30-day crude mortality lower for weekend presentations (0.99% vs. 1.03%)
- Risk-adjusted 30-day mortality: no significant difference (Odds ratio 1.02, 95% CI: 0.997–1.022)
- Model C statistic: 0.92
- Increased mortality on Sunday and Monday compared to Wednesday

### Admissions via A&E
- Lower admission rate at weekends (27.5% vs. 30%)
- Higher 30-day crude mortality at weekends (3.59% vs. 3.42%)
- Higher risk-adjusted mortality at weekends (Odds ratio 1.054, 95% CI: 1.040–1.069)
- Model C statistic: 0.91

### Direct Admissions
- Fewer admissions at weekends (3402 vs. 1317)
- More likely to be children, young adults, or >90 years old
- Different primary diagnoses at weekends
- Higher crude mortality at weekends (2.72% vs. 2.37%)
- Higher risk-adjusted mortality at weekends (Odds ratio 1.212, 95% CI: 1.162–1.263)
- Model C statistic: 0.92

## Authors’ Conclusions
Higher mortality rates at weekends are found only among admitted patients. Differences in patient demographics and conditions likely contribute to outcome differences.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Large, multi-centre study
  - Appropriate statistical analysis
  - High accuracy risk adjustment models
  - Minimal exclusions
- **Weaknesses**
  - Cannot demonstrate causality, only associations
  - No data on out-of-hospital deaths
  - Unclear handling of readmissions
  - Risk model lacks physiological/biochemical parameters
  - High percentage of unclassifiable/missing diagnoses
  - Proprietary risk-adjustment model not externally validated

## Bottom Line
In a large UK population, attending A&E at the weekend is not associated with a significant difference in risk-adjusted 30-day in-hospital mortality compared to weekdays. However, being admitted at the weekend is associated with increased risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality, particularly for direct admissions.
