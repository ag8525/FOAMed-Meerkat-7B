# Title
Effect of an Early Resuscitation Protocol on In-hospital Mortality Among Adults With Sepsis and Hypotension (SSSP2 Trial)  
**Andrews.**  
JAMA 2017; 318(13): 1233-1240. doi:10.1001/jama.2017.10913

# Clinical Question
In Zambian adults with sepsis and hypotension, does an early resuscitation protocol improve in-hospital mortality compared with usual care?

# Background
- Sepsis protocols have reduced mortality in developed countries.
- High sepsis mortality persists in developing countries.
- Uncertainty exists about the effectiveness of early sepsis resuscitation protocols in resource-limited settings.
- Usual care may lead to under-resuscitation, suggesting a protocol promoting aggressive fluid, blood, and vasopressor use might improve outcomes.
- The FEAST study indicated increased mortality with fluid boluses in African children with sepsis.
- A Zambian trial on fluids and vasopressors in sepsis was halted due to potential harm in patients with hypoxia and tachypnoea.

# Design
- Single-centre, randomised controlled trial
- Permuted block randomisation by computer
- Unblinded participants and clinicians; blinded data analysts
- Allocation concealment with sealed envelopes
- Power: 80% (beta), Significance: 0.05 (alpha)
- Assumed baseline mortality: 65%, Expected ARR: 20%
- Required sample size: 212 patients

# Setting
- 1500-bed national referral university hospital in Zambia
- Study period: October 2012 to November 2013

# Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Adults ≥18 years with sepsis (suspected infection + ≥2 SIRS criteria) and hypotension (SBP ≤90 mm Hg or MAP ≤65 mm Hg)

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Hypoxaemia and severe tachypnoea (O₂ saturation <90%, RR >40/min)
- Gastrointestinal bleeding without fever, CHF exacerbation, ESRD, elevated JVP, incarceration, or need for immediate surgery

**Enrollment:**
- 209 patients received interventions and completed follow-up

# Intervention
- Haemodynamic management for 6 hours post-enrolment
- Initial 2000 ml isotonic crystalloid bolus within 1 hour, followed by 2000 ml over 4 hours (total 4 litres)
- Fluid therapy stopped if:
  - O₂ saturation decreased by 3%
  - RR increased by 5 breaths/min
  - JVP reached 3 cm above sternal angle
  - 4 litres administered
- Dopamine infusion (10 µg/kg/min) if MAP <65 mm Hg after initial bolus
- Blood transfusion to maintain Hb >7 g/dL or with severe pallor

# Control
- Usual care: clinician-determined fluid, vasopressor, and transfusion use
- Typically <2 litres fluid in first 6 hours, <50% received fluid bolus, <20% transfusion, <2% vasopressor

# Outcomes

## Primary Outcome
- In-hospital mortality higher in sepsis protocol group: 48.1% vs 33.0%
  - Absolute Risk Increase (ARI): 15.1% (95% CI: 2.0%-28.3%; p = 0.0344)
  - Relative Risk (RR): 1.46 (95% CI: 1.04-2.05)
  - Number Needed to Harm (NNH): 7

## Secondary Outcomes
- Median hospital stay: 5 days (protocol) vs 7 days (usual care); p = 0.01
- Similar adverse event rates
- More fluid (median 3.5 L vs 2.0 L; p < 0.001) and dopamine use (14.2% vs 1.9%; p < 0.001) in protocol group
- More hypoxemia/tachypnea in protocol group (35.8% vs 22.3%; p = 0.03)
- Greater lactate reduction in protocol group

# Authors’ Conclusions
Early resuscitation with fluids and vasopressors increased in-hospital mortality in a resource-limited setting among adults with sepsis and hypotension, most of whom were HIV positive.

# Strengths
- Addresses sepsis management in developing countries
- Excluded hypoxic patients to mitigate fluid-related harm
- Robust randomisation and allocation concealment
- Intention-to-treat analysis
- Clear separation in fluid and dopamine administration between groups
- Consistent with previous trials in similar settings

# Weaknesses
- Limited generalisability to developed countries
- Single-centre, unblinded study
- Convenience sampling
- Usual care involved dedicated nurse monitoring, not typical in sub-Saharan Africa
- Potential baseline hypotension due to malnutrition
- Dopamine use limits generalisability
- Mortality outcomes limited to 28 days

# Bottom Line
Protocolised fluids and dopamine appear harmful in African patients with severe sepsis and should be abandoned in this setting. Results are not applicable to developed countries due to differences in patient characteristics and resources.
