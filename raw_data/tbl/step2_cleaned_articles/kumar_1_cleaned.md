# Duration of Hypotension Before Initiation of Effective Antimicrobial Therapy as a Critical Determinant of Survival in Human Septic Shock

## Clinical Question
Does a delay in antibiotic administration in patients with septic shock result in increased mortality?

## Design
- Retrospective cohort study
- Conducted across 14 ICUs in 10 hospitals
- Data collected from three cohorts:
  - 1 academic tertiary hospital in Manitoba, Canada (June 1989–April 1999)
  - 7 hospitals in Manitoba, Canada (May 1999–June 2004)
  - 3 academic hospitals in the USA (July 1999–June 2004)

## Setting
- 14 ICUs (4 medical, 4 surgical, 6 mixed)
- 4 academic and 6 community hospitals
- Study period: July 1989 to June 2004

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Adults ≥18 years with septic shock (SCCM/ACP definition 1991)
  - 2+ SIRS criteria + organ dysfunction + sepsis-induced hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation
  - MAP < 65 mmHg or SBP decrease ≥ 40 mmHg

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Other causes of shock
- Sepsis or severe sepsis resolving without therapy or after <2L 0.9% NaCl

**Enrollment:**
- 2,731 patients reviewed; 2,154 included after exclusions

## Intervention
- Timing of antibiotic administration from onset of septic shock
- Onset defined as hypotension persisting despite >2L 0.9% NaCl or transient improvement with fluid resuscitation

## Control
- None

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- Overall hospital mortality: 56.2%
- Survival rates based on timing of effective antimicrobial administration:
  - 82.7% if within 30 minutes
  - 77.2% if within 1 hour
  - 42% if within 6 hours
- Each hour delay in antimicrobial therapy associated with a 7.6% decrease in survival (range 3.6-9.9%)

### Secondary Outcomes
- Univariate analysis: antimicrobial delay significantly affected survival to ICU and hospital discharge (p<0.001)
- Multivariate analysis: time to effective antimicrobial therapy most strongly associated with outcome (p<0.0001)

## Authors’ Conclusions
Effective antimicrobial administration within the first hour of documented hypotension is associated with increased survival to hospital discharge in septic shock patients. Despite increasing mortality with delays, only 50% received antimicrobials within 6 hours.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Large cohort from multiple institutions
  - High clinical relevance
  - Clear documentation of diagnostic criteria
  - Independent review of data by blinded infectious disease/intensivists
- **Weaknesses**
  - Non-randomised design
  - Temporal confounding due to study span (1989–2004)
  - Unclear cohort distribution
  - Lack of data on source control timing
  - Potential bias in data adjudication
  - Limited data on organ dysfunction and vasoactive drugs
  - Supported by pharmaceutical grants (no influence on study design or reporting)

## Bottom Line
This landmark study highlights the critical importance of early and appropriate antibiotic therapy in septic shock. Despite methodological limitations, the findings underscore the necessity of timely antimicrobial administration as part of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign.
