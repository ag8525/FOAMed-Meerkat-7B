# Apneic Oxygenation during Prolonged Laryngoscopy in Obese Patients: A Randomized, Controlled Trial of Nasal Oxygen Administration

## Clinical Question
In obese patients with simulated difficult laryngoscopy, does the use of nasal oxygen prolong the duration that oxygen saturations remain ≥95%?

## Design
- Randomised controlled trial  
- Non-blinded  
- Allocation concealment maintained  
- Power calculation: 30 patients required to detect a difference of ≥60 seconds with a 5% probability of false positive and 20% probability of false negative  

## Setting
- Single university hospital, USA  
- Dates of data collection not reported  

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Elective surgery with general anaesthesia  
- BMI 30-35  

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Female  
- Smoker  
- Cardiac/respiratory disease  
- Reflux  
- Raised intracranial pressure  
- SpO₂ ≤ 97% on 100% oxygen via face mask  
- Grade 3 or 4 mask ventilation or laryngoscopic view (current or past)  

**Enrollment:**
- 33 patients randomised, 30 analysed  

## Intervention
- 5 L/min O₂ via nasal prongs  
- Applied prior to pre-oxygenation and continued until post-intubation  

## Control
- No O₂ via nasal prongs  
- All patients received standardised induction technique  
- Pre-oxygenation with head up 25° and at least 8 vital capacity breaths until end-tidal O₂% > 90%  
- Laryngoscopy performed at 60 seconds  
- If Cormack and Lehane grade 3 or 4, excluded from study  
- If grade 1 or 2, force on laryngoscope blade reduced to simulate a grade 4 view  
- Tracheal intubation at 6 minutes or when SpO₂ decreased to 95%  

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- Patients not given nasal O₂ had a significantly shorter duration of time with SpO₂ ≥ 95% compared to those who received nasal oxygen  

### Secondary Outcomes
- More patients with SpO₂ ≥ 95% at 6 minutes in the nasal O₂ group  
- Lowest SpO₂ recorded was significantly lower in patients without nasal O₂  

## Authors’ Conclusions
Nasal oxygen significantly increased the duration of SpO₂ ≥ 95% during simulated difficult laryngoscopy in obese patients.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Randomised  
  - Maintained allocation concealment  
- **Weaknesses**
  - Single centre  
  - Small sample size  
  - Non-blinded  

## Bottom Line
The addition of nasal oxygen from pre-oxygenation until post-intubation significantly prolonged the duration that oxygen saturations remained ≥ 95% in simulated difficult airways. Despite some methodological limitations, particularly the lack of blinding, the significant benefits and minimal side effects of nasal oxygen suggest it should become standard practice for critically ill patients. If hypoxia occurs during RSI without nasal O₂, the rationale for its omission should be questioned.
