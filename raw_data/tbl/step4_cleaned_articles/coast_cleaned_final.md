# Randomised Controlled Trial of Oxygen Therapy and High-Flow Nasal Therapy in African Children with Pneumonia (COAST Trial)

## Clinical Question
In hypoxic African children with pneumonia, does oxygen therapy reduce mortality compared to a permissive hypoxia strategy? In severely hypoxic children, does high-flow nasal therapy reduce mortality compared to low-flow oxygen therapy?

## Background
Severe pneumonia is a leading cause of mortality in African children under 5 years. In-hospital mortality ranges from 9-16%. Oxygen supply is variable, and many hospitals lack pulse oximeters, leading to oxygen therapy based on non-specific clinical signs. WHO recommends oxygen therapy for severe pneumonia or SpO₂ <90%, but evidence is minimal. Recent studies suggest conservative oxygen strategies may be appropriate.

## Design
- Two-stratum, multi-centre, open-label, fractional-factorial randomised controlled trial
- Severe hypoxaemia stratum: randomised 1:1 to high-flow nasal therapy (HFNT) or low-flow oxygen (LFO)
- Hypoxaemia stratum: randomised 1:1:2 to HFNT, LFO, or permissive hypoxaemia
- Sample size: 4200 children for 90% power to detect a 25% RR for HFNT vs. LFO in severe hypoxaemia and a 33% RR for liberal vs. permissive hypoxaemia
- Randomisation: computer-generated, stratified by trial centre, with opaque envelopes for allocation concealment
- Intention-to-treat analysis

## Setting
- Four hospitals in Uganda and two in Kenya
- Study period: 14 February 2017 to 28 February 2020

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Children aged 28 days to 12 years with respiratory illness, hypoxaemia, and any WHO severe pneumonia criteria

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Known uncorrected cyanotic heart disease
- Chronic lung disease (excluding asthma)
- Oxygen administered at another facility or >3 hours at current hospital
- Previous COAST study enrolment

**Enrollment:**
- 1842 children enrolled
  - Severe hypoxaemia: 388 children (194 HFNT, 194 LFO)
  - Hypoxaemia: 1454 children (363 HFNT, 364 LFO, 727 permissive hypoxaemia)

## Intervention

**HFNT Group:**
- Delivered by AIRVO2 device, initiated at 21% with flow rates and oxygen titrated
- Children unable to tolerate HFNT switched to LFO

**LFO Group:**
- Delivered by nasal cannulae/prongs, escalated to higher flow rates by standard masks

## Control

**Severe Hypoxaemia Stratum:**
- LFO delivered by nasal cannulae/prongs, escalated to higher flow rates by standard masks

**Hypoxaemia Stratum:**
- Permissive hypoxaemia allowed for SpO₂ 80-91%
- LFO commenced if SpO₂ <80%

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcomes
- **48-hour mortality**:
  - Severe hypoxaemia: 9.3% HFNT vs. 13.4% LFO (Adjusted OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.33-1.06; p=0.076)
  - Hypoxaemia: 1.1% HFNT, 2.5% LFO, 1.4% permissive hypoxaemia (Adjusted OR for liberal vs. permissive 1.16, 95% CI 0.49-2.74; p=0.728)

- **28-day mortality**:
  - Severe hypoxaemia: 18.6% HFNT vs. 23.4% LFO
  - Hypoxaemia: 3.3% HFNT, 4.1% LFO, 3.9% permissive hypoxaemia

### Secondary Outcomes
- Longer median duration on respiratory support in HFNT vs. LFO in severe hypoxaemia
- Lower mean oxygen volume used in HFNT vs. LFO in severe hypoxaemia
- Similar median duration of respiratory support in hypoxaemia stratum
- Lower oxygen use in permissive hypoxaemia strategy
- No significant differences in treatment failure, hospital readmissions, or neurocognitive sequelae

## Authors’ Conclusions
The trial supports further investigation into oxygen-sparing strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings. HFNT shows potential mortality benefits in severely hypoxaemic children, warranting further study.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Pragmatic, well-designed study addressing an important clinical question
  - Challenged conventional oxygen therapy practices
  - High compliance and retention rates
- **Weaknesses**
  - Early cessation limited assessment of permissive hypoxaemia and HFNT impact
  - Baseline imbalances and open-label design may introduce bias
  - Generalisability to other settings is uncertain

## Bottom Line
HFNT may offer mortality benefits for severely hypoxaemic African children with pneumonia. Permissive hypoxaemia strategies could be safe for most children, challenging conventional oxygen therapy practices.
