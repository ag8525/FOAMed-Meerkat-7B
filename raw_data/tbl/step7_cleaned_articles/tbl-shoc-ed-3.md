## Summary

The SHoC-ED 3 study investigates the impact of a standardized point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) protocol on resuscitation length, intervention rates, and clinical outcomes in emergency department patients experiencing cardiac arrest. Conducted as a retrospective analysis at a Canadian tertiary center, the study included 223 patients, with 180 undergoing POCUS assessment. Results indicated that POCUS use, particularly when cardiac activity was visualized, led to longer resuscitation efforts and higher rates of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and survival to hospital admission, though not to hospital discharge. Despite these findings, the study's non-randomized design and lack of quality assessment of POCUS examinations limit its conclusions, emphasizing the need for further prospective trials to validate these observations.

## Clinical Point

In patients presenting to the Emergency Department (ED) in cardiac arrest, does a standardised point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) protocol, compared with usual care, affect the length of resuscitation, frequency of interventions, and clinical outcomes?

## Background

The use of POCUS has been increasing in acute medical specialties, with several protocols developed (e.g., RUSH). Despite this, studies show mixed outcomes regarding patient improvement. The SHoC-ED trial previously showed no mortality benefit in patients with undifferentiated shock treated with a standardised POCUS protocol compared to usual care. Retrospective analysis of the MIMIC-III database indicated improved 28-day mortality with transthoracic echocardiography in critically ill patients. In cardiac arrest, POCUS can diagnose aetiology and guide care but may interrupt basic life support.

## Bottom Line

The observation that patients with cardiac activity visualised on POCUS received longer resuscitation attempts, more interventions, and had a higher rate of ROSC is unsurprising. The decision to terminate or stop intervention is a self-fulfilling prophecy. Despite higher rates of ROSC in patients with cardiac activity on POCUS, the rate of survival to hospital discharge falls to that of patients who received no POCUS. I will continue to use POCUS during cardiac arrest management, but priority remains on good quality chest compressions and resuscitation. Further testing by prospective trial is needed as this study is hypothesis-generating, has biological plausibility, and is relevant to health economics and safety.
