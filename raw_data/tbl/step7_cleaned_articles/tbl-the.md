## Summary

The IRIS Cricoid Trial investigated whether cricoid pressure during rapid sequence induction (RSI) of anesthesia prevents pulmonary aspiration compared to a sham procedure. This randomized, double-blinded, non-inferiority trial included 3472 patients at risk for aspiration, with findings showing no significant difference in aspiration rates between the cricoid pressure group (0.6%) and the sham group (0.5%). The study did not demonstrate non-inferiority of the sham procedure, and cricoid pressure was associated with a higher incidence of difficult intubation and increased Cormack-Lehane grades, though these were not statistically significant. The trial underscores the unproven benefits and potential harms of cricoid pressure, indicating a need for further research, particularly in diverse patient populations such as pregnant women.

## Clinical Point

In patients undergoing rapid sequence induction (RSI) of anesthesia, does the use of cricoid pressure versus a sham procedure prevent pulmonary aspiration?

## Background

Cricoid pressure involves applying digital pressure against the cricoid cartilage to compress the esophagus and prevent gastric aspiration. First described by Sellick in 1961, it is often considered a standard of care in RSI, though its efficacy is debated.

## Bottom Line

This RCT failed to demonstrate the non-inferiority of a sham procedure compared with cricoid pressure in preventing pulmonary aspiration during RSI. The study highlights the unproven benefits and potential harms of cricoid pressure, suggesting further research is needed, especially including pregnant women.
