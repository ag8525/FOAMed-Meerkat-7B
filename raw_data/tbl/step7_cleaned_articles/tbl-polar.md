## Summary

The POLAR trial investigated whether early and sustained prophylactic hypothermia improves neurological outcomes in patients with severe blunt traumatic brain injury (TBI) compared to standard care. Conducted across multiple international centers, the study involved 510 patients randomized to either a hypothermia group, where body temperature was reduced to 33°C, or a normothermia group, maintaining temperatures between 36.5-37.5°C. The primary outcome, favorable neurological status at six months, showed no significant difference between the groups, with 48.8% in the hypothermia group and 49.1% in the normothermia group achieving favorable outcomes. Secondary outcomes also revealed no significant differences in mortality or intracranial pressure, but the hypothermia group experienced higher rates of pneumonia and other complications. The study concluded that hypothermia does not improve outcomes in severe TBI and is associated with increased adverse events, suggesting normothermia should be the target in these patients.

## Clinical Point

In patients with severe blunt traumatic brain injury (TBI), does early and sustained cooling compared with standard care improve neurological outcomes at 6 months?

## Background

- TBI is a leading cause of death and disability, with 37% of injury-related mortality in Europe associated with TBI.
- Severe TBI (GCS < 8) often results in unfavourable outcomes.
- Hypothermia is considered neuroprotective, potentially reducing secondary injury.
- Previous studies have shown mixed results; rapid induction of hypothermia may be key.

## Bottom Line

Normothermia should be targeted in severe TBI patients. Hypothermia did not improve outcomes and increased complications.
