## Summary

In a retrospective observational study across 22 hospitals, the initiation of inappropriate empiric antimicrobial therapy in patients with septic shock was associated with a fivefold reduction in survival to hospital discharge. The study included 5715 adult patients with septic shock, defined by infection, persistent hypotension requiring pressors, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome criteria. Appropriate antimicrobial therapy, defined by in vitro activity against isolated pathogens or adherence to local norms for culture-negative cases, was initiated in 80.1% of patients, significantly improving survival rates (52% vs. 10.3%, OR 9.45). The study highlights the critical importance of timely and appropriate antimicrobial therapy in septic shock management, with the greatest impact observed in bloodstream, urinary tract, anaerobic, and yeast infections. Despite its retrospective nature and potential biases, the study underscores the necessity of rapid, appropriate antimicrobial intervention to enhance survival outcomes in septic shock.

## Clinical Point

In patients with septic shock, what is the relationship between the appropriateness of initial empiric antimicrobial therapy and survival?

## Bottom Line

In adult patients with septic shock, inappropriate initial empiric antibiotic therapy is associated with a ~5-fold absolute reduction in survival, even after correcting for co-morbidities, clinical presentation, and organism. The effect size is greatest in patients with primary bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and infections due to anaerobes or yeasts.
