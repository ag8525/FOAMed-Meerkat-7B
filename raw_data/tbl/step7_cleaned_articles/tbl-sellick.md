## Summary

The document discusses the use of cricoid pressure during the induction of anesthesia to prevent regurgitation and pulmonary aspiration of gastric or esophageal contents. The study, conducted at Middlesex Hospital, involved high-risk patients undergoing various surgical procedures. Cricoid pressure was applied by an assistant and maintained until intubation, resulting in no regurgitation in patients where the pressure was maintained, while regurgitation occurred in those where it was released prematurely. The authors conclude that cricoid pressure is effective in controlling regurgitation and preventing gastric distension from positive-pressure ventilation, though it is contraindicated during active vomiting. The study's limitations include its non-randomized design, lack of statistical analysis, and outdated practices such as head-down positioning. Despite its historical significance, the evidence supporting cricoid pressure remains weak, and current guidelines are being revised to potentially reduce its emphasis in rapid sequence induction (RSI).

## Clinical Point

Does the application of cricoid pressure during induction of anaesthesia control the regurgitation and pulmonary aspiration of gastric or oesophageal contents?

## Bottom Line

There is no empirical evidence supporting cricoid pressure beyond this case series and expert opinion (grade 4-5 evidence). Advocates emphasize high mortality risk if aspiration occurs, while opponents highlight poor evidence for safety and potential harm, such as difficulty in intubation and ventilation. The Difficult Airway Society (DAS) is revising guidelines to potentially de-emphasize cricoid pressure in RSI.
