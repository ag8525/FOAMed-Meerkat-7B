# Randomized Comparison of Three Transducer Orientation Approaches for Ultrasound-Guided Internal Jugular Venous Cannulation

## Clinical Question
In patients undergoing internal jugular cannulation under ultrasound guidance, does the use of an oblique approach compared to short- and long-axis approaches increase success rate and decrease complications?

## Design
- Single-centre, prospective, randomised study
- Unblinded
- Randomisation by computer-generated number list
- Intention-to-treat analysis

## Setting
- Single hospital in Spain
- Study period: April 2012 to March 2013

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Patients aged 18 years and above
- Indication for internal jugular vein (IJV) cannulation

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Age <18 years
- History of previous surgical intervention at the cannulation site
- Presence of a central venous catheter (CVC) in the same vein within the past 72 hours
- Infection, haematoma, or surgical emphysema near the puncture site
- Recent cervical trauma with neck immobilisation
- Altered haemostasis (INR >2, platelet count <50,000)
- Agitated or uncooperative patients
- Cannulation planned outside the surgical area
- Urgent cannulation

**Enrollment:**
- 231 patients screened, 220 randomised

## Intervention

**Common Intervention:**
- Performed by three anaesthetists
- Structured training programme: 2-hour lecture, 1-hour bedside teaching, 1-hour supervised practice using phantom
- Each anaesthetist had previously performed 10 successful cannulations in each view
- Linear array probe used
- Procedures performed in theatre or post-anaesthetic care unit
- Full asepsis maintained
- Real-time ultrasound guidance
- Seldinger technique for CVC insertion
- Chest radiograph to assess catheter position and complications

**Approaches:**
- **Short-axis (SAX):** Probe placed transversely over neck; needle introduced perpendicular to transducer's long axis
- **Long-axis (LAX):** Probe placed longitudinally over neck; needle introduced under probe's footprint following its long axis
- **Oblique-axis (OAX):** SAX view obtained first, then probe rotated midway between SAX and LAX; needle introduced under probe's footprint aiming lateral to medial

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- **First needle pass cannulation rate:**
  - Higher success in OAX vs LAX (OR 3.7, 95% CI: 1.71–8)
  - Higher success in SAX vs LAX (OR 2.37, 95% CI: 1.16–4.86)
  - No difference between OAX and SAX

### Secondary Outcomes
- **Number of needle passes until successful cannulation:**
  - More passes required in LAX vs OAX
  - More passes required in LAX vs SAX
  - No difference between OAX and SAX

- **Cannulation time (seconds):**
  - Longer in LAX vs SAX

- **Incidence of mechanical complications:**
  - Higher in SAX compared to LAX and OAX
  - Commonest complication: posterior wall puncture

- **Catheter-related bloodstream infection:**
  - 2 cases in LAX group (not statistically significant)

## Authors’ Conclusions
OAX and SAX are superior to LAX in terms of cannulation quality outcomes. OAX is a safe and effective approach for IJV cannulation and should be encouraged.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Comprehensive training programme for operators
  - Clear definition of a needle pass
  - Analysis between operators
- **Weaknesses**
  - Small number of experienced operators
  - Elective setting
  - Majority of insertions (87%) in anaesthetised patients

## Bottom Line
This study supports that ultrasound-guided IJV cannulation is quicker and technically less demanding in SAX compared to LAX but carries a higher risk of complications. OAX combines advantages of SAX and LAX but requires further evaluation and training.
