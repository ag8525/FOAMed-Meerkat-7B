  Delayed Sequence Intubation (DSI) is a technique evaluated in a prospective observational study involving 62 patients across three hospitals, aimed at improving pre-intubation oxygen saturation in patients who do not tolerate traditional pre-oxygenation methods. The study utilized ketamine to facilitate pre-oxygenation, resulting in a significant increase in SpO₂ from 88.9% to 98.8% prior to muscle relaxation. The intervention involved positioning patients head-up, using a non-rebreather mask and nasal cannula, and switching to CPAP if SpO₂ remained below 95%. No major complications were reported, and the study concluded that DSI is a safe and effective method for improving oxygenation in challenging intubation scenarios, though further randomized controlled trials are recommended to validate these findings.

# Delayed Sequence Intubation: A Prospective Observational Study

## Clinical Question
In patients requiring emergency intubation who do not tolerate pre-oxygenation, does the use of 'delayed sequence intubation' (DSI) improve pre-intubation oxygen saturations (SpO₂)?

## Design
- Prospective observational case series
- Non-randomised
- Non-blinded
- Convenience sample

## Setting
- 3 hospitals in the USA/Denmark
- Level 1 trauma centre or quaternary referral centre
- Study period: May 2011 to December 2013

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Adult patients spontaneously breathing who required emergency intubation
- Not predicted to have a difficult airway
- Did not tolerate pre-oxygenation despite maximal conventional attempts

**Enrollment:**
- 62 patients included in analysis out of 64 enrolled

## Intervention
- Ketamine: Initial dose 1 mg/kg, followed by 0.5 mg/kg aliquots until dissociated
- Positioned ≥30 degrees head up and pre-oxygenated with non-rebreather mask and nasal cannula
- If SpO₂ <95%, switched to CPAP 5–15 cm H₂O
  - 39 patients received pre-oxygenation with CPAP
  - 23 patients did not require CPAP
- After 3 minutes, paralysed with suxamethonium/rocuronium and intubated after further 45–60 seconds

## Control
- No control group

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- Difference in SpO₂ after pre-oxygenation with:
  - Standard attempts vs. ketamine (just prior to muscle relaxation)
  - Significant increase post-ketamine: 88.9% vs. 98.8% (increase of 8.9%, 95% CI: 6.4–10.9)

### Secondary Outcomes
- Subgroup analysis: SpO₂ after pre-oxygenation with ketamine in patients with initial SpO₂ ≤93% after standard attempts
  - All increased SpO₂ to >93%
- Complications: No pre-muscle relaxant apnoea, peri-intubation emesis, cardiac arrest, or death
  - 2 patients had decreased SpO₂ after ketamine (99% to 98% and 95% to 93%)
  - Both received pre-oxygenation by non-rebreather masks without nasal cannula oxygen during dissociation
- Post-hoc analysis: 2 patients with asthma improved sufficiently with ketamine and CPAP, avoiding intubation

## Authors’ Conclusions
DSI safely allowed pre-oxygenation in patients who did not tolerate traditional methods.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Multi-centre
  - Prospective
  - Standard protocol for DSI
  - Sensitivity analysis for non-parametric distribution
- **Weaknesses**
  - No control group
  - Non-blinded
  - Convenience sample, potential selection bias
  - DSI performed by clinicians experienced with ketamine sedation; results may not generalise to less experienced practitioners

## Bottom Line
In this prospective observational study, DSI significantly improved oxygen saturations in patients requiring emergency intubation who did not tolerate traditional pre-oxygenation methods. A randomised controlled trial is warranted to compare DSI with other techniques. Meanwhile, DSI can be considered in selected cases based on this level 4 evidence.
