  The document discusses the use of cricoid pressure during the induction of anesthesia to prevent regurgitation and pulmonary aspiration of gastric or esophageal contents. The study, conducted at Middlesex Hospital, involved high-risk patients undergoing various surgical procedures. Cricoid pressure was applied by an assistant and maintained until intubation, resulting in no regurgitation in patients where the pressure was maintained, while regurgitation occurred in those where it was released prematurely. The authors conclude that cricoid pressure is effective in controlling regurgitation and preventing gastric distension from positive-pressure ventilation, though it is contraindicated during active vomiting. The study's limitations include its non-randomized design, lack of statistical analysis, and outdated practices such as head-down positioning. Despite its historical significance, the evidence supporting cricoid pressure remains weak, and current guidelines are being revised to potentially reduce its emphasis in rapid sequence induction (RSI).

# Cricoid Pressure to Control Regurgitation of Stomach Contents During Induction of Anaesthesia

## Clinical Question
Does the application of cricoid pressure during induction of anaesthesia control the regurgitation and pulmonary aspiration of gastric or oesophageal contents?

## Design
- Case series
- Single-centre study

## Setting
- Middlesex Hospital, London

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Patients at high risk of regurgitation of oesophageal or stomach contents

**Patient Groups:**
- 18 laparotomy for intestinal obstruction
- 3 gastrectomy for pyloric stenosis
- 2 oesophagoscopy for achalasia of the cardia
- 2 forceps delivery
- 1 resection for oesophageal cancer

## Intervention
- Application of cricoid pressure by an assistant (nurse or midwife)
- Cricoid palpated and lightly held between thumb and second finger
- Firm pressure applied as anaesthesia begins and maintained until intubation and cuff inflation
- Ryle’s or oesophageal tubes aspirated and removed before induction
- Patients positioned in slight head-down tilt
- Pre-oxygenation advised
- Use of intravenous barbiturate and muscle relaxant; inhalational induction for seriously ill patients
- Short-acting muscle relaxant for faster intubation

## Control
- No control group

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- Regurgitation of oesophageal or gastric content visualised into the pharynx
  - 23 patients: cricoid pressure maintained until endotracheal cuff inflated; 0 witnessed regurgitation
  - 3 patients: cricoid pressure released after induction and before intubation; all 3 had regurgitation

### Secondary Outcome
- Author concludes cricoid pressure prevents gastric distension from bag-valve-mask ventilation, though not detailed in results

## Authors’ Conclusions
Cricoid pressure can control regurgitation of stomach or oesophageal contents during induction of anaesthesia and prevent gastric distension from positive-pressure ventilation. It is contraindicated during active vomiting.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Important historical case series
  - Technique based on contemporary practice, easily applicable at the time
- **Weaknesses**
  - Not randomised
  - Only 3 patients had cricoid pressure released; no statistical analysis possible
  - Removal of orogastric tube in high-risk patients is questionable by modern standards
  - Head-down positioning not current practice; may increase regurgitation risk
  - Force used with cricoid pressure not specified or validated
  - No mention of anaesthetic agents or grades of anaesthetists involved
  - Variability in practice, including use of positive pressure ventilation, affects outcomes
  - Grade 4 evidence; low strength of recommendation by current standards

## Bottom Line
There is no empirical evidence supporting cricoid pressure beyond this case series and expert opinion (grade 4-5 evidence). Advocates emphasize high mortality risk if aspiration occurs, while opponents highlight poor evidence for safety and potential harm, such as difficulty in intubation and ventilation. The Difficult Airway Society (DAS) is revising guidelines to potentially de-emphasize cricoid pressure in RSI.
