# A Randomised Trial of High-Flow Oxygen Therapy in Infants with Bronchiolitis

## Clinical Question
In infants (<12 months) with bronchiolitis and hypoxaemia (saturations <92%) in the emergency department or general paediatric ward, does early treatment with high-flow therapy versus standard oxygen therapy result in fewer infants having treatment failure that leads to escalation of care?

## Background
Bronchiolitis is the leading cause of non-elective hospital admissions in infants, with a significant increase in intensive care unit admissions. There are no disease-modifying therapies for bronchiolitis, and the effectiveness of high-flow therapy in reducing intensive care admissions or intubation has been primarily reported in observational studies.

## Design
- Non-blinded, multicentre randomised controlled trial
- Randomisation via computer-generated sequence in blocks of 10, stratified by centre
- Sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes used for allocation
- Investigators blinded to trial outcomes until data lock in December 2016

## Setting
- 17 hospitals in Australia and New Zealand (6 tertiary, 11 regional/metropolitan)
- Study period: October 2013 to August 2016

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Infants <12 months with clinical signs of bronchiolitis requiring supplemental oxygen

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Immediate need for respiratory support/ICU admission
- Cyanotic heart disease, base of skull fracture, upper airway obstruction, craniofacial malformations
- Receiving supplemental oxygen therapy at home

**Enrollment:**
- 20,795 infants screened; 18,578 excluded
- 1,638 infants randomised; 1,472 included in analyses

## Intervention

**High-Flow Oxygen Therapy Group:**
- Flow of 2 L/kg/min to a maximum of 25 L/min
- Weaning: Cease if saturations maintained in target range for >4 hours with FiO₂ 0.21
- Feeding: Nasogastric tube inserted and vented every 4 hours; no oral feeding unless stable

## Control

**Standard Oxygen Therapy Group:**
- Supplemental oxygen to a maximum of 2 L/min to maintain saturations 92-98%
- Weaning: Cease if saturations maintained in target range for >4 hours on air
- Feeding as per clinician discretion

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- **Treatment failure:**  
  - Standard therapy: 167/733 (23%)  
  - High-flow therapy: 87/739 (12%)  
  - Risk difference: -11 percentage points (95% CI: -15 to -7; p < 0.001)  
  - Number needed to treat: 9  

### Secondary Outcomes
- No significant differences in:
  - ICU admissions/transfers: 9% vs. 12% (OR 1.37; 95% CI 0.96 to 1.95; p = 0.08)
  - Duration of ICU stay: 2.72 vs. 2.63 days (Mean Difference -0.09; 95% CI -0.74 to 0.55; p = 0.78)
  - Duration of hospital stay: 2.94 vs. 3.12 days (OR 0.18; 95% CI -0.09 to 0.44; p = 0.19)
  - Duration of oxygen therapy: 1.87 vs. 1.81 days (Mean difference -0.06; 95% CI -0.28 to 0.16; p = 0.61)
  - Intubation rate: 1% vs. 1% (OR 1.99; 95% CI 0.6 to 6.65; p = 0.39)

**Adverse Events:**
- No differences between groups: Apnoeas (3 each), Pneumothorax (1 each)

## Authors’ Conclusions
High-flow oxygen therapy significantly reduces the rate of escalation of care due to treatment failure in infants with bronchiolitis compared to standard therapy.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Large, multicentre RCT addressing a common paediatric issue
  - Adequately powered to detect a 5% absolute reduction in treatment failure
  - Well-balanced intervention and control arms
  - Objective measurements of clinical deterioration used for treatment failure
  - Pragmatic design reflecting current clinical practice

- **Weaknesses**
  - Unblinded study design
  - Composite primary outcome may have limited clinical relevance
  - Potential selection bias with missed enrolment opportunities
  - Clinicians could escalate care based on clinical judgement, introducing detection bias
  - Differences in escalation thresholds between centres

## Bottom Line
High-flow therapy reduces treatment failure in infants with bronchiolitis but does not significantly impact ICU admissions or other secondary outcomes. It may be suitable for ward use with appropriate education and support.
