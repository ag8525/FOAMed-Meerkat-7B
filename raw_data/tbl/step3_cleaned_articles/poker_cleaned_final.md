# Propofol or Ketofol for Procedural Sedation and Analgesia in Emergency Medicine: The POKER Study

## Clinical Question
In adult Emergency Department patients requiring intravenous sedation for noxious procedures, does a combination of propofol and ketamine (ketofol), compared with single-agent propofol, cause fewer adverse respiratory events?

## Design
- Randomised, double-blinded clinical trial
- Convenience sample
- Block randomisation in blocks of four using a web-based program
- Study packs pre-prepared; medical staff blinded to contents
- Powered to demonstrate a reduction in primary endpoint from 20% to 10%
- Sample size: 526 (263 in each arm)
- Intention-to-treat analysis
- Analysis with chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test

## Setting
- Three Australian Emergency Departments (urban, rural, and tertiary hospitals)
- Study period: April 2013 to April 2015

## Population

**Inclusion Criteria:**
- Adult patients requiring deep procedural sedation for painful procedures in the ED

**Exclusion Criteria:**
- Inability to provide informed consent
- Pregnancy
- Allergy to study medications
- Reduced level of consciousness
- BP >160/90 mmHg
- Severe cardiac or systemic disease

**Enrollment:**
- 591 patients randomised from 1028 screened
- 14 patients excluded post-randomisation for meeting exclusion criteria

## Intervention

**Ketofol Group:**
- 100 mg ketamine and 100 mg propofol in 20 ml syringe
- Initial bolus: 0.05 ml/kg
- Additional injections: 0.025 ml/kg, no more frequently than every 60 seconds

## Control

**Propofol Group:**
- 200 mg propofol in 20 ml syringe
- Initial bolus: 0.05 ml/kg
- Additional injections: 0.025 ml/kg, no more frequently than every 60 seconds

**Common to Both Groups:**
- Titrated IV opiates encouraged for pain prior to randomisation
- Procedure commenced at physician discretion
- Vital signs and sedation depth recorded every three minutes
- Open-label propofol used if additional sedation required after 20 ml of study medication
- Prophylactic oxygen administration at physician discretion

## Outcomes

### Primary Outcome
- Occurrence of adverse respiratory events (SpO₂ <93%, RR <8, apnoea, laryngospasm, aspiration, need for rescue intervention)
- No significant difference: Ketofol 7% vs. Propofol 9% (difference 2%, 95% CI: -2 to 6%)

### Secondary Outcomes
- **Hypotension (BP <90 mmHg):** More common in propofol group (1% vs. 8%, difference 7%, 95% CI: 4-10%)
- **Time to discharge score of 7:** Longer in ketofol group (33 min vs. 24 min, difference 9 min, 95% CI: 7-11)
- **Emergence delirium:** More likely in ketofol group (27% vs. 20%, difference 7%, 95% CI: 4-10)
- **Pain score 30 min post-procedure:** Higher in propofol group (0/10 vs. 3/10, difference 3, 95% CI: 2-3)
- No difference in pain during procedure, recall, or patient satisfaction

## Authors’ Conclusions
Adult procedural sedation using ketofol versus propofol alone results in similar frequency of adverse respiratory events requiring intervention. Propofol was associated with a slightly higher incidence of hypotension, which is of dubious clinical consequence, and patients were highly satisfied with both agents.

## Strengths and Weaknesses
- **Strengths**
  - Investigated an important clinical question relevant to daily ED practice
  - Generalisable to the UK population
  - Robust efforts to conceal study drug and reduce bias
  - Appropriate primary outcomes with clinically significant endpoints
  - Relevant secondary endpoints for future research
- **Weaknesses**
  - Potential selection bias due to convenience sampling
  - Initial sedative doses lower than routine clinical practice
  - More opiates given to ketofol group prior to inclusion
  - Possible bias from detectable ketamine effects
  - Hypotension in control group may not be clinically benign
  - Preoxygenation flow rate lower than expected

## Bottom Line
This study suggests that ketofol and propofol are equally safe regarding respiratory complications for procedural sedation in the ED, provided patients receive adequate analgesia and are sedated using an appropriate protocol. Clinicians should continue to use the agent they are most familiar with.
