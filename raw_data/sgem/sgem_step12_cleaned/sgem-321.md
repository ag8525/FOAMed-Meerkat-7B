## Summary
A 70-year-old man with a history of atrial fibrillation and hypertension, on apixaban, presents with persistent epistaxis unresponsive to initial conservative measures. The case highlights the management of epistaxis in patients on anticoagulants, emphasizing the potential role of tranexamic acid (TXA) as an adjunctive treatment. A recent multicenter, double-blind RCT (NoPAC) evaluated the efficacy of topical TXA in reducing the need for anterior nasal packing in refractory epistaxis, finding no significant difference compared to placebo. Despite the study's limitations, including a narrow patient cohort and lower TXA dosing than previous studies, it suggests that TXA may not be universally effective in this context. Clinicians should consider individual patient factors and existing literature when deciding on TXA use for epistaxis management.

## Case
A 70-year-old man presents with epistaxis. He has a history of atrial fibrillation and hypertension. His medications include apixaban, metoprolol, and perindopril. He states that the nosebleed started that morning suddenly. Vitals are; a blood pressure of 145/73 mmHg, heart rate 64 beats/minute, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. You apply direct pressure for 10 minutes, but the bleeding continues. You administer phenylephrine topically, reapply pressure, and consider the use of tranexamic acid (TXA).

## Background
Epistaxis, a prevalent condition with a lifetime incidence of 60%, accounts for over 450,000 Emergency Department visits annually, particularly affecting the elderly with often unidentified causes. Standard treatment for anterior epistaxis includes applying pressure, local vasoconstrictors like oxymetazoline, silver nitrate, and anterior nasal packing, though the latter can be uncomfortable, especially for patients on antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Recently, topical tranexamic acid (TXA) has been introduced as a treatment option, showing promise in small studies despite limitations such as single-center designs, small sample sizes, and lack of blinding. TXA is noted for its minimal safety concerns and cost-effectiveness.

## Clinical Point
Does the use of topical, intranasal TXA reduce the need for application of anterior nasal packing in ED patients with epistaxis who fail conservative management?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Evidence on the use of TXA in epistaxis is inconsistent and it depends on the patient population and treatment protocol whether or not it demonstrates efficacy.

## Case Resolution
You decide to use the protocol that has been shown to be successful in a few other RCTs. You soak a small gauze with 500mg of TXA and place it in the affected nostril. Ten minutes later, the patient’s bleeding has stopped. You watch them for another 30 minutes and then discharge the patient home with clear directions on when to return to the emergency department.

## Clinical Application
Each new study needs to be considered individually and in context of the other relevant literature. Three other RCTs have been published on this topic. All three of these trials demonstrated efficacy in treating patients with epistaxis. They too have limitations: single center, unclear what care was provided prior to the intervention, smaller sample size, and being unblinded. They also used a higher initial dose of 500mg of TXA instead of 200mg TXA (Zahed 2013, Zahed 2017, and Akkan2019).
This study definitely calls into Point the routine use of TXA in this patient with epistaxis. Each clinician will need to interpret the literature for themselves, reflect upon their clinical experience, and ask the patients what they would prefer. Essentially, we would encourage clinicians to practice evidence-based medicine.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We have a medicine called TXA that may help stop your nosebleed faster and get you out of the emergency department quicker. It requires that I place this piece of cotton soaked in the medication up your nose and leave it there for about ten minutes. No major side effects have been identified in any of the research studies. Would you like to try it and see if it stops your bleeding?
