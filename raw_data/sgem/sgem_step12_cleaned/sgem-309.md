## Summary
The document discusses the effectiveness of public mask-wearing in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection, focusing on a Danish randomized controlled trial (RCT) that evaluated the impact of recommending surgical masks to adults in a low-prevalence setting. The trial found no significant reduction in infection rates among mask wearers compared to non-wearers, with a primary outcome showing a 0.3% difference in infection rates (1.8% vs. 2.1%). Methodological concerns include adherence issues and the study's focus on personal protection rather than source control. The authors conclude that mask-wearing did not significantly protect wearers, but the broader Point of masks' population-level impact remains unanswered. The document emphasizes the need for cautious interpretation of these results and continued advocacy for mask mandates based on existing evidence and precautionary principles.

## Case
Alberta is the last province in Canada that has yet to enact a mandatory mask policy. Should they do it?

## Background
The utility and risk of facial masks in preventing viral transmission during respiratory pandemics remain debated, with experts like Dr. Trisha Greenhalgh advocating for stricter mask regulations based on the precautionary principle. The novel nature of SARS-CoV-2 necessitated extrapolation from prior research on masks and respiratory illnesses, despite limited specific evidence. Historically, masks have been used for over a century as an infectious disease strategy, originating with surgeons in the late 1890s. A cluster randomized control trial by MacIntyre et al. (2015) found that medical masks were more effective than cloth masks or no masks in reducing clinical respiratory illness and laboratory-confirmed infections among healthcare workers, though it did not address mask use in the general public. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Chu et al. (2020) suggested that face masks, particularly N95 respirators, significantly reduce infection risk, though the evidence was of low certainty due to the observational nature of included studies. While potential harms of mask-wearing are minimal, with low pre-test probability of individual harm, the need for more robust evidence persists, as highlighted by the first randomized control trial on public mask-wearing to prevent COVID-19 transmission.

## Clinical Point
Does recommending surgical mask use outside the home reduces wearers’ risk for SARS-CoV-2 infection in a setting where masks were uncommon and not among recommended public health measures?

## SGEM Bottom Line
We do not have high-quality evidence that public mask wearing prevents the wearer from getting COVID19. We need to be careful not to overinterpret these results and incorrectly conclude public mask wearing does not work.

## Case Resolution
Joe will continue to advocate for Alberta to enact a mandate for people to wear a mask in public.

## Clinical Application
This clustered randomized control trial asked a very narrow Point that makes it hard to apply clinically to the broader Point of does public mask wearing lower the burden of COVID19 in the general population. It was reassuring that they did not see any signal of harm.
However, we still need to make recommendations based on the best evidence even if we don’t have high-quality evidence.
