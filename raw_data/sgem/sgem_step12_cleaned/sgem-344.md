## Summary
The document discusses the management of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) patients who achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) but do not exhibit ST-segment elevation on their post-ROSC EKG. The study, a randomized controlled trial, evaluated whether immediate coronary angiography improves outcomes compared to a delayed approach. The trial found no significant difference in 30-day all-cause mortality between immediate and delayed angiography groups, with a hazard ratio of 1.28. Secondary outcomes, including myocardial infarction, severe neurologic deficit, and ICU length of stay, also showed no significant differences. The study highlights that immediate angiography does not confer a patient-oriented benefit and suggests that such patients should be admitted to the ICU for further evaluation rather than undergoing immediate angiography. The authors conclude that a strategy of immediate unselected coronary angiography offers no advantage over a delayed approach in this patient population.

## Case
A 70-year-old woman is found unresponsive and apneic at home by her partner. EMS arrives and finds the patient in monomorphic ventricular tachycardic (VT) cardiac arrest. She has a history of hypertension and non-insulin dependent diabetes mellitus. The paramedics achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after CPR, advanced cardiac life support (ALCS), and intubation. She arrives in the emergency department (ED) with decreased level of consciousness and shock. The EKG shows sinus tachycardia with nonspecific changes and no ST segment elevations, Q waves, or hyperacute T waves. Her point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) shows appropriate-appearing global ejection fraction and no marked wall motion abnormalities. Cardiology has been consulted and asks for a neurology consultation given her mental status.

## Background
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical condition with approximately 350,000 cases annually in the United States, half of which are witnessed. Ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia are common causes, with defibrillation being the primary treatment, though it often fails to achieve sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) accounts for 60% of OHCAs, and patients with ROSC and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) benefit from immediate angiography and possible angioplasty. The American Heart Association (AHA) recommends emergent coronary angiography for OHCA patients with suspected cardiac etiology and ST elevation (Class I, LOE B-NR), and considers it reasonable for comatose patients without ST elevation but with suspected cardiac origin (Class IIa, LOE B-NR). However, a multicenter RCT in the Netherlands (COACT trial) found no advantage in immediate angiography over delayed angiography for patients without ST elevation, highlighting the need for further research, especially since the trial only included patients with shockable rhythms, representing 60% of OHCA cases.

## Clinical Point
Does early coronary artery angiography following resuscitation of Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest benefit patient outcomes in patients without ST Segment elevation patterns on their post-ROSC EKG?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There appears to be no patient-oriented benefit to take OHCA patients with ROSC and no ST segment elevation on EKG immediately for angiography.

## Case Resolution
This 70-year-old patient was seen shortly after arrival by cardiology and after much discussion between both services, decided to have no indication to be immediately taken to the cath lab. She was admitted to the ICU and underwent angiography the next day where no culprit lesions were found. The patient unfortunately died a few days later in the ICU after never regaining consciousness.

## Clinical Application
In patients presenting via EMS for OHCA who are resuscitated, achieved ROSC, unconscious and without ST elevation on their EKG, they should not routinely be taken immediately for angiography but rather admitted to the ICU.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I would tell their partner that they have had a devastating cardiac arrest. Their heart is beating but they are unconscious. We are going to admit them to the ICU. She may be taken for angiography in the next day or two to check out her arteries. Immediate angiography has not been shown to help and may even hurt her. Overall, her prognosis is not very good.
