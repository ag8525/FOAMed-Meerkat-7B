## Summary
A five-year-old boy presents with symptoms consistent with an upper respiratory tract infection (URI), commonly known as a cold, characterized by rhinorrhea, congestion, and nocturnal cough, with no significant findings on chest examination. The document discusses the limited efficacy of over-the-counter (OTC) cough medications in children, highlighting a systematic review that found no strong evidence supporting their use, with many studies showing industry bias. The FDA and AAFP advise against using these medications in children under four due to potential harm. Honey is suggested as a potential remedy for cough in children over one year, with some evidence indicating it may be more effective than no treatment or diphenhydramine, though not superior to dextromethorphan. However, honey should not be given to children under one year due to the risk of botulism.

## Case Scenario
Five-year-old boy presents to the emergency department with a 2-day history of rhinorrhea and congestion. He has been coughing, and it is especially bad at night. Mild fever is reported at home. He is eating and drinking well. On examination, he looks well, is in no apparent distress, and vital signs are all normal. Chest exam reveals no focal crackles or wheeze. You diagnose him with an upper respiratory tract infection (URI) "cold".

## Background
A child presenting to the emergency department with a cough may have a differential diagnosis that includes both infectious and non-infectious causes. Infectious etiologies can be categorized into upper respiratory tract infections such as pharyngitis, otitis media, and croup, and lower respiratory tract infections like bronchiolitis or pneumonia. Non-infectious causes to consider include asthma, foreign body aspiration, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Accurate diagnosis is crucial for effective management and may involve clinical evaluation, imaging, and laboratory tests to differentiate between these conditions.

## BEEM Bottom Line
If you have a child with a cough older than 1 year of age, try a teaspoon of honey every 6 to 8 hours as needed.
