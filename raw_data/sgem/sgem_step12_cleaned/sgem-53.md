## Summary
The document discusses the management of anterior epistaxis, particularly comparing the effectiveness of topical tranexamic acid (TXA) versus traditional nasal packing. It highlights that anterior epistaxis is commonly due to bleeding from Kiesselbach’s Plexus and can be managed by visualizing the bleed and using direct therapies such as topical vasoconstrictors or packing. A randomized controlled trial indicated that TXA-soaked packing was superior to traditional packing with epinephrine and lidocaine, resulting in faster bleeding cessation, reduced emergency department length of stay, and higher patient satisfaction, although the study had limitations such as lack of blinding and imbalance in patient history of epistaxis. The document suggests considering TXA for anterior epistaxis to improve outcomes, while noting that the evidence is based on a single RCT and may not apply to posterior bleeds or patients with bleeding disorders.

## Case
A 72-year-old man presents with epistaxis. He has no history of coagulopathy but does have a history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, and osteoarthritis. His vital signs are BP 154/92, HR 70, and RR 14. He is taking an ACE-I, hydrochlorothiazide 25mg, and ASA 81mg.

## Point
Is topical tranexamic acid (TXA) better than nasal packing for an anterior epistaxis?

## Background
Aortic stenosis is a condition characterized by the narrowing of the aortic valve opening, which can lead to significant cardiovascular complications. It is clinically significant due to its prevalence, particularly in the elderly population, and its association with increased morbidity and mortality if left untreated. The condition can result in symptoms such as chest pain, syncope, and heart failure. Standard diagnostic approaches include echocardiography, which assesses the severity of valve narrowing and cardiac function. Treatment options vary based on the severity of the condition and patient factors, with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) being common interventions for severe cases.

## BEEM Bottom Line
For anterior epistaxis consider soaking the packing in TXA to stop the bleeding and get them home sooner.

## Case Resolution
The 72-year-old man is informed the traditional method involves packing his nose for three days with a follow-up to remove. The alternative is to try packing with another solution which has been shown in one study to stop the bleeding earlier, get you out of the emergency department faster, no difference in side effects, less re-bleeding, and has greater patient satisfaction. He chose wisely and went with the TXA packing, left after one hour, and did not bounce back to the emergency department within a week.
