## Summary
The document discusses the management of distal radius torus (buckle) fractures in children, highlighting findings from the FORCE trial, a randomized controlled equivalence study. The trial compared rigid immobilization with a soft bandage in children aged 4-15 years with confirmed torus fractures, finding no significant difference in pain or functional outcomes between the two groups over six weeks. The study supports the use of a soft bandage as an equivalent alternative to rigid immobilization, emphasizing the importance of shared decision-making and symptom management without the need for extensive follow-up or imaging. The trial underscores the potential for clinical inertia, where less intervention can be part of high-quality care, and suggests that these injuries can be managed similarly to wrist sprains, with simple analgesia and encouragement of normal use as tolerated.

## Case
Jack is nine years old, and he presents to the emergency department (ED) with an arm injury. Today he was running at school, and he fell over onto his outstretched arm. His right arm is neurovascularly intact, with no swelling or deformity. He has bony tenderness at the distal radius. The X-ray shows a buckle fracture of his right distal radius.

## Background
Buckle fractures, also known as torus fractures, are characterized by a compression of the bony cortex on one side while the opposite cortex remains intact, distinguishing them from greenstick fractures where the opposite cortex is not intact. These fractures are the most common type seen in children, particularly affecting the distal radius, and frequently present in emergency departments. Despite their prevalence, there is significant variability in their management, with some clinicians opting for casts, others for splints, and differing opinions on the necessity of orthopedic follow-up. A randomized controlled trial conducted 12 years ago found no significant difference in discomfort, function, or fracture displacement between treatment with a soft bandage for four weeks and a below-elbow back slab cast for one week followed by a circumferential cast for three weeks, although the soft bandage was associated with more pain in the first week. This ongoing debate highlights the challenges in translating clinical research into practice, as evidenced by the continued discussion on appropriate management strategies for buckle fractures.

## Clinical Point
What is the appropriate management of torus fractures in children?

## Case Resolution
Jack gets offered a soft bandage which he is happy to use. He is discharged home with simple analgesia (ibuprofen or paracetamol) and no scheduled follow-up is arranged.

## Clinical Application
It’s always great to have more evidence to back up what we are already clinically doing. These torus fractures in children heal well, don’t need rigid immobilization, and can be treated the same way we treat wrist sprains.

**What Do I Tell the Parent?**
Jack has a small bump in his bone that is essentially the same as a sprain, and we manage it in the same way. You can give some analgesia over the next few days if needed and encourage Jack to use his arm as he feels comfortable. It will improve over the next couple of weeks.
