## Summary
In this case study, a 65-year-old man with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) was managed pre-hospital with standard care, including aspirin, nitroglycerin, and oxygen, while being transported to a PCI center. The document discusses the potential use of remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) as an adjunctive treatment to reduce myocardial reperfusion injury, a concept supported by studies indicating improved cardiac outcomes. RIC involves inflating a blood pressure cuff on an extremity to induce brief ischemic episodes, potentially limiting infarct size. However, the study's limitations, such as lack of blinding, per-protocol analysis bias, and logistical challenges in emergency settings, suggest that while RIC shows promise, further large-scale trials are needed to confirm its efficacy and feasibility in pre-hospital STEMI management. The EMS team, after consulting with medical control, opted not to perform RIC, highlighting the need for more evidence before integrating it into standard protocols.

## Case
Your rural EMS unit is dispatched to a call for a 65-year-old man with chest pain. On scene, a 12-lead ECG is acquired in six minutes, revealing ST elevation in V3 through V6. The patient is given 324 mg of chewable aspirin, sublingual nitroglycerin which reduces his 9/10 chest pressure, oxygen by nasal cannula at 2 lpm to maintain his SaO2 above 94%. The patient is removed from the second floor of his home by stairchair, placed on the stretcher, loaded into the ambulance, and transport is initiated to the closest PCI center. The EMS provider calls the hospital with a STEMI alert with a 30-minute ETA. En route, the patient is monitored, a serial 12-lead is done, another nitro is given when chest discomfort returns, but otherwise, you settle in for the ride. You recall reading an article in a journal recently about remote ischemic conditioning, which is done by inflating a BP cuff on the patient’s arm to 200 mmHg for 5 minutes, then release the pressure for 5 minutes, and repeat three more times. The article said this technique reduces myocardial damage from reperfusion injury after the PCI. It’s not in your protocols so you call online medical control to discuss it.

## Background
Ischemic preconditioning is a technique initially studied in dogs to reduce myocardial infarct size by alternating periods of coronary artery ischemia and reperfusion, enhancing myocardial resistance to subsequent ischemic events. In humans, remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) is a related method involving the inflation of a blood pressure cuff on an extremity, typically the arm, to 200 mmHg for 5 minutes, repeated four times. This approach is believed to involve neural and humoral interactions, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear. RIC has been evaluated in large trials involving cardiac surgery and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), demonstrating benefits in improving cardiac markers and reducing infarct size, thus highlighting its clinical significance in managing myocardial ischemic injury.

## Clinical Point
Does pre-hospital remote ischemic conditioning performed on STEMI patients decrease myocardial reperfusion injury and improve their long-term outcome?

## SGEM Bottom Line
The technique has promise as it is cheap and can be done by all levels of EMS provider. However, there needs to be a large multicentered trial using RIC showing improved patient-oriented outcomes before this becomes a routine treatment in the pre-hospital setting for STEMI patients.

## Case Resolution
You discuss the recommendation with your EMS medical director via cell phone. You and the doctor decide not to perform the procedure, but agree to meet and review the literature to see if the procedure should be considered for inclusion in statewide protocols.
