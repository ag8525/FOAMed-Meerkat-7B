## Summary
A 14-month-old boy with respiratory distress and hypoxemia was evaluated in the ED, presenting with symptoms consistent with bronchiolitis. Despite initial interventions, including beta agonists, his oxygen saturation remained low, prompting consideration of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy. The PARIS-2 trial, a multicenter randomized clinical study, assessed the efficacy of HFNC versus standard oxygen therapy in children with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. The trial concluded that HFNC did not significantly reduce hospital stay length compared to standard oxygen therapy and was associated with a longer duration of oxygen therapy and higher ICU escalation rates. The study highlighted the importance of judicious use of HFNC, suggesting standard oxygen therapy as a preferable initial treatment for mild to moderate cases to avoid unnecessary prolongation of hospital stay.

## Case
A 14-month-old boy presents to the emergency department (ED) with two days of upper respiratory symptoms and respiratory distress. He has a cough, fever of 38.5°C, runny nose, and increased work of breathing that started today. He is breathing at a rate of 48 breaths per minute with intercostal retractions and oxygen saturation of 88%. His lung exam reveals bilateral wheezing, rhonchi but no focal findings. He is drinking well, and parents report normal urine output. He is suctioned and given a trial of Beta agonist because he has a history of eczema and a sibling with asthma with no change. Despite your interventions, he continues to breathe rapidly with an oxygen saturation of 89% on room air. You are working with an eager medical student, and she asks, "Should we start high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) at 2L/kg/min and admit the patient?"

## Background
Respiratory illnesses are a leading cause of hospital admissions in children under five, with some cases progressing to acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. Traditionally, treatment options were limited to observation or mechanical ventilation via intubation. However, since the early 2000s, high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) has emerged as a noninvasive ventilation alternative. Research indicates that HFNC can reduce the need for escalation of care, although it does not significantly affect ICU admission rates or the length of hospital stay.

## Clinical Point
Does the early use of HFNC reduce the length of hospital stay in pediatric patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure compared with standard oxygen therapy?

## Case Resolution
You tell the medical student that you agree with her conclusion that the child requires admission. However, given the results of the recent PARIS-2 trial you would not start high-flow oxygen therapy at this time. The patient is admitted to the general pediatric floor with oxygen via face mask.

## Clinical Application
High-flow oxygen therapy is very popular, but this isn’t the first time that medicine has embraced an intervention with limited evidence. It does not seem to decrease length of stay or escalation to the ICU or decrease risk of requiring non-invasive/invasive ventilation. 
We are overusing HFNC and really need to be thoughtful about what we are treating. Is it severe respiratory distress or our own discomfort? The indiscriminate use of HFNC can be worse for patients as it extends their length of stay. For those practicing in the emergency department, we are often the ones making that initial decision. So, for pediatric patients with mild to moderate hypoxemic respiratory failure, start them on standard oxygen therapy rather than HFNC.

**What Do I Tell the Patient/Family?**
Your child has something called bronchiolitis and is breathing very fast. His oxygen level on the monitor is also a bit low which can happen in this disease. Many of these children improve on their own with time, but I believe he needs some oxygen to help him out right now. We can start with oxygen delivered by face mask and have you stay in the hospital for close monitoring.
