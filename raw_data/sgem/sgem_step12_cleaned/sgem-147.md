## Summary
In the management of stable supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), a modified Valsalva manoeuvre has been shown to be more effective than the standard technique in converting patients to sinus rhythm. This method involves a strain followed by supine positioning with leg elevation, significantly increasing conversion rates (43% vs. 17%) with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 4. The study by Appelboam et al. demonstrated that this approach reduces the need for adenosine and other anti-arrhythmic treatments without increasing adverse events. Given its simplicity and efficacy, the modified Valsalva manoeuvre is recommended as a first-line treatment for stable SVT in the emergency department, offering a non-pharmacologic and non-electrical alternative that can be easily taught to patients.

## Case
A 24-year-old female presents to the emergency department with palpitations. She feels anxious but is hemodynamically stable and her ECG demonstrates supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). This condition has happened several times before and she hates the medication she is usually given in the emergency department that makes her feel like she is dying.

## Background
Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is a condition where patients experience an abnormally fast heart rhythm originating above the heart's ventricles, often leading them to seek emergency care. Clinically significant due to its potential to cause hemodynamic instability, SVT can be treated through electrical, pharmacologic, and non-pharmacologic methods. Synchronized cardioversion is the preferred treatment for hemodynamically unstable patients. For stable patients, pharmacologic interventions include adenosine, calcium channel blockers, and beta blockers, with adenosine often causing patient anxiety due to its transient effects on heart rhythm. Non-pharmacologic methods include the mammalian dive reflex, more commonly used in children, where the patient immerses their face in ice-cold water, and the Valsalva maneuver, which increases vagal tone by raising intrathoracic pressure. Carotid massage is another option but carries risks, particularly in elderly patients.
