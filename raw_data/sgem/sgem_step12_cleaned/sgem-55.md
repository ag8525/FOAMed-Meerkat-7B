## Summary
The document discusses the issue of opioid prescribing in emergency departments (EDs), particularly in the context of dental pain, and evaluates whether departmental guidelines can reduce opioid abuse. It highlights the historical context of pain management in the ED, where opioids were liberally prescribed due to patient satisfaction metrics, leading to a public health crisis of opioid addiction and mortality. A study is referenced, showing that implementing a controlled substance prescribing guideline in two rural EDs resulted in a significant reduction in opioid prescriptions for dental pain and a decrease in related ED visits. However, the study's limitations, such as potential biases and its applicability to larger urban settings, are noted. The document concludes that while the guideline showed promise, it lacks sufficient evidence to be broadly implemented, emphasizing the need for careful opioid prescribing and patient education on pain management. The case resolution for a patient with dental pain involved non-opioid management and referral to a dentist, reflecting a multimodal approach to pain management.

## Case Scenario
33-year-old man presents to the emergency department at 2AM complaining of an infected tooth. At triage, he requests that “oxy” stuff that worked really well last time.

## Point
Can a departmental guideline on controlled substance prescriptions reduce opioid abuse?

## Background
The designation of pain as the "fifth vital sign" by the Joint Commission in 2001 heightened awareness of oligoanalgesia in emergency departments (EDs) and inadvertently incentivized the liberal prescription of opioids, contributing to a public health crisis. ED physicians, among the most frequent prescribers, often aim to eliminate pain entirely, despite the risks of addiction and respiratory depression associated with opioids. Educated patients tend to prefer managing some pain over the risks of opioid use, highlighting a gap in patient education regarding pain causes and opioid risks. The opioid crisis, exacerbated by the introduction of long-acting oxycodone, led to a significant increase in opioid-related mortality, with many deaths linked to recent prescriptions. In response, the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) issued guidelines in 2012 advocating for individualized, time-limited opioid use, reserving them for severe or refractory acute pain, and advising against their use for chronic pain exacerbations.

## Bottom Line
Opioid prescribing in the ED will continue to be a problem and this study does not provide enough high-quality information to implement this guideline at my hospital.

## Case Resolution
The 33-year-old man with dental pain is given a dental block, 600mg ibuprofen, a prescription of amoxicillin 500mg TID, and information on accessing a dentist the next morning.
