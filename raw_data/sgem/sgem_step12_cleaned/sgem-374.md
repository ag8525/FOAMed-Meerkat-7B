## Summary
In the context of rising opioid overdose deaths, this document discusses the initiation of medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) in the emergency department (ED) as a critical intervention point. It highlights the low uptake of buprenorphine post-ED discharge and explores patient perspectives on MOUD initiation, emphasizing the importance of shared decision-making (SDM) to enhance patient-provider interactions. The study reveals that while patients support offering buprenorphine in the ED, they also advocate for methadone options, stressing individualized treatment plans. Key factors influencing decision-making include logistical ease, withdrawal management, and the need for non-judgmental clinician interactions. The document underscores the necessity of integrating psychiatric care and peer support in OUD management, recognizing the varied definitions of recovery and the role of relapse in treatment journeys. The clinical application involves offering buprenorphine with clear instructions and coordinating follow-up care, aiming to tailor interventions to patient readiness and needs.

## Case
A 24-year-old male presents to the emergency department (ED) after a fentanyl overdose. He is successfully resuscitated using naloxone and is stable after an observation period. You are interested in seizing this opportunity to offer some type of help to this patient to prevent another opioid overdose in his future.

## Background
Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) is a significant public health issue in the United States, with opioids being the leading cause of drug overdose deaths. Methadone and buprenorphine are the most effective treatments for reducing illicit opioid use and mortality associated with OUD. The emergency department (ED) is recognized as a critical setting for initiating medications for OUD (MOUD), even in resource-limited environments. Despite the availability of buprenorphine, its uptake post-ED discharge is low, with less than 5% of patients filling a prescription within 90 days after a non-fatal opioid overdose. While clinician-reported barriers to prescribing buprenorphine in the ED have been studied, patient perspectives remain underexplored. Implementing a shared decision-making (SDM) framework could enhance patient-provider interactions by addressing misconceptions and fostering informed discussions, potentially increasing the administration of MOUD in the ED.

## Clinical Point
What are patient’s perspectives regarding the initiation of medications for opioid use disorder in the ED?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Consider offering MOUD to patients in the ED and tailor treatment to the individual needs and circumstances of each patient.

## Case Resolution
You discuss the availability of buprenorphine which can be prescribed from the ED and methadone from clinics within your city. You discuss the pros and cons of each treatment as best you understand them, and he is interested in trying buprenorphine at home. You also provide him with a list of outpatient clinics that can help with the multifactorial interventions needed to address his OUD.

## Clinical Application
The patient agrees to take home four doses of buprenorphine-naloxone as well as instructions on when to take the first dose with respect to the development of significant withdrawal symptoms. He will try to follow up at a local clinic tomorrow.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
I (Elizabeth) tell them they have to wait as long as they can – the worse they feel, the more it will help. They can take acetaminophen, clonidine, etc., to get them as far as they possibly can past their first use before they take it. We also give them instructions to let them escalate their dose – don’t be stingy, 4mg is probably not enough – start with 8mg and let them go to 16mg or 32mg on the first day if needed.
