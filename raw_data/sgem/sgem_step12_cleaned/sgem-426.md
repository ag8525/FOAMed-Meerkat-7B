## Summary
In the context of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), a retrospective observational study evaluated the impact of using small-volume bag-valve masks (BVMs) versus standard large BVMs on the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The study, conducted on adult patients with advanced airway management in a single urban EMS system, found that the use of small BVMs was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving ROSC compared to large BVMs, with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.74. Despite similar ventilation rates between groups, the small BVM cohort had higher end-tidal CO2 levels. The study highlights the importance of high-quality CPR and suggests that standard large BVMs remain the best practice for OHCA resuscitation, cautioning against changes based solely on theoretical benefits without robust evidence. The findings underscore the need for a randomized controlled trial to further investigate the optimal BVM size for OHCA management.

## Case
You are dispatched to an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). The patient is a 54-year-old man who collapsed in front of his family after complaining of chest pain for several hours. On your arrival, first responders from the fire department are performing high-quality basic cardiac life support. You continue with compressions and defibrillations and your partner places an advanced airway. Your EMS agency has equipped you with small-volume adult bag-valve masks (BVMs), the first responders have been utilizing a standard adult BVM thus far during the resuscitation. Your partner asks you which of the two BVMs you should use to continue the resuscitation.

## Background
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) presents a critical situation where appropriate ventilation strategy is debated, particularly concerning the use of bag-valve masks (BVMs). Standard BVMs can deliver volumes exceeding normal tidal volumes, raising concerns about potential hyperventilation. To mitigate this risk, some experts advocate for the use of smaller BVMs to ensure more controlled ventilation.

## Clinical Point
Is using a small adult BVM during resuscitation of out of hospital cardiac arrest associated with return of spontaneous circulation?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There is a lack of high-quality evidence to support the use of small-volume BVMs. This retrospective study showed that small bags were associated with worse outcomes than the standard large bags. There is a need for a high-quality RCT to determine if one size fits all or if there is a superiority to small bags.

## Case Resolution
You continue to use the adult BVM that is already in use. You focus your resuscitative efforts on proven interventions, high-quality CPR, minimizing interruptions in chest compressions and avoiding overventilation. The patient achieves ROSC, and the post-ROSC EKG demonstrates a ST elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI). You arrange to rendezvous with an air ambulance to transport the patient to the nearest STEMI center which is two hours away by ground. The patient undergoes successful cardiac catheterization and stent placement. He is discharged home neurologically intact on hospital day five.

## Clinical Application
Small volume BVMs appear to decrease the likelihood of ROSC during OHCA when compared with standard adult BVMs. Performing high-quality CPR utilizing a standard adult BVM remains the best practice for treating patients in out of hospital cardiac arrest. This is also another cautionary tale to not implement sweeping changes (large bag to small bag) based on pathophysiology and other low-quality evidence. The time to change practice is when there is sufficient evidence. Sufficient evidence did not exist to make this change in 2017.

**What Do I Tell the Patient’s Family?**
We’re going to do everything we can to restart your loved one’s heart. We’re going to use some special equipment to breathe for him while we continue CPR. If we’re able to get his heart restarted, we’ll transfer him to the closest appropriate hospital as quickly and safely as we can.
