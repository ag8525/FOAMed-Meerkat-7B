## Summary
The document discusses the REACT-2 trial, a randomized controlled study comparing immediate total-body CT scanning to conventional imaging with selective CT in severe trauma patients. The study found no significant difference in in-hospital mortality between the two approaches, although immediate total-body CT resulted in higher radiation exposure. Secondary outcomes such as time to diagnosis and length of stay showed minor statistical differences but were not clinically significant. The study highlights the lack of a validated clinical decision tool for major trauma involving chest, abdominal, and pelvic injuries, and suggests a selective imaging strategy may be preferable to reduce unnecessary radiation. The authors conclude that future research should focus on identifying patients who would benefit most from immediate total-body CT.

## Case
A 53-year-old woman in good health is brought in by ambulance after a motor vehicle collision. She did not lose consciousness but did bump her head. Her main complaint is chest pain with difficulty breathing. She is tachycardic, tachypnic, and has some mild abdominal pain. You suspect a flail chest on your clinical examination. A FAST examination is performed and is negative.

## Point
Should trauma patients get an immediate total-body (pan scan) CT or should you do usual X-rays followed by selective CT scanning?

## Background
Major trauma involving the chest, abdomen, and pelvis lacks a validated clinical decision tool for initial radiologic evaluation, unlike head or cervical spine injuries which utilize the Canadian CT Head Rules and Canadian C-Spine Rules. The use of pan scanning, or immediate total-body CT scans, has been studied, with retrospective data suggesting a reduction in mortality, as demonstrated by Huber-Wagner et al. in 2009, where the number needed to treat (NNT) varied from 17 to 32 based on injury severity. A systematic review by Sierink et al. in 2012 indicated potential benefits of pan scanning but highlighted the necessity for a large, prospective randomized clinical trial with patient-oriented outcomes. The population of interest includes adults with severe injuries or compromised vital signs, excluding pregnant individuals, those referred from other hospitals, low-energy trauma cases, and patients too unstable for CT scans. The intervention involves an immediate total-body CT scan, compared to standard ATLS-guided workup, with primary outcomes focusing on in-hospital mortality and secondary outcomes including 24-hour and 30-day mortality, imaging time, diagnosis time, length of stay, mechanical ventilation duration, radiation exposure, adverse events, transfusion needs, missed injuries, and hospital costs. Sub-group analysis targets mortality in patients with an Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 16 or higher and those with traumatic brain injury (TBI).

## SGEM Bottom Line
There is no clear evidence that immediate total body scanning of trauma patients provides better clinically important patient-oriented outcomes but does result in more radiation exposure.

## Case Resolution
Your patient has a selective strategy getting a thoracic CT with plain X-rays of the spine and pelvis. A flail chest was identified along with a pneumothorax, which we drained. The patient was then admitted to the trauma service.

## Clinical Application
Although this study has some limitations, this paper supports a selective strategy rather than an immediate total body scan in the evaluation of trauma patients. Radiation exposure should be considered before "routinely" using a total-body CT protocol.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have been in a car accident. We need to get some x-rays and a CT scan. This will show us what you have injured and help us give you the best care possible.
