## Summary
Intranasal fentanyl has been shown to be an effective and timely analgesic option for children with sickle cell disease (SCD) experiencing vaso-occlusive pain episodes (VOE) in the emergency department (ED), potentially increasing the likelihood of discharge. This secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study across 20 pediatric EDs found that children receiving intranasal fentanyl had greater odds of being discharged compared to those who did not, despite the variability in admission rates and potential confounders such as pain severity and opioid administration timing. The study highlights the importance of rapid pain management, aligning with NHLBI guidelines, and suggests that intranasal fentanyl can serve as a valuable alternative when IV access is challenging. However, the study's non-randomized design and selection biases necessitate further prospective, randomized controlled trials to confirm these findings and optimize pain management strategies for SCD patients in diverse clinical settings.

## Case
A 14-year-old male with sickle cell disease (SCD) presents to your emergency department (ED) with a vaso-occlusive pain episode (VOE) of his bilateral legs and back. He tells you that he has had similar pain from previous VOEs, but his sister is graduating from high school tomorrow, and he really hopes he can go home so he can attend her graduation. He denies any fever or difficulty breathing. On exam, he appears uncomfortable with tenderness to palpation of the bilateral shins and lower back. The rest of his exam is unremarkable. As you begin ordering bloodwork and a dose of intravenous (IV) morphine, his nurse says to you, “He looks very uncomfortable. Do you want to give him a dose of intranasal (IN) fentanyl while we’re waiting for an IV?”

## Background
Pediatric pain is often under-treated despite the availability of various pharmacological options, such as analgesics, NSAIDs, nerve blocks, sub-dissociative dose ketamine, and opioids, as well as non-pharmacological methods like distraction, music, and splinting. In children with sickle cell disease (SCD) experiencing vaso-occlusive episodes (VOE) in the emergency department (ED), effective and timely pain management is crucial, with opioids being the primary treatment. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) 2014 guidelines recommend prompt parenteral opioid administration for VOE. However, factors such as ED crowding, boarding, and staffing shortages often delay care. Intranasal fentanyl is a safe alternative for delivering analgesia in pediatric patients without requiring intravenous access.

## Clinical Point
How does intranasal fentanyl for the treatment of vaso-occlusive pain episodes in children with sickle cell disease impact disposition?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Intranasal fentanyl *may* be helpful for children with SCD presenting with VOE but prioritize the timely and adequate administration of parenteral opioids.

## Case Resolution
You decide to give the patient a dose of IN fentanyl while he is awaiting IV access. After IV access is obtained, he receives a few doses of parenteral morphine and tells you that he feels like his pain is much better controlled. He is discharged from the ED.

## Clinical Application
Intranasal fentanyl offers a quick and effective method of providing pain control for children with SCD presenting with VOE. This is important to keep in mind especially in situations where IV access is difficult and delayed. If the nurses are struggling to get that IV access, after two tries sometimes the kids will change their mind about using IN fentanyl. It was also noted in the study that some kids received only IN fentanyl and did not require an IV.
While this study suggests that administration of IN fentanyl is associated with increased odds of discharge from the ED, there is a wide confidence interval and multiple confounders. Given the boarding crises we are facing it is not unreasonable to try IN fentanyl in hopes of being able to discharge a patient home from the ED, but multicenter, prospective randomized, controlled trials are needed.
Interestingly, the authors mention in their discussion that there was a study protocol published in 2012 for a randomized, controlled trial comparing IN fentanyl to IV morphine for severe SCD-associated pain but it has not yet been published. We reached out to the investigators to see whether they would be willing to share the results but have not heard from them yet.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I am sorry you are so uncomfortable. I want to get an IV and give you some IV pain medications. There is some research to suggest that a dose of fentanyl through your nose will help with pain but also may increase the chances of you going home. Let’s give it a try as we are waiting for your IV to be placed. Does that sound okay?
