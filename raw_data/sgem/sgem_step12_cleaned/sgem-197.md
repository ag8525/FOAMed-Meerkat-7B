## Summary
The document discusses the impact of tracheal intubation during adult in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) on survival outcomes, referencing a study by Andersen et al. published in JAMA 2017. The study, which included 108,079 adult patients, found that early tracheal intubation within the first 15 minutes of resuscitation was associated with decreased survival to hospital discharge compared to those not intubated during that time. The primary outcome showed lower survival rates for intubated patients (16.3%) versus non-intubated patients (19.4%), with secondary outcomes indicating less return of spontaneous circulation and favorable neurological outcomes in the intubated group. The study's observational design and propensity-matching technique highlight potential confounding factors, suggesting that clinical judgment should guide intubation decisions during cardiac arrest. The findings do not support routine early intubation, and further randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify the role of intubation in IHCA management.

## Case
You are working a regular shift in the emergency department when you hear a code blue called. You are the first physician to respond and you begin to resuscitate the patient. Your respiratory therapist is adequately ventilating the patient with a bag valve mask, and they ask you if they should prepare to intubate at the pulse and rhythm check.

## Background
In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) refers to cardiac arrests occurring within a hospital setting, with the American Heart Association reporting 209,000 cases in 2016. The survival rate for adults to hospital discharge is 24.8%, a significant improvement from 13.7% in 2000. Notably, over 80% of adult survivors achieve a favorable neurologic outcome, defined by a Cerebral Performance Category score of 1 or 2. IHCA is a critical emergency with high mortality, and the decision to intubate during such events is complex and varies among clinicians. The intubation process in cardiac arrest is challenging to study, with existing research primarily observational, and prior to recent studies, there was a lack of specific research on IHCA intubation and survival outcomes.

## Clinical Point
Does tracheal intubation during adult in-hospital cardiac arrest affect survival?

## SGEM Bottom Line
We should continue to use our clinical judgment and be selective with who we intubate during a cardiac arrest.

## Case Resolution
You tell your respiratory therapist not to intubate and to continue to use the bag valve mask. At your pulse check, the patient is in ventricular fibrillation, you defibrillate them with 200J, and resume CPR. At the next pulse check the patient has return of spontaneous circulation and is transferred to the intensive care unit.

## Clinical Application
Given the data, it appears reasonable to continue to be selective with intubation in sudden cardiac arrest. This data is not strong enough to have an impact on my care. There may be some patients, especially those with respiratory insufficiency, that would derive benefit from intubation during their arrest. A randomized control trial is needed to Point the Point "to intubate or not to intubate in the adult IHCA patient." This observational cohort study suggests equipoise and could be used to request ethics approval for a randomized control trial.

**What do I tell my patient?**
My patient is currently, actively dying so I don’t tell them anything. If my staff asks while coding the patient, I will tell them that unless the patient becomes difficult to ventilate with the bag valve mask, we will hold off on intubation for now.
