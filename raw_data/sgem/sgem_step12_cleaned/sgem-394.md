## Summary
The document discusses the use of sodium bicarbonate during pediatric cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and its association with outcomes in in-hospital cardiac arrest. The study analyzed data from pediatric patients who received CPR across multiple intensive care units, excluding those with terminal conditions or on ECMO. The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge, with secondary outcomes including return of spontaneous circulation and neurologic status. Results indicated that sodium bicarbonate use was common but associated with lower survival rates and no improvement in return of spontaneous circulation. The study highlights the lack of evidence supporting routine sodium bicarbonate use in pediatric cardiac arrest, suggesting it may be detrimental. Clinicians are advised to focus on high-quality CPR and targeted interventions rather than routine sodium bicarbonate administration.

## Case
A 6-month-old boy presents to the emergency department (ED) with three days of worsening cough, cold symptoms, and fever. Parents note that he has been progressively more tired and difficult to arouse. He is found to be in hypoxic respiratory failure and septic shock. Intravenous (IV) access is obtained. He is quickly intubated. Despite multiple fluid boluses, he remains hypotensive and is started on vasoactive support. His blood gas reveals a mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis with a lactate of 5.0. Despite your best efforts, he has an episode of agitation leading to hypoxia and subsequent cardiac arrest. Your team begins high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). An arterial blood gas is obtained demonstrates a pH of 7.0, PaCO2 of 70, PaO2 of 28, HCO3– of 7, Base Deficit of -10, and Lactate 10.0.

A team member asks if you want to administer some sodium bicarbonate (1mEq/kg).

## Background
Cardiac arrest management in the emergency department or ICU often involves interventions like high-quality CPR and early defibrillation, but the use of sodium bicarbonate during CPR lacks strong evidence. Historically, sodium bicarbonate was used to alkalize blood pH and treat metabolic acidosis, based on assumptions that low pH reduces cardiac function and catecholamine responsiveness, and that increasing pH would improve these. However, many supporting studies were conducted on animal models or in vitro, and rapid bicarbonate infusion can cause intracellular acidosis due to CO2 imbalance. Due to insufficient evidence of benefit and potential harm, sodium bicarbonate was removed from the American Heart Association's guidelines. The 2020 AHA guidelines state that no new evidence supports routine sodium bicarbonate use in cardiac arrest, and it may worsen survival and neurological outcomes, a finding consistent in pediatric literature.

## Clinical Point
What is the association between sodium bicarbonate use and pediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest mortality and morbidity?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Sodium bicarbonate should not be routinely used in pediatric cardiac arrest. Clinicians should be deliberate and targeted with the use of sodium bicarbonate to address specific pathophysiologic states.

## Case Resolution
You refrain from giving sodium bicarbonate at this time and have the team focus on performing high quality CPR and identifying any shockable rhythms. You give him empiric antibiotics to treat sepsis and increase the respiratory rate on the ventilator to help him blow off some carbon dioxide. He regains pulses and is transferred to the PICU for further care.

## Clinical Application
There is limited evidence regarding the use of sodium bicarbonate in pediatric cardiac arrests to improve acidosis and hemodynamics. Additionally, it does not seem to improve survival and may have potentially detrimental effects.

**What Do I Tell My Patient/Team Members?**
After the patient the stabilized and brought to the PICU, you debrief with your team. When asked about the decision to use or withhold sodium bicarbonate, I would tell them that there is limited data that sodium bicarbonate use has significant impact on addressing acidosis and improving hemodynamics. More importantly, its use is not associated with improved patient outcomes and could potentially be detrimental.
