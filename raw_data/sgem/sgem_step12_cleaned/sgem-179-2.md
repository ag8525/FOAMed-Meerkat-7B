## Summary
In this clinical case, a 45-year-old male with a suspected heroin overdose was successfully revived with naloxone, an opioid antagonist, administered by EMS. The document discusses the increasing prevalence of heroin use and the critical role of naloxone in reversing opioid toxicity, highlighting its administration by first responders and laypeople. The literature review suggests that complications from opioid rebound toxicity are rare, and patients who appear stable post-naloxone may be safely discharged after careful assessment, including observation of respiratory patterns and mental status without stimulation. However, the review notes limitations in the evidence due to methodological quality and the evolving nature of heroin adulteration with substances like fentanyl, which may require higher naloxone doses. The case resolution emphasizes the importance of thorough clinical evaluation before discharge and the provision of addiction counseling referrals.

## Case
A 45-year-old male arrives via emergency medical services (EMS) complaining that he wants to be discharged. EMS states they found him unresponsive and with paraphernalia consistent with intravenous heroin use. His prehospital vital signs were oxygen saturation of 89% and respiratory rate of six breaths per minute prior to administration of oxygen and 1 mg of naloxone. After naloxone administration he is alert and oriented times three with a normal pulse oximetry and clear lung fields. It is 20 minutes after he received naloxone and he is asking to be discharged.

## Background
Heroin use in the USA has been rising since 2007, leading to an increase in heroin-related deaths due to the opioid's depressive effects on heart rate and respiration, which can result in fatal overdoses. Naloxone, an opioid antagonist, is the standard treatment for heroin overdoses, effectively reversing the effects by competitively binding to opioid receptors in the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract. It is available in various forms, including intranasal, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intravenous, nebulization, or endotracheal administration, and is increasingly accessible to first responders and laypeople. The American Heart Association's 2015 Guidelines introduced recommendations for naloxone education, training, and distribution, as well as opioid overdose treatment protocols. Despite naloxone's effectiveness, some individuals may refuse further medical treatment or transport to emergency departments after revival.

## Point
How effective is naloxone administration in heroin users by first responders and bystanders and what are the risks associated with naloxone distribution programs?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Delayed opioid toxicity is more likely overlooked opioid toxicity rather than rebound toxicity. There are patients that are safe to discharge, but perform a careful clinical exam and be certain to observe the patient’s respiratory pattern and mental status in a non-stimulated state. Of course, exercise caution in the ever changing world of heroin abuse and all its adulterants.

## Case Resolution
When observed from the entrance to the room without stimulation, it was obvious that when not stimulated the patient’s respiratory rate would decrease and their oxygen saturation would dip to 90%. Rather than administering additional naloxone, you apply supplemental oxygen and allow the patient to sleep for another hour. At that time, you remove the oxygen and recheck the patient without overly stimulating him. He appears to be alert and oriented times three with clear lung fields and had no signs of respiratory depression; he can walk to the bathroom on his own and back without assistance. The patient is safe for discharge and receives a referral to addiction counselling and treatment.

## Clinical Application
The results of this study provide weak evidence applicable to EMS determining refusal of transport decisions after heroin overdoses, emergency department physicians assessing the same patients once brought into the emergency department, and naloxone distribution programs using bystanders or lay people

**What do I tell my patient?**
You have just had a heroin overdose and you feel better because we gave you a drug that blocks the effects (naloxone). If you feel like you are getting worse you should return immediately. Preferably go home or be with someone that can observe you for a period of time. If you would like to be referred to a detox program or perhaps even get a prescription for naloxone to take home we can start talking about that as well.
