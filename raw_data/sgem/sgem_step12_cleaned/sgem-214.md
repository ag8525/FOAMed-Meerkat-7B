## Summary
The document discusses the management of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), focusing on the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) as a treatment option. PPH is a significant cause of maternal mortality, defined by the WHO as blood loss of ≥1,000 mL or accompanied by hypovolemia within 24 hours post-birth. TXA, an antifibrinolytic agent, has been recommended by ACOG for use when initial medical therapy fails, with a Level B recommendation. The WOMAN trial, a large international study, evaluated TXA's efficacy in reducing mortality and hysterectomy rates in PPH. While TXA significantly reduced mortality due to bleeding, it did not show a statistical difference in the primary composite outcome of all-cause mortality or hysterectomy. The study's results are debated due to issues like subjective inclusion criteria, potential selection bias, and the influence of pharmaceutical funding. The SGEM concludes that TXA may or may not improve survival in PPH, suggesting its use should be considered on a case-by-case basis rather than routinely incorporated into treatment protocols.

## Case
37-year-old primiparous woman has a spontaneous vaginal delivery following an induction of labour at 39 weeks for gestational diabetes for which she is treated with insulin. She delivered a vigorous 3800g boy following a brief episode of shoulder dystocia. She is otherwise healthy but does have a remote history of asthma. Aside from insulin and prenatal vitamins, she takes no medications.
Approximately five minutes after delivery of the placenta, as you are evaluating her perineum, she begins to have a moderate amount of vaginal bleeding. Her nurse performs fundal massage which temporarily improves her flow.

## Background
Tranexamic acid (TXA), a synthetic analog of lysine, was discovered in the 1950s by Utako and Shosuke Okamoto as a potential treatment for postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), a condition defined by the World Health Organization as a blood loss of 1,000 mL or more, or blood loss with hypovolemia symptoms within 24 hours post-birth, and a leading cause of maternal mortality globally. TXA functions as an antifibrinolytic agent by binding to lysine receptor sites on plasminogen, thereby inhibiting its action on fibrin and reducing bleeding. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends TXA with a Level B recommendation for obstetric hemorrhage when initial medical therapy is ineffective, citing mortality reduction benefits. Pacheco et al. suggest administering 1 g of TXA intravenously within 3 hours of birth, with a possible second dose within 24 hours if needed, noting its 2-hour half-life and 7–8 hour antifibrinolytic effect duration, with a single dose typically being sufficient.

## Clinical Point
Does TXA improve survival in women with post-partum hemorrhage?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Tranexamic acid MAY or MAY NOT improve survival in women with post-partum hemorrhage.

## Case Resolution
You determine that your patient’s bleeding to be secondary to atony, the number one cause of post-partum hemorrhage. You perform a bimanual exam to evacuate any remaining tissue and clots while asking the bedside nurse to initiate an oxytocin infusion. You also request for the post-partum hemorrhage kit in the room which has essential medications. Her bleeding has improved and she’s no longer passing clots. You decide she needs a uterotonic beyond oxytocin but given her history of asthma, you elect for ergotomine which the nurse administers intra-muscularly.
Over the next 15 minutes, you repair her second-degree laceration which has mostly been hemostatic. You assess her total estimated blood loss to be 1000mL (which is probably inaccurate) so you ask the nurse to administer 1g TXA by IV.
She has no further bleeding and is transferred to post-partum. On post-partum day one her hemoglobin is 83g/L (8.3g/dl) and she is discharged with a prescription for iron polysaccharide.

## Clinical Application
We do not have good evidence that TXA provides a mortality benefit in post-partum hemorrhage. Until there is better evidence of efficacy, it should not be incorporated into protocols for routine use but rather may be considered as suggested by the ACOG level B recommendation.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
When you are having heavier bleeding after your vaginal delivery. We have strong evidence for some medicines that can improve bleeding. There is a newly purposed medicine called TXA that may or may not have a benefit. There is not enough evidence to suggest we should use TXA routinely in favor of the other medications that we know are effective.
