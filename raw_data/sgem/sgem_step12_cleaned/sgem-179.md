## Summary
A 45-year-old male with a history of intravenous heroin use was found unresponsive and treated with naloxone, resulting in restored alertness and normal vital signs. Naloxone, an opioid antagonist, is effective in reversing opioid overdoses and is widely used by first responders. The document discusses the safety of discharging patients post-naloxone if they exhibit normal mentation and vital signs, with a low risk of rebound opioid toxicity in the absence of co-intoxicants. However, careful clinical assessment is crucial, particularly in the context of potential fentanyl adulteration in heroin, which may require higher naloxone doses. The case resolution emphasizes the importance of observing the patient in a non-stimulated state to ensure stability before discharge, alongside providing addiction counseling referrals.

## Case
A 45-year-old male arrives via emergency medical services (EMS) complaining that he wants to be discharged. EMS states they found him unresponsive and with paraphernalia consistent with intravenous heroin use. His prehospital vital signs were oxygen saturation of 89% and respiratory rate of six breaths per minute prior to administration of oxygen and 1 mg of naloxone. After naloxone administration he is alert and oriented times three with a normal pulse oximetry and clear lung fields. It is 20 minutes after he received naloxone and he is asking to be discharged.

## Background
Heroin use in the USA has been on the rise since 2007, leading to an increase in heroin-related deaths due to its depressive effects on heart rate and respiration, which can result in fatal overdoses. Naloxone, an opioid antagonist, is the standard treatment for heroin overdoses, effectively reversing the effects by competitively binding to opioid receptors in the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract. It is available in various forms, including intranasal, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intravenous, nebulization, or endotracheal administration, and is increasingly accessible to first responders and laypeople. The American Heart Association's 2015 Guidelines introduced new recommendations emphasizing naloxone education, training, and distribution, as well as its administration by trained providers to patients in respiratory arrest due to suspected opioid overdose. Despite the availability of naloxone, some individuals may refuse further medical treatment or transport to emergency facilities after initial revival.

## Point
What are the medical risks to a heroin user treated with naloxone who refuses transport to the emergency department?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Delayed opioid toxicity is more likely overlooked opioid toxicity rather than rebound toxicity. There are patients that are safe to discharge, but perform a careful clinical exam and be certain to observe the patient’s respiratory pattern and mental status in a non-stimulated state. Of course, exercise caution in the ever-changing world of heroin abuse and all its adulterants.

## Case Resolution
When observed from the entrance to the room without stimulation, it was obvious that when not stimulated the patient’s respiratory rate would decrease and their oxygen saturation would dip to 90%. Rather than administering additional naloxone, you apply supplemental oxygen and allow the patient to sleep for another hour. At that time, you remove the oxygen and recheck the patient without overly stimulating him. He appears to be alert and oriented times three with clear lung fields and had no signs of respiratory depression; he can walk to the bathroom on his own and back without assistance. The patient is safe for discharge and receives a referral to addiction counseling and treatment.

## Clinical Application
The results of this study provide weak evidence applicable to EMS determining refusal of transport decisions after heroin overdoses, emergency department physicians assessing the same patients once brought into the emergency department, and naloxone distribution programs using bystanders or lay people

**What do I tell my patient?**
You have just had a heroin overdose and you feel better because we gave you a drug that blocks the effects (naloxone). If you feel like you are getting worse you should return immediately. Preferably go home or be with someone that can observe you for a period of time. If you would like to be referred to a detox program or perhaps even get a prescription for naloxone to take home we can start talking about that as well.
