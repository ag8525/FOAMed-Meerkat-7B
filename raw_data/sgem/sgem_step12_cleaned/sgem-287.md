## Summary
In the evaluation of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and white blood cell count (WBC) can aid in diagnosis, with CRP being the most accurate among them. CRP levels below 10 mg/L are useful for ruling out pneumonia, while levels above 50 mg/L are indicative of pneumonia. However, reliance solely on biomarkers in the emergency department is cautioned against, as they should not replace clinical judgment and imaging, such as chest x-rays, which remain the standard for diagnosing CAP. The study emphasizes the integration of CRP into clinical workflows due to its availability and cost-effectiveness, but highlights the necessity of clinical correlation and imaging for accurate diagnosis and management.

## Case
A 47-year-old healthy, non-smoker, presents to the emergency department (ED) with a productive cough, fever and says it has been difficult to breathe for the past four days. He appears well, with a temperature of 38.7 Celsius, heart rate of 90 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and room air oxygen saturation of 91%. On auscultation, you hear some fine crackles at the bases. You wonder if there is value in ordering any bloodwork, particularly a biomarker such as C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), or a complete blood count for white blood cell count (WBC) in addition to doing a chest x-ray (CXR).

## Background
Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a prevalent condition that significantly contributes to adult morbidity and mortality. Accurate diagnosis is crucial for timely treatment and to prevent unnecessary antibiotic use in non-CAP cases. Diagnosis typically relies on an abnormal chest x-ray in conjunction with signs and symptoms of a lower respiratory tract infection. Biomarkers such as white blood cell count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin are associated with an increased likelihood of CAP, with CRP being recommended by guidelines like those from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) to minimize inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions. These biomarkers are accessible in emergency departments in the US and primary care settings in Europe, aiding in the clinical assessment and management of CAP.

## Clinical Point
What is the accuracy of biomarkers for the diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Do not rely only upon a biomarker in the emergency department to rule in or rule out community-acquired pneumonia.

## Case Resolution
You order CXR which is reported by the radiologist as a "right lower lobe infiltrated consistent with early pneumonia, clinical correlation required". You prescribe the antibiotic doxycycline and provide appropriate follow-up and discharge instructions.

## Clinical Application
We are not going to use these biomarkers routinely to make the diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia in the emergency department.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It looks like you have pneumonia in the base of your right lung on the CXR. Here is a prescription for an antibiotic. If it is a bacterial pneumonia, the medicine should work. If it is a viral pneumonia, the antibiotic is unlikely to help. Follow up with your family physician next week. Return to the emergency department if you develop a rash, get increasing shortness of breath, or are worried.
