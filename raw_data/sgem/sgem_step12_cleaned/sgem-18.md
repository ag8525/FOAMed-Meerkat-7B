## Summary
Traumatic hyphema, characterized by blood in the anterior chamber of the eye, typically results from direct trauma and is more common in young males. The condition is generally self-limiting and rarely leads to permanent vision loss. A Cochrane review evaluated various interventions, including antifibrinolytics like tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid, which may reduce the risk of secondary hemorrhage but do not improve visual acuity and can delay hyphema resolution. Non-medical interventions such as eye patching and bed rest have shown no significant benefit. In the emergency department, the recommended management for isolated traumatic hyphema is conservative, involving referral to ophthalmology without immediate medical intervention, as most cases resolve without complications.

## Case Scenario
18-year-old male who was *“doing nothing”* when someone jumped him and punched him in the face. He has a hyphema in his left eye. His visual acuity is 20/20 OU and has no other injuries.

## Background on Traumatic Hyphema
Hyphema is the presence of blood in the anterior chamber of the eye, typically resulting from direct trauma. It predominantly affects young men, with a male-to-female ratio of 3:1, and sports injuries account for 60% of cases. Although traumatic hyphemas rarely lead to permanent vision loss and often resolve without intervention, antifibrinolytics have been used orally or topically to prevent potential vision loss.

## Point
What should be done for a traumatic hyphema in the Emergency Department?

## BEEM Bottom Line
Most patients with isolated traumatic hyphema do well. Nothing seems to affect visual acuity. There may be a benefit with antifibrinolytic agents to prevent re-bleeds but delays resolution of primary bleed and has side effects. There is also no evidence for non-medical interventions. The ED management of traumatic hyphemas would be to prescribe nothing and call ophthalmology.

## Case Scenario Conclusion
This young man who was out for a night of quiet conversation and drinks and was *“doing nothing”* was referred to ophthalmology. He did not keep his outpatient appointment. You know this because he re-presented to the ED three months later with his hyphema resolved with no visual complications. However, he now has a painful swollen fifth MCP of his right dominant hand and you suspect a boxer fracture.
