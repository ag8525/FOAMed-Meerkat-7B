## Summary
In the emergency department, the evaluation of suspected pulmonary embolism (PE) often involves risk stratification using tools like the Well’s Criteria and the revised Geneva Score (rGS), which do not account for patient sex. However, a retrospective cohort study by Jarman et al. highlights sex differences in the diagnostic approach, showing that females are more likely to receive guideline-consistent care but are less frequently diagnosed with PE. The study, conducted at two U.S. academic hospitals, found that 70% of females received care consistent with guidelines compared to 63% of males, with a significant p-value of 0.003. Despite these differences, the study emphasizes the importance of using validated risk stratification tools to guide clinical decision-making and avoid unnecessary testing, as demonstrated in a case where a male patient underwent CT-pulmonary artery scanning (CTPA) without prior d-dimer testing due to high risk, while a female patient with elevated d-dimer also received CTPA. Understanding and mitigating biases in clinical practice is crucial for optimizing patient care.

## Case
During a shift in the emergency department (ED), you see two patients with pleuritic chest pain, one female and one male. Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a diagnosis you are considering for both. After d-dimer testing your female patient, you order a CT scan of the chest for the male patient. One of the nurses on shift asks you why you didn’t order a d-dimer for the male, like you did the female patient.

## Background
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a critical condition often evaluated in emergency departments when patients present with chest pain and shortness of breath. Risk stratification tools such as the Well’s Criteria and the revised Geneva Score (rGS) are commonly used to assess the likelihood of PE without immediate imaging. The Well’s Criteria includes seven weighted items, categorizing patients into low, moderate, or high risk, or as unlikely or likely to have PE. The rGS consists of eight weighted items, similarly classifying patients into low, intermediate, or high risk. Notably, these tools do not account for patient sex, despite evidence that females undergo more diagnostic testing with lower diagnostic yield and are more frequently subjected to imaging, yet are less often diagnosed with PE compared to males.

## Clinical Point
Are male and female patients equally likely to receive care consistent with guidelines for suspected PE?

## SGEM Bottom Line
When working patients up for a PE, consider the guideline and risk stratify with validated decision tools or clinical gestalt to avoid over testing and overuse of advanced imaging.

## Case Resolution
The male patient was getting a CTPA without d-dimer testing. The female patient had an elevated d-dimer, and therefore also underwent CTPA imaging. Neither patient was diagnosed with a PE.

## Clinical Application
Understanding our own biases is important. One tool to try to avoid a bias in this case is to consider using a validated risk-stratification tools to help our decision-making processes.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You explain to your nurse that when you utilized risk-stratification tools, the female patient was low risk, while the male was high, and this explains the difference in initial workups.
