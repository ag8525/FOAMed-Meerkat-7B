## Summary
A 65-year-old African American male presented with angioedema of the tongue and lips, likely induced by the ACE-Inhibitor Lisinopril, which he was taking for hypertension. Despite initial treatment with steroids, antihistamines, and epinephrine, the swelling persisted, highlighting the poor response of ACE-I induced angioedema to standard anaphylaxis therapy due to its bradykinin-mediated mechanism. A study on icatibant, a bradykinin receptor antagonist, showed a significantly faster resolution of edema compared to standard care, though its high cost and limited availability suggest it should be reserved for severe cases with potential airway compromise. The patient was managed with observation and did not require intubation, emphasizing the importance of discontinuing ACE-I in such cases.

## Case
65yo African American male presents to the emergency department complaining of swelling of the tongue/lips. It started when he woke at 08:30. He went to Urgent Care and was treated with intramuscular steroids, benadryl, and epinephrine. The swelling did not get any better. The patient was referred to the emergency department. Swelling started >4 hours ago when you see him. He states no change in the swelling. On examination, his vital signs are normal, no stridor, no respiratory distress, no hypoxia but he does have a swollen tongue and slightly slurred speech. He has a history of hypertension and the only medication he takes is the ACE-Inhibitor Lisinopril.

## Background
Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACE-Inhibitors), such as Lisinopril, are medications approved in the 1980s for treating hypertension, congestive heart failure, post-myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, and diabetic nephropathy. They function by inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, preventing the conversion of Angiotensin I to Angiotensin II, which normally increases blood vessel resistance, aldosterone, thirst, antidiuretic hormone, vascular hypertrophy, and noradrenaline. ACE-Inhibitors also prevent the breakdown of bradykinin, leading to its accumulation. Common side effects include hypotension, hyperkalemia, and a chronic dry cough, with hyperkalemia occurring in approximately 3% of users and cough affecting 5-35% of patients. The cough is believed to result from increased bradykinin and other substances, and while stopping the medication is the primary treatment, the cough may persist. Angioedema, occurring in up to 0.7% of users, is another side effect linked to bradykinin buildup, presenting in mucosal tissues and more frequently in individuals of African descent. It is generally self-limiting with a low fatality rate.

## Point
Is icatibant of any benefit in ACE-I associated angioedema?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Icatibant is an expensive drug that appears to work well for the off-label use of ACE-I induced angioedema but should be reserved for those rare cases of impending airway compromise.

## Case Resolution
This gentleman’s swelling had not gone down over a course of a few hours with Epinephrine, Steroids, and Antihistamines. He had no respiratory distress. He was not intubated as he had been stable over hours, but given the severity of tongue swelling, he was admitted for observation with a trach kit at bedside, with ENT and Anesthesiology aware of him. He had no deterioration, did not require intubation, and went home the following day, knowing not to take any more ACE-inhibitors.

## Clinical Application
I am not able to use this clinically because it is not available in places I work.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have swelling of your tongue, which is likely caused by your medication, Lisinopril. This can occur any time after starting the medicine. There is a medication that may decrease the swelling of your tongue, but I don’t know if it will improve your breathing such that you won’t need your airway protected. Harms of the medicine may include pain, swelling, itching, and redness at the injection site. One dose of this medicine costs thousands of dollars.
