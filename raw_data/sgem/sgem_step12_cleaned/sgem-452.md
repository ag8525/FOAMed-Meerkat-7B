## Summary
The document discusses the management of self-reported beta-lactam allergies in the emergency department (ED), focusing on a study by Anderson et al. that evaluated the safety of full-dose beta-lactam challenges in patients with moderate, severe, or unknown allergies. The study found that only 2.7% of patients experienced mild allergic reactions, with no anaphylactic events, supporting the safety of this approach. The document highlights the high prevalence of inaccurately reported penicillin allergies, which often lead to suboptimal antibiotic use and increased healthcare costs. It emphasizes the importance of accurate allergy verification to improve antibiotic stewardship and reduce resistance. The study's retrospective design and limitations, such as missing data and unaddressed confounding factors, are acknowledged, but the findings align with existing evidence, suggesting that EDs can safely implement beta-lactam challenges to optimize antibiotic therapy.

## Case
It’s another day, another dollar in the emergency department (ED). The next patient is a 63-year-old woman with a cough and fever. After assessment, you diagnose her with pneumonia. You would normally prescribe ceftriaxone but during your assessment, she told you she had a bad reaction to amoxicillin in her twenties, with widespread hives and some facial swelling. You wonder if she could have an allergy challenge dose of ceftriaxone in the ED.

## Background
Penicillin allergy is frequently reported, with up to 10% of the US population claiming such an allergy, though evidence suggests over 90% of these cases are not true allergies. This mislabeling often results in the use of alternative antibiotics, which can be more costly, less effective, and contribute to antibiotic resistance. In emergency settings, where rapid decision-making is crucial, the inability to verify these allergies can lead to the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Studies, such as those by Raja et al., indicate that penicillin skin testing reveals most self-reported allergies as false positives. The historical belief in cross-reactivity between penicillin and other beta-lactams is being reconsidered, with recent insights suggesting that reactions may be due to the R1 side chain rather than the beta-lactam ring itself. Efforts to de-label patients, such as direct oral challenges, have shown success in ICU settings, though not yet evaluated in emergency departments. Initiatives to accurately assess and remove penicillin allergy labels are crucial for improving antibiotic stewardship, reducing healthcare costs, and addressing antibiotic resistance.

## Clinical Point
What are the outcomes of patients with documented moderate, severe or unknown beta-lactam allergies challenged with a full-dose administration of a beta-lactam antibiotic in the ED?

## Case Resolution
You talk to the patient about the low rates of cross-reactivity between amoxicillin and ceftriaxone and she agrees to have a test dose of ceftriaxone in the ED. You ensure that this is administered in a closely monitored area and take the opportunity to explain to your nursing colleagues caring for her your rationale.

## Clinical Application
You put an item on the agenda for the next ED staff meeting to work with your pharmacy and ID colleagues to formalize an antibiotic challenge policy.
