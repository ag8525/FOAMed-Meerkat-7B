## Summary
In the context of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), the use of epinephrine has been shown to increase the rate of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and survival to hospital admission, but it does not improve survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological outcomes. The Perkins et al. study demonstrated a statistically significant increase in 30-day survival with epinephrine compared to placebo, but this was accompanied by a higher incidence of severe neurological impairment among survivors. The evidence, including the OPALS trial and subsequent studies, suggests that while epinephrine may enhance short-term survival metrics, it does not translate into improved long-term outcomes or quality of life. Therefore, current protocols should prioritize interventions with proven benefits, such as high-quality CPR and early defibrillation, over routine epinephrine administration in adult OHCA cases.

## Case
A 51-year-old man experiences a cardiac arrest on the street. You are the first provider on scene with Emergency Medical Services (EMS) and start high-quality Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR). A cardiac defibrillator is hooked up and the patient is in ventricular fibrillation. He is unsuccessfully shocked. An oral airway is placed, peripheral intravenous (IV) line started successfully and the paramedic asks her partner if you want to administer IV epinephrine?

## Background
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical emergency where the American Heart Association (AHA) recommends a five-step Chain-of-Survival, with Step Four focusing on advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) skills such as intubation and intravenous drug therapy. The clinical significance of this step is debated, as studies like the OPALS trial and subsequent research (Olavseengen et al. 2009, Hagihara et al. 2012, Cournoyer et al. 2017) have shown that while ACLS, including the use of epinephrine, improves return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and survival to hospital admission, it does not enhance survival to hospital discharge or improve neurological outcomes. The observational nature of these studies limits their conclusiveness, and the only randomized control trial by Jacobs et al. in 2011, which also indicated better ROSC with epinephrine but not improved discharge survival, was underpowered due to insufficient sample size. Consequently, there is a lack of definitive high-quality evidence to guide the use of epinephrine in OHCA.

## Clinical Point
Does the use of epinephrine in cardiac arrest improve survival rates with a favorable neurological outcome?

## SGEM Bottom Line
The use of epinephrine in adults with OHCA to improve survival with favorable neurologic outcome is not supported by the literature and protocols should be changed to reflect the data.

## Case Resolution
The patient is loaded into the ambulance with high-quality CPR being provided and epinephrine is given automatically as part of the existing protocol. The patient has ROSC and survives to hospital admission but dies soon after in the ICU.

## Clinical Application
Resuscitation efforts should focus on things that have been demonstrated to improve patient-oriented outcomes like high-quality CPR and early defibrillation. In contrast, epinephrine should not be routinely given to adult patients with OHCA.
