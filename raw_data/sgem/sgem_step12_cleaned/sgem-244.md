## Summary
In the management of atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (RVR) in the emergency department, intravenous magnesium sulfate (MgS) has been evaluated as an adjunct to traditional rate control agents. A randomized controlled trial by Bouida et al. compared low (4.5g) and high (9g) doses of MgS to placebo, demonstrating that MgS can enhance rate control when combined with AV nodal blockers, though the higher dose was associated with more side effects such as flushing and transient hypotension. The study found that the low dose was more effective in achieving rate control and sinus rhythm conversion at 24 hours compared to the high dose, suggesting a potential ceiling effect of MgS. Despite these findings, the external validity of the trial is questioned due to the predominant use of digoxin, which is less common in North American practice where diltiazem or beta-blockers are preferred. The SGEM bottom line suggests that while MgS may be beneficial as an adjunct, its use should be balanced against the risk of side effects.

## Case
You are working in your local freestanding emergency department (ED). This is an ED not physically attached to a hospital, for the non-American listeners. A 64-year-old male patient presents with a feeling of “palpitations” for about one week. His heart rate is 130-140 beats per minute, irregular, and his EKG shows atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (RVR). You want to control his rate and have recently heard some of your colleagues talking about using intravenous magnesium in addition to their typical rate control agents.

## Background
Atrial fibrillation is the most common cardiac arrhythmia, often presenting in the emergency department (ED) with symptoms such as increased heart rates, chest pain, and weakness. The management of atrial fibrillation involves a debate between rate control and rhythm control, with the United States favoring rate control and Canada more often employing rhythm control, particularly for recent onset cases (less than 48 hours). In Canada, an aggressive protocol from Ottawa uses procainamide and electrical cardioversion to rapidly restore normal sinus rhythm, with a study by Stiell et al reporting a 97% discharge rate from the ED and 93% achieving normal sinus rhythm. For chronic atrial fibrillation or cases with unknown onset and rapid ventricular response, rate control and anticoagulation are standard. A randomized controlled trial reviewed by Dr. Anand Swaminathan found that diltiazem achieves more rapid rate control than metoprolol in the ED, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 2. Magnesium has been explored as an alternative or adjunct for rate control, deemed safe and effective, though its use in the ED and optimal dosing remain under-researched.

## Clinical Point
Can IV magnesium sulfate reduce the ventricular rate safely and effectively in ED patients with rapid atrial fibrillation?

## SGEM Bottom Line
In patients receiving rate control for atrial fibrillation in the ED, magnesium may be a useful adjunct but can be associated with more side effects.

## Case Resolution
You decide to give your patient diltiazem for rate control and within one hour, the ventricular rate is approximately 95-100 bpm.

## Clinical Application
When giving rate control medications for rapid atrial fibrillation, specifically digoxin, magnesium can be considered as an adjunctive agent, with the caveat that minor side effects may be increased.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your heart rate is very high and irregular. We are going to treat you with a medication that should bring it down over the next few hours. If that doesn’t work, there are other options we may consider.
