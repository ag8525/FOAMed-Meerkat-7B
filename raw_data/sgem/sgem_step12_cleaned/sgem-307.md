## Summary
The document discusses the management of a 3 cm laceration on a patient's forearm, focusing on reducing the pain of local anesthetic injections. It highlights the use of buffered lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate to decrease the burning sensation during infiltration, with a 3:1 mixing ratio being more effective than a 9:1 ratio. The study referenced involved healthy volunteers and demonstrated that buffered lidocaine significantly reduces pain compared to unbuffered solutions. Despite the practical challenges such as the short shelf life of buffered solutions and the need for small volumes, the document suggests considering this method to improve patient comfort during wound care. The clinical application emphasizes the importance of addressing patient concerns about pain and using buffering techniques to enhance the experience of local anesthesia.

## Case
A 35-year-old female arrives to the emergency department with a 3 cm laceration to the palmar surface of her left forearm sustained by a clean kitchen knife while emptying the dishwasher. The patient reports a fear of needles and has concerns about locally anaesthetizing the area because, "I got stitches on my arm once before and that shot burned like crazy!" The patient asks the practitioner if there is any chance she can get a shot that "burns less" than her last one.

## Background
Wound care in the Emergency Department involves addressing both patient and clinician priorities, with patients often prioritizing function for non-facial wounds and cosmetic outcomes for facial wounds, while clinicians focus on preventing infection. Standard practices include using tap water for wound cleaning instead of sterile solutions, and non-sterile gloves are sufficient for simple wound treatment, reserving sterile gloves for procedures like lumbar punctures. Epinephrine-containing local anesthetics are safe for use on extremities without risk of tissue necrosis. Not all simple lacerations require sutures; for instance, hand lacerations under 2cm can be treated with glue, and scalp lacerations can be managed by criss-crossing hair. The practice of buffering lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate to reduce injection pain was reviewed in a Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 23 studies, though it was later withdrawn due to non-compliance with sponsorship policies, and the review will not be updated by the non-conflicted authors.

## Clinical Point
Does buffering lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate make local anesthetic less painful?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Consider buffering your lidocaine with a 3:1 sodium bicarbonate mixture to decrease the discomfort of local anesthetic infiltration.

## Case Resolution
You inform her that there is a way to make the local injection burn less. You mix up your 1% lidocaine in a 3:1 mixture with sodium bicarbonate. She leaves very happy with post-suture instructions.

## Clinical Application
If the patient expresses fears about the anesthetic injection, buffering the lidocaine may be a suitable option. The practical aspects of how sodium bicarbonate comes (volume) and short shelf life limit the generalizability to use in all patients at this time.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I want to make sure you are all numbed up around your cut. This will make it easier for you and for me to do the stitches. I know the “freezing” can really burn and this can be scary for many people. I can buffer the injection with something called bicarb. Mixing it with the lidocaine can help lessen the burn and pain from injection.
