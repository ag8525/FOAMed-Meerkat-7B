## Summary
The document discusses the management of septic shock in a rural emergency department setting, focusing on the comparison between Early Goal Directed Therapy (EGDT) and usual care. The ProCESS trial, a multicenter study, found no significant difference in mortality between EGDT, protocol-based care, and usual care, suggesting that invasive monitoring may not be necessary for effective resuscitation. Key management steps include early recognition, intravenous crystalloid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and lactate screening. The study supports the use of crystalloids over colloids and emphasizes the importance of early intervention, aligning with the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines. The findings are particularly relevant for smaller or rural settings lacking critical care facilities, as they validate less invasive approaches while maintaining effective patient outcomes.

## Case Scenario
You are working in a rural community emergency department. The next patient you see is a 71-year-old man who has been sick for three days with fever, chills, and a productive cough. On arrival, the vital signs were as follows: Temp 38.7C, HR 110, RR 24, BP 95/60 (after a 500cc normal saline bolus by ambulance), oxygen saturations 88% on room air. Skin looks mottled, and the patient seems to be confused on questioning.

You diagnose the patient to be in septic shock, and administer another IV crystalloid bolus, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and oxygen by mask. Your hospital does not have critical care facilities, and if the patient requires intubation or invasive vascular monitoring (e.g., CVP, arterial line for MAP), the patient will have to be transferred out to another larger center.

You are aware that the Early Goal Directed Therapy protocols mandated in the 2013 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines include such invasive measures. You are also aware that there have been numerous concerns that such aggressive invasive measures may not be necessary for resuscitating septic patients, and that more conservative measures (intravenous crystalloid boluses, broad-spectrum antibiotics, lactate screening) may be just as effective.

## Point
Is early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) or other protocol-based care superior to usual care for septic shock patients in the ED?

## Background
Early Goal-Directed Therapy (EGDT) is a treatment protocol for sepsis that was shown to reduce mortality from 47% to 31%, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 6, according to a single-center randomized controlled trial by Dr. Emmanuel Rivers. EGDT emphasizes early recognition of sepsis, as each 60-minute delay in treatment can increase mortality by 7.5%. The protocol includes administering intravenous fluids, specifically a 30cc/kg IV bolus of crystalloids, which are preferred over colloids. Normal saline or Ringer's lactate can be used, with the latter not affecting lactate levels. Broad-spectrum antibiotics are also a critical component, targeting common sources such as respiratory, genital, and urinary infections. The approach involves the use of vasopressors, inotropes, and blood transfusions, with central venous catheter placement for monitoring.

## Clinical Application
This information is what most ED physicians have been waiting for since the original EGDT paper in 2001, and confirms what most already suspected: generate a protocol based on early recognition, intravenous crystalloids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and lactate screening. This is **READY FOR PRIME TIME, NOW**!

## What do I tell my Patient
It looks like you have a serious infection. We are going to give you intravenous fluids, intravenous antibiotics, and admit you to the hospital.

## Case Resolution
71-year-old man with sepsis probably from a respiratory infection. You have given him 2L of fluid now and intravenous antibiotics. He is looking a little better, his blood pressure is responding, and lactate level is going down. You discuss the case with the patient and the family. Ask them if they would like to be transferred to a higher level of care with central monitoring or stay locally. They decide to stay in your rural facility and consider transfer if he takes a turn for the worst.
