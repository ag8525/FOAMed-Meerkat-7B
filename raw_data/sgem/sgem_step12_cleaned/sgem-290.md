## Summary
The document discusses the effectiveness of emergency physicians versus neurologist-led stroke teams in administering thrombolytic therapy for acute ischemic stroke. The study found that neurologists administered tPA 13% more frequently than emergency physicians, but this did not translate into statistically significant differences in patient outcomes, such as the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at discharge. The study, conducted at a single center, highlights potential biases, including differences in time to thrombolysis and the possibility of treating stroke mimics. Despite neurologists' higher tPA administration rates, the lack of significant outcome differences suggests that the absence of a neurologist-led team may not adversely affect patient outcomes. The study's limitations include its retrospective nature and lack of comprehensive harm data, such as intracranial hemorrhage rates. The findings suggest that while neurologist-led teams may increase tPA administration, they do not necessarily improve patient-oriented outcomes.

## Case
56-year-old female with sudden onset of left arm and leg weakness with slurred speech presents to the emergency department (ED). She was last seen well two hours prior. Her past medical history includes hypertension and type II diabetes. She is not on any anticoagulation except ASA. There is no previous history of stroke. The neurology led stroke team is not available and you wonder if that will affect her outcome.

## Background
Acute ischemic stroke is a medical condition characterized by the sudden loss of blood flow to a part of the brain, leading to potential brain damage. Its clinical significance is underscored by its high prevalence and the associated risks of morbidity and mortality. The use of thrombolytics, a treatment aimed at dissolving blood clots to restore cerebral blood flow, has been a subject of debate among medical professionals. Concerns about the evidence supporting thrombolytic therapy in acute ischemic stroke have been notably raised by Dr. Jerome Hoffman, highlighting the ongoing discussion regarding its efficacy and safety in clinical practice.

## Clinical Point
Does the presence of a neurologist led stroke team affect the likelihood of receiving tPA and does that improve a patient-oriented outcome?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Neurologists led stroke teams give tPA more often but it did not result in statistically significant better patient-oriented outcomes in this study.

## Case Resolution
Based on this study you can reassure the patient that the lack of a neurologist led stroke team may decrease her chances of getting thrombolysis (clot busting drug) but that will probably not affect her outcome.

## Clinical Application
It appears that while a "neurologist led stroke team" may be important for other reasons, it appears that in the absence of one only decreases the chance of getting tPA but doesn’t affect the outcome. It is unsure how a neurologist led stroke team would impact outcomes in the new era of endovascular treatment (EVT).

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You appear to be having a stroke and we have a system in place to treat your stroke even though the neurologist is not here at this moment. We will take good care of you and the evidence is that your outcomes will be just as good as if the stroke team was led by a neurologist in our hospital.
