## Summary
The document discusses the management of symptomatic hyponatremia, focusing on a 60-year-old male patient with severe hyponatremia (serum sodium of 118 mmol/L) due to thiazide diuretic use. The SALSA trial compared two treatment strategies: rapid intermittent bolus (RIB) and slow continuous infusion (SCI) of hypertonic saline. Both methods were found effective and safe, with no significant difference in the risk of overcorrection, although RIB showed a lower incidence of therapeutic relowering and achieved target sodium levels more quickly. The trial supports the use of RIB as a preferred treatment strategy, aligning with current guidelines, and highlights the importance of careful monitoring to prevent complications such as osmotic demyelination syndrome. The case resolution involved administering a 2 ml/kg bolus of 3% saline, resulting in symptom improvement and cessation of the thiazide diuretic.

## Case
A 60-year-old man presents to the emergency department (ED) after his wife found him to be drowsy and confused at home. He had been vomiting that morning. He had a background of hypertension treated with a thiazide diuretic. His wife reports that he had experienced diarrhea in the week prior to this presentation. On arrival to the ED, his vitals are normal aside from a decreased level of consciousness, and he is found to have a serum sodium concentration of 118 mmol/L. You are unsure as to the best way to correct his sodium and are aware that rapid overcorrection may lead to an osmotic demyelination syndrome. However, he is also at risk of a seizure and further harm at this level.

## Background
Hyponatremia, defined as a low sodium level, is the most prevalent electrolyte abnormality in clinical practice, affecting 14% to 42% of hospitalized patients and associated with high mortality. Traditionally, symptomatic hyponatremia has been managed with a slow continuous infusion of hypertonic saline to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), which includes central pontine and extrapontine myelinolysis. Recent expert consensus guidelines advocate for rapid, intermittent boluses of hypertonic saline, citing benefits such as rapid partial correction of hyponatremia, reduced risk of overcorrection, and elimination of complex calculations. However, prior to the SALSA trial, there was limited randomized data supporting the superiority of either treatment approach, with most studies focusing on marathon and ultra-marathon runners, a population not commonly encountered in emergency departments.

## Clinical Point
When treating symptomatic hyponatremia, what are the risks of overcorrection in patients using either a slow continuous infusion vs. a rapid intermittent bolus of hypertonic saline strategy?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Both rapid intermittent boluses of hypertonic saline and slow continuous infusions appear efficacious strategies with no statistical difference in safety.

## Case Resolution
Our patient is admitted to the high dependency area and receives a 2 ml/kg bolus of 3% saline over 20 minutes. He feels much better and is monitored closely. A second bolus at 6 hours relieves his symptoms. His thiazide diuretic is ceased and he is monitored on the ward.

## Clinical Application
This trial demonstrates the efficacy and apparent safety of two strategies to correct hyponatremia. Hospitals can use this data to develop their own protocols to manage these patients. Although this is a negative trial, it does give us a lot of important data to guide our care.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We are going to give you a dose of salty water in your cannula (IV) over 20 minutes. It should fix the salt imbalance in your blood. We will monitor you closely and give you another dose if needed. We hope to relieve your symptoms as soon as we can safely.
