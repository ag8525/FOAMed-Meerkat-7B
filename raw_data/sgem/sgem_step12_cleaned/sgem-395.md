## Summary
In the management of uncomplicated anterior epistaxis, the use of intranasal tranexamic acid (TXA) has shown promise in reducing the need for anterior nasal packing and shortening emergency department stays, as demonstrated in a single-center, double-blind RCT conducted in a specialized ENT ED in Iran. The study found that TXA was superior to usual care, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 7 to prevent anterior packing, and it also reduced rebleeding within 24 hours. Despite the positive findings, the study's external validity is limited due to its specific setting and patient population, and the standard care practices may differ from those in other regions. While TXA is a cost-effective and low-risk intervention, its application should be considered in the context of local practice standards and patient characteristics.

## Case
Jim is a 50-year-old male who presents to the emergency department with anterior epistaxis. He reported it started last night in his bedroom where he used a space heater. He denies nose picking. He tried applying pressure, but it didn’t work. Vitals are stable and he is not on any anticoagulants.

## Background
Epistaxis, commonly known as nosebleed, is a frequent clinical condition, particularly anterior epistaxis, which involves bleeding from the front part of the nasal septum. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 216 adult patients demonstrated that topical tranexamic acid (TXA), a synthetic lysine derivative that inhibits fibrinolysis, was effective in stopping bleeding within 10 minutes, reducing discharge time to under 2 hours, minimizing rebleeding within 24 hours, and improving patient satisfaction compared to anterior nasal packing. TXA has been evaluated in various medical conditions, showing a 1.5% absolute mortality benefit in trauma (CRASH-2) but no significant mortality or primary outcome benefits in isolated traumatic brain injury (CRASH-3), postpartum hemorrhage (WOMAN), gastrointestinal bleeding (HALT-It), or intracranial hemorrhage (TICH-2 & ULTRA). A subsequent RCT confirmed TXA's efficacy in patients on antiplatelet medications. However, the NoPAC trial, the largest double-blinded RCT with 496 participants, found no reduction in the need for anterior packing with intranasal TXA, particularly in patients who had already failed initial pressure and packing with a vasoconstrictor, and used a lower TXA dose. This trial's differing primary outcome and high percentage of anticoagulant users contributed to mixed results, leading to uncertainty about TXA's role in epistaxis management. Further research, such as the trial by Hosseinialhashemi et al., aims to clarify TXA's efficacy in uncomplicated anterior epistaxis.

## Clinical Point
Should we use TXA for uncomplicated anterior epistaxis?

## SGEM Bottom Line
It is reasonable to add TXA to your cotton pledgets for adults with uncomplicated anterior epistaxis.

## Case Resolution
Jim blew out his nose and you applied cotton pledgets soaked with vasoconstricting solution and TXA. His epistaxis resolved without the need for anterior packing. You provide him instructions to avoid nose picking, you improved patient satisfaction and ED flow.

## Clinical Application
If TXA is rapidly available, consider adding it to patients with atraumatic anterior epistaxis in your cotton pledgets. An NNT 7 for decreasing the need for anterior packing is clinically important, TXA is cheap, and a single topical application is very unlikely to cause harm.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Blow your nose to remove some of the blood. Then we will put a cotton pack up your nose. It will have two medications on the cotton. One medication stops bleeding by pinching off the blood vessels. The other medication stops bleeding by helping your body form a clot/scab. We will leave the packing in for 15 minutes to see if it works. After 15 minutes it will be pulled out. If you are not bleeding, you can be on your way with some advice. If it is bleeding, we will pack it, send you home and see you back tomorrow.
