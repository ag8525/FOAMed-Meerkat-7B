## Summary
In the context of a 58-year-old female presenting with a sudden severe headache, a CT scan confirmed subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), raising the Point of whether tranexamic acid (TXA) could improve outcomes. The ULTRA trial, a randomized controlled study, evaluated the efficacy of ultra-early TXA administration in patients with CT-confirmed SAH. The study concluded that TXA did not improve clinical outcomes at six months, as measured by the modified Rankin Scale, with no significant difference in primary or secondary outcomes, including mortality and adverse events. The trial's open-label design and potential selection biases were noted, and while TXA showed no superiority over usual care, the study's findings challenge its routine use in SAH management. Current practice remains unchanged, emphasizing the importance of timely neurosurgical intervention and monitoring, with TXA not recommended for routine use in SAH due to insufficient evidence of benefit.

## Case
You’re working a busy evening shift in your community emergency department (ED) when a 58-year-old female presents with a rapid onset terrible intensity headache. She has no significant headache history and you are concerned for subarachnoid hemorrhage so you order a head CT which confirms your suspicions. You page neurosurgery at the bigger ED in town, and while you wait for the page back, you wonder if giving tranexamic acid (TXA) could help improve the patient’s chances for a good outcome given its effects in other bleeding processes.

## Background
The Ottawa SAH Rule is a clinical decision tool designed to identify subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) in alert patients over 15 years old with a sudden, severe non-traumatic headache reaching peak intensity within an hour. It excludes patients with new neurological deficits, previous aneurysms, SAH, brain tumors, or recurrent headaches. Initially deemed not ready for widespread use, the rule was later validated by Dr. Perry's study, showing 100% sensitivity but only 13.6% specificity. Despite its high sensitivity, the rule requires further external validation and impact analysis before broad adoption. The diagnostic approach for SAH often involves a CT scan, with studies suggesting that a CT performed within six hours of headache onset by a neuroradiologist may negate the need for a lumbar puncture. However, the necessity of lumbar puncture post-normal CT remains debated, with a UK study indicating a high number needed to tap to diagnose an aneurysm missed by CT. The use of tranexamic acid (TXA) in SAH treatment was evaluated in the 2018 TICH trial, which found no significant benefit over placebo in improving outcomes, suggesting that routine TXA administration is not currently recommended. While TXA has been considered for various conditions, its efficacy in SAH remains unproven, and further evidence is needed to support its use.

## Clinical Point
Does rapid administration of tranexamic acid in patients with CT confirmed SAH improve clinical outcome?

## SGEM Bottom Line
TXA use in subarachnoid hemorrhage does not presently have sufficient evidence of improving patient outcomes to recommend its routine use.

## Case Resolution
You start nimodipine and monitor the patient’s blood pressure closely. Neurosurgery accepts the patient and you transfer the patient to the larger hospital in town.

## Clinical Application
No change in current practice, but it is helpful to know the limitations to the uses of TXA and to avoid falling for indication creep.
