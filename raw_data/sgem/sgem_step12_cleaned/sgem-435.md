## Summary
Nirsevimab, a monoclonal antibody, has been approved for preventing RSV-associated hospitalizations in infants, demonstrating an 83.2% efficacy in reducing hospitalizations for RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections compared to standard care. The Phase 3b trial included 8,058 infants and showed that 0.3% of those receiving nirsevimab were hospitalized versus 1.5% in the standard care group. The study also reported a 75.7% efficacy in preventing very severe RSV infections requiring supplemental oxygen. Despite the promising results, the trial's open-label design and industry involvement raise concerns about potential biases. Nonetheless, nirsevimab is recommended by the CDC and AAP for infants entering their first RSV season, offering immediate protection without relying on the infant's immune response.

## Case
A 4-month-old twin girl is brought by her parents to the emergency department (ED) for respiratory distress. She has had congestion, a runny nose, and a cough for the past three days. Her parents think her breathing has been getting worse, and she is breathing faster. On your examination, you see that she is tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 66 breaths per minute. You also note subcostal retractions. Her oxygen saturation on room air is 86%. After nasal suctioning, she remains tachypneic, but her oxygen saturation remains under 90%. A viral swab comes back positive for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). The decision is made to put her on supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula and admit her to the hospital for close observation. Her parents tell you, “She has a twin brother at home. We heard about this new vaccine for RSV. Does it work?”

## Background
Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a significant cause of respiratory illness in young children, commonly leading to bronchiolitis with symptoms such as coughing, wheezing, and difficulty breathing. It is a major reason for infant hospitalizations, particularly during the fall and winter months. Efforts to develop an RSV vaccine began in the 1960s with a formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine, which resulted in worsened infections and fatalities, delaying vaccine development. Advances in molecular biology and immunology in the 2000s renewed interest in RSV vaccines, leading to the exploration of protein subunit, vectored, live-attenuated, and mRNA vaccines. Recent clinical trials have focused on vaccines for infants, older children, and at-risk adults, including the elderly and pregnant women. In 2022 and 2023, Nirsevimab, a monoclonal antibody, was approved in the EU, UK, US, and Canada for RSV prevention. Nirsevimab targets a critical viral protein, providing immediate protection by neutralizing RSV without relying on the recipient's immune response, marking a shift towards monoclonal antibody therapy for RSV prevention.
