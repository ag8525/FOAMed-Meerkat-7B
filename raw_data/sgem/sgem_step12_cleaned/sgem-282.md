## Summary
In the case of a 33-year-old female presenting with pleuritic chest pain, the clinical focus is on ruling out pulmonary embolism (PE) using a pretest probability-based approach. Despite an elevated d-dimer of 850 ng/ml, the patient is considered low risk according to the Well’s criteria, allowing for the application of the PEGeD strategy, which adjusts the d-dimer threshold to <1,000 ng/ml for low-risk patients. This approach, supported by the Kearon et al. study, suggests that imaging can be safely avoided in such cases, reducing unnecessary exposure to radiation and potential overdiagnosis. The study highlights the importance of Bayesian thinking in clinical decision-making, emphasizing that a combination of low clinical pre-test probability and an adjusted d-dimer can effectively rule out PE without imaging, thus aligning with the ADJUST PE and YEARS criteria. This strategy is particularly relevant in environments like Canada, where it can lead to a significant reduction in diagnostic imaging, though its applicability in different healthcare systems may vary due to differing medico-legal pressures.

## Case
You are caring for a 33-year-old female who comes to your emergency department with chest pain. She was attending the incredible EM Vision 2020 conference and while listening to Dr. Alan Drummond talk about over-crowding, she developed sudden onset of pleuritic chest pain.

She is not on any hormone replacement therapy, no history of venous thromboembolism, has not been immobile, has no hemoptysis, and has no history of malignancy. Her vital signs are as follows: heart rate 105 beats/minute, blood pressure 110/70, respiratory rate 18 breaths/minute, oxygen saturation 96% on room air. You order a d-dimer test to rule out pulmonary embolism (PE) and it comes back at 850 and your first reaction is DOH!!

You think to yourself, *“well my hands are tied”* and you are about to order the CT angiogram when you remember hearing about a PEGeD study. She is Well’s score low, but how are you going to handle that elevated d-dimer?

## Background
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a critical condition characterized by the obstruction of pulmonary arteries, often presenting with non-specific symptoms common to other conditions, making diagnosis challenging. The D-dimer test, a highly sensitive but low-specificity assay, is traditionally used in PE diagnosis, with a threshold of 500 ng/ml to determine the need for further imaging such as CT angiography or ventilation-perfusion scans. Recent advancements include the ADJUST PE Study, which proposes an age-adjusted D-dimer threshold for patients over 50, calculated as age multiplied by 10, to improve diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, the YEARS criteria, which consider clinical signs of deep vein thrombosis, hemoptysis, and the likelihood of PE, allow for a higher D-dimer cut-off of 1,000 ng/ml if the score is zero, while maintaining the traditional cut-off for scores of one or more. These approaches aim to refine the diagnostic process by incorporating Bayesian thinking, which emphasizes the interpretation of test results based on pre-test probability and clinical context.

## Clinical Point
Can a clinical pretest probability-based D-dimer safely rule out the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism without imaging?

## SGEM Bottom Line
It is reasonable to use the PEGeD strategy to safely rule out PE in low-risk patients.

## Case Resolution
With our patient who is low risk C-PTP and a d-dimer under 1,000 ng/ml, it is reasonable to rule out PE and forgo any further chest imaging in our Canadian practice environment.

## Clinical Application
Adjusting d-dimer threshold to less than 1,000 ng/ml for low risk clinical pre-test probability patients can reduce imaging and appears safe.

We were concerned you might have had a blood clot in your lung. This can be a very serious and even deadly problem. We ran some blood tests and they were normal. It’s very unlikely you have a blood clot. Now we need to look for other causes of your chest pain.
