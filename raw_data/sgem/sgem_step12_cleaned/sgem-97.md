## Summary
In the case of a 36-year-old male presenting with symptoms suggestive of renal colic, the clinical focus is on the diagnostic approach for nephrolithiasis in the emergency department. The document discusses a study comparing ultrasonography to computed tomography (CT) for diagnosing kidney stones, highlighting that ultrasound is associated with lower radiation exposure without compromising diagnostic accuracy or increasing adverse outcomes. The study, involving 2,759 patients, found no significant differences in high-risk diagnoses or serious adverse events between the two modalities, with ultrasound offering the advantage of reduced radiation. The clinical application suggests that emergency physicians should consider ultrasound as a first-line diagnostic tool for suspected nephrolithiasis to minimize radiation exposure, reserving CT for cases where further evaluation is necessary. The case resolution involved successful management of the patient's symptoms with ultrasound confirming a 4mm distal ureteral stone, followed by appropriate analgesic and antiemetic treatment, and arranging urological follow-up.

## Case
A 36-year-old previously healthy white male is attending his family's Thanksgiving supper. He develops waves of right flank pain associated with vomiting. One of the relatives suggests it might be food poisoning. This does not go over well with the matriarch of the family. A second relative suggests he has probably been drinking too much, which leads to another argument among some of the family members. Another relative who is a nurse suggests it could be renal colic and suggests he goes to the emergency department for assessment.
He arrives at the emergency department doing the hippy hippy renal colic shake. He describes the pain as sharp/stabbing and that has progressively become worse since onset. He says that over the last couple of hours he has felt that it is radiating down towards his groin. The pain has been associated with nausea and vomiting. He denies seeing any blood in his urine and has not experienced any pain with urination. There has been no diarrhea. He denies having a fever or a history of renal colic. The ibuprofen he took last night provided some temporary pain relief.

## Background
Renal colic is a severe pain caused by kidney stones obstructing the urinary tract, particularly the ureter. It is clinically significant due to its prevalence and the intense discomfort it causes, often leading to emergency department visits. A randomized clinical trial in France assessed the efficacy of tamsulosin, an alpha-blocker, for aiding the expulsion of distal ureteral stones, but found no superiority over placebo in a small sample of 129 patients. Additionally, a Cochrane Systematic Review by Dr. Andrew Worster evaluated the use of fluids and diuretics for renal colic, concluding that there is no reliable evidence supporting their effectiveness. Another Cochrane review indicated that tamsulosin is generally ineffective for ureteral colic in emergency settings unless the stone is larger than 4mm. These findings highlight the need for effective diagnostic and treatment strategies for renal colic.

## Clinical Point
In ED patients with suspected renal colic, is ultrasound as effective as CT as a diagnostic tool?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Bedside emergency department ultrasound is safe and has several advantages over CT for the diagnosis of kidney stones.

## Case Resolution
The 36-year-old man who presented looking like renal colic gets an ultrasound. This confirms a 4mm stone in the distal ureter. His pain and vomiting have settled in the department with intravenous ketorolac and ondansetron.
You write him a prescription for analgesia and antiemetics. You also arrange a follow-up with a urologist and provide him with strict return precautions. He thanks you and you leave the room.

## Clinical Application
Emergency medicine physicians should consider ultrasound for suspected nephrolithiasis when appropriate. We could be doing a favor for our patients in reducing radiation exposure, and are not putting the patient at increased risk or harm.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I would tell my patients that it looks like you have a kidney stone because you are doing the hippy hippy shake. We should start with ultrasound to lessen your exposure to radiation. Radiation increases the chance of developing cancer later in life. If further concerns arise, we can always get a CT scan. The ultrasound is just as good at diagnosing kidney stones and we may be able to get you out of the emergency department faster.
