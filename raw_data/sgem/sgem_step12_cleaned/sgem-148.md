## Summary
The document discusses the use of skin glue to secure peripheral intravenous (IV) catheters in the emergency department (ED) to reduce failure rates. A randomized controlled trial by Simon Bugden et al. demonstrated that applying cyanoacrylate glue to IV sites significantly decreased the failure rate from 27% to 17% at 48 hours, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 10. The study highlights that while skin glue is effective in reducing dislodgement, its application may not be necessary for all patients, particularly those not admitted for extended periods. The trial's limitations include its single-center design, exclusion of certain patient groups, and lack of cost analysis. Despite these limitations, the study supports considering skin glue for high-stakes IVs where catheter retention is critical.

## Case
You are working hard in the emergency department. It is a busy night and you are working flat out dealing with the stream of patients. In the middle of this busy night one of your nurses asks you to resite an IV on an 80-year-old lady with confusion and urosepsis. You had placed the IV yourself under ultrasound guidance earlier and it took some time and effort. The line has fallen out and she needs re-siting before heading off to the ward. Your nursing colleague asks if you want to glue this one to stop it from getting pulled out?

## Background
Intravenous (IV) placement is a common invasive procedure in emergency departments, essential for critically ill patients but not without risks such as infection, restricted movement, and the need for frequent monitoring and replacement. These issues can detract from other clinical care priorities and pose risks to patients. The use of cyanoacrylate glue, or skin glue, has been explored to secure central venous catheters and arterial lines, particularly in high-stakes situations where line dislodgement could be dangerous. While peripheral IVs are often used primarily for blood sampling, their secure placement remains crucial in cases where delays in administering IV fluids or antibiotics could harm the patient. Thus, securing IVs effectively is a significant clinical intervention.

## Clinical Point
Does the addition of skin glue decrease the failure rate of emergency department inserted peripheral intravenous catheters compared to standard peripheral intravenous catheter care?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Skin glue does appear to decrease the failure rate of IVs in patients admitted to hospital from the ED at 48 hours. We do not know if this is a good idea for all ED patients and we do not know the true effect size, but for high stakes cannulas that we really want to stay in this intervention should be considered.

## Case Resolution
On the basis of this trial, this patient would appear to be a good candidate for glue fixation of her cannula. You resite the line using a dab of skin glue to fix it in place. She leaves for the ward looking happier already.

**What do I tell my patient?**
We know that about 1 in 5 IVs fail in the first 48 hours. It looks as though you will need one for at least a couple of days. Putting a little skin glue on the IV site can make it less likely to fail. The skin glue makes it a little trickier to remove but we think it is a good idea to use it in your case.
