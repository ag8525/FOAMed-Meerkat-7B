## Summary
In the evaluation of febrile infants, lumbar puncture (LP) is a critical procedure to assess for invasive bacterial infections such as meningitis. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined the impact of infant positioning during LP on procedural success and adverse events. The study found no significant difference in LP success rates between lateral decubitus and sitting positions, though lateral decubitus was associated with increased episodes of bradycardia and oxygen desaturation. The review highlighted the need for further research using validated pain scoring tools to better understand the impact of positioning on pain and other patient-centered outcomes. Clinicians are advised to perform LPs in the position they are most comfortable with, as no position has been shown to significantly improve success rates.

## Case
A five-day-old girl is brought to the emergency department (ED) for fever by her parents. She was born full-term and seemed to be doing well after the family returned home. Her mother had an uneventful pregnancy and delivery. Today, the parents thought she was feeling warm and took the girl’s temperature, which was 101°F (38.3°C). They called the pediatrician, who told them to go to the ED for more testing and warned them of the likelihood that their baby may need a lumbar puncture. The worried father asks you, “What is a lumbar puncture? Will it hurt?” Her mother asks you, “Is it like when I got an epidural before delivering? Will you sit her up for it? She can’t sit yet.”

## Background
A lumbar puncture (LP) is a medical procedure commonly performed in febrile infants in the emergency department to evaluate for invasive bacterial infections, such as meningitis. The procedure involves specific techniques and considerations, including patient positioning and analgesia. Infants are typically positioned either in the lateral decubitus position, with the neck flexed and knees drawn to the chest, or seated upright with the head and shoulders bent forward. Proper positioning often requires assistance to hold the infant, which can occasionally lead to episodes of oxygen desaturation if maintained for extended periods.

## Clinical Point
How does the positioning of infants during lumbar puncture (lateral decubitus vs sitting vs prone) affect success rates and adverse events?

## Case Resolution
After obtaining blood and urine studies, you explain the process of a lumbar puncture to the family and the reasoning behind why you are performing it. After the parents’ consent to the procedure, you follow the appropriate sterile procedures and have someone help hold the baby in the position that you are most accustomed to performing an LP. After you successfully obtain CSF, start empiric antibiotics, and admit the baby to the hospital.

## Clinical Application
Perform the LP in the position you are most comfortable with. Be ready to troubleshoot if the LP attempt is unsuccessful.

**What Do I Tell the Family?**

We do not know if she is having a fever because she has a virus or if she has bacteria in her urine or blood. The last thing we test is the cerebrospinal fluid or CSF. This is the fluid that surrounds her brain, and we do not want to miss an infection there. Let’s talk a bit more about what the process of a lumbar puncture to get CSF will look like.
