## Summary
The document discusses the historical practice of bloodletting in the treatment of camp fever, a form of epidemic typhus, during the Peninsular War. It highlights a study involving 366 soldiers, where bloodletting significantly increased mortality, with a Number Needed to Harm (NNH) of 4. The study lacked blinding and detailed baseline characteristics, raising concerns about its validity. Despite historical use, modern understanding and evidence suggest bloodletting is harmful, particularly in febrile illnesses like typhus, and should not be used. Current management of such infections focuses on antibiotics and supportive care, emphasizing prevention over cure. The document concludes that bloodletting is detrimental and should be abandoned in favor of evidence-based treatments.

## Case
23-year-old soldier presents to the medical tent with camp fever. On examination, “the pulse is frequently, full and soft. Respirations somewhat affected. Heat increased. Anorexia. Thirst. Tongue furred. Urine high coloured. Constipation.”

## Background
Epidemic typhus, also known as camp fever, is a form of typhus caused by the bacterium Rickettsia prowazekii and transmitted by the human body louse. It often results in epidemics following wars, such as during the Peninsular War, and is characterized by high fever, rash, cough, myalgia, headache, hypotension, delirium, and can lead to sepsis, septic shock, and death if untreated. Modern treatment involves antibiotics and supportive care. Historically, treatments included aromatics, essential oils, tonics, opium, ether, and bloodletting, a practice rooted in the ancient humoral theory that persisted into the 20th century despite evidence of its ineffectiveness. Bloodletting was widely used for various diseases and was performed by surgeons, often in barbershops, symbolized by the red-and-white-striped pole. Although largely abandoned, bloodletting, now termed phlebotomy, remains a treatment for specific conditions like polycythemia and hemochromatosis.

## Clinical Point
Does bloodletting a soldier with camp fever increase chances of survival?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Bloodletting is harmful for soldiers with camp fever and cannot be recommended.

## Case Resolution
The soldier is provided with the usual care but does not have bloodletting performed. He recovers and goes on to fight another day.

## Clinical Application
Given the mounting evidence of the potential harm of bloodletting for febrile illnesses, the lance should not be employed any longer to treat patients with camp fever or other febrile illnesses.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It looks like you have camp fever. We are going to treat you with the best medical care possible. This used to involve bloodletting but we have found this to hasten death. Therefore, we will not be employing the lance at this time.
