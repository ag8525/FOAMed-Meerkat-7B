## Summary
Hands-on defibrillation during ongoing chest compressions in cardiac arrest is a debated practice due to potential risks to rescuers from electrical exposure. While high-quality chest compressions and rapid defibrillation are critical for survival in ventricular fibrillation, studies, including those by Lemkin et al., indicate that current personal protective equipment may not adequately protect rescuers from electrical energy during hands-on defibrillation. Despite some reports of feasibility, the risk of electrical exposure remains significant, with measured rescuer exposure voltages and energy doses exceeding safe levels. Therefore, the practice is not recommended, and compressions should be paused briefly for defibrillation to ensure rescuer safety.

## Case
You receive in your emergency department a 55-year-old male who goes into cardiac arrest minutes after he arrives. He's rushed into the shock room and CPR is started. Chest compressions are ongoing and as soon as the monitor pads are attached, you notice the patient to be in ventricular fibrillation. A resident next to you says that he has read that the patient can be shocked without stopping chest compressions. Everyone in the room immediately turns their eyes on you to make the call and the intern doing CPR asks if it's really safe to do so.

## Background
Defibrillation is the primary treatment for rhythm disturbances such as ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia, with rapid and early defibrillation significantly increasing survival rates after cardiac arrest. While initial CPR before defibrillation was considered, a systematic review by Simpson et al. found no advantage in delaying defibrillation. High-quality chest compressions are crucial, as interruptions can worsen survival outcomes, as emphasized by the American Heart Association guidelines. The concept of hands-on defibrillation, which aims to minimize interruptions in chest compressions, has been explored, with studies indicating that rescuers wearing polyethylene gloves did not feel shocks during defibrillation, although some shocks exceeded safe current levels. The safety of this approach remains debated, particularly concerning the electrical exposure to rescuers. A study by Sullivan and Chapman assessed the protective efficacy of various glove types, finding that a significant percentage of both single and double gloves allowed current flow during defibrillation, with only a few gloves limiting current to safe levels.

## Point
Is it safe to shock a patient during ongoing chest compressions (so-called hands-on defibrillation)?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Performing hands-on defibrillation poses a risk and it’s a practice that should NOT be performed.

## Case Resolution
You quickly state to the team that a recent study in resuscitation demonstrates that hands-on defibrillation is potentially dangerous and should not be done. You keep the compressions going while you charge the defibrillator to avoid longer pauses but do stop compressions for the electrical discharge and immediately resume CPR.

## Clinical Application
Although you can find many case reports of people doing hands-on defibrillation, this study shows that there’s a risk involved and that this practice should be avoided. Instead, you can charge the defibrillator during the compressions and just stop for a brief period to give the discharge and resume compressions immediately.

**What Do I Tell Patients (Providers)?**
The evidence available to this day shows that hands-on defibrillation is potentially dangerous and should not be done.
