## Summary
The document discusses the use of inhaled budesonide in high-risk outpatients with COVID-19, focusing on the PRINCIPLE trial, which evaluated its efficacy in reducing hospital admissions, deaths, and illness duration. The trial found no significant difference in hospitalization or death rates between the budesonide and usual care groups, but a quicker recovery was noted in the budesonide group. However, the study's unblinded design and the addition of a subjective primary outcome (illness duration) limit the reliability of these findings. The authors conclude that while budesonide may improve recovery time, its impact on more objective outcomes remains uncertain, and further research is needed. The document emphasizes the importance of maintaining rigorous scientific standards during the pandemic and suggests that inhaled budesonide should not be routinely used for COVID-19 management without more definitive evidence.

## Case
A 65-year-old woman with a history of diabetes, hypertension, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) presents with three days of fever, cough, and myalgias. She is fully vaccinated against COVID-19. Her husband tested positive for COVID-19 yesterday, and she used a home rapid test this morning that is also positive. Her vitals signs are all normal and she feels well enough to isolate at home. As you are preparing to discharge her, she asks if there is anything you can prescribe her to help. She thinks her friend might have been prescribed a puffer of some sort.

## Background
COVID-19 is a respiratory infection primarily affecting the lungs, and systemic steroids have been shown to improve outcomes in severe cases. The potential benefit of inhaled steroids, such as budesonide, has been explored due to the pulmonary nature of the disease. Initial observational data suggested an association between inhaled steroids and increased mortality in COVID-19 patients with asthma and COPD, likely due to confounding factors where sicker patients are more frequently prescribed steroids. The STOIC trial, a phase 2 open-label randomized control trial, investigated inhaled budesonide in patients with mild COVID-19 symptoms and found a significant reduction in COVID-19-related urgent care visits, emergency department assessments, or hospitalizations in the budesonide group (15% vs 3%, p=0.009). However, the trial's unblinded design, composite outcome, and early termination limit the reliability of these findings.

## Clinical Point
Does inhaled budesonide improve clinical outcomes in high-risk outpatients with COVID-19?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Currently, there does not appear to be a role for the routine use of inhaled budesonide in the management of COVID-19.

## Case Resolution
You discuss the risks, benefits, and costs of inhaled budesonide, as well as the significant remaining uncertainty with your patient. She decides against treatment.

## Clinical Application
After two randomized control trials, it remains unclear if inhaled budesonide has any role in the management of COVID-19. It may relieve symptoms, but it also may just be an expensive placebo. This is a well-known medication with a relatively low risk of side effects, so shared decision making is reasonable in the face of this uncertainty.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
There is a puffer that has been tested against COVID-19. At this point, we don’t know if it provides any real help, but it might help relieve your cough a day or two earlier. However, all medications have possible side effects. This is a steroid puffer that has been used for a long time, and it's pretty safe if used for the short term, but side effects like thrush, sore throats, and pneumonia do occur. It is also relatively expensive. I do not routinely recommend it, but if you want to know more, I am happy to discuss further.
