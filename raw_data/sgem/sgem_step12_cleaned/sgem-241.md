## Summary
The document discusses the management of opioid overdose, focusing on the use of naloxone and the Hospital Observation Upon Reversal (HOUR) rule for risk stratification and early discharge from the emergency department. The case study involves a 33-year-old male who was successfully revived with naloxone after an opioid overdose. The HOUR rule, validated by Clemency et al., suggests that a one-hour observation period may suffice for discharge if the patient is ambulatory, with normal vital signs and a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15. The study found that the HOUR rule and clinician judgment had similar sensitivity and specificity, but both missed some adverse events, highlighting the importance of clinical judgment. The study supports the use of the HOUR rule as a tool, but emphasizes that it should complement, not replace, clinical judgment in discharge decisions. The patient in the case was discharged with a naloxone kit and offered addiction counseling, underscoring the importance of follow-up care and monitoring.

## Case
A 33-year-old man arrives via emergency medical services (EMS) after initially being found unresponsive with an oxygen saturation of 89%, respiratory rate of six, a systolic blood pressure of 75 mmHg, and pinpoint pupils. The EMS crew observes drug paraphernalia and suspect an intravenous (IV) opioid overdose. They quickly place an IV line and start a fluid bolus of normal saline; supplemental oxygen is applied and 1mg of naloxone IV given. He is alert and oriented times three with normal vital signs by the time he arrives in the emergency department. Sixty minutes after receiving naloxone he is GCS 15 and walking to the desk demanding to be discharged.

## Background
Opioid overdose, a critical public health issue, has resulted in nearly 400,000 deaths in the United States from 1999 to 2017, with opioids involved in two-thirds of all drug overdoses in 2016. The opioid crisis has evolved through three waves: an increase in prescription opioid overdose deaths in the 1990s, a surge in heroin-related deaths starting in 2010, and a significant rise in deaths from synthetic opioids like fentanyl beginning in 2013. Opioids can depress heart rate and breathing, leading to fatal overdoses. Naloxone, an opioid antagonist, is the standard treatment for opioid overdoses and is increasingly accessible to first responders. It can be administered via various routes, including intranasal and intravenous. Traditional guidelines suggested a four to six-hour observation period for opioid overdoses, but a systematic review by Willman et al. in 2017 recommended a one-hour observation if patients exhibit normal ambulation, vital signs, and a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15, based on the St. Paul’s Early Discharge Rule. This Clinical Decision Rule, developed in Vancouver, BC, has not been externally validated.

## Clinical Point
Can the Hospital Observation Upon Reversal (HOUR) rule be used to risk stratify patients for safe discharge from the emergency department after suspected opioid overdose?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Clinical judgement is important and should not be underrated. This study supports the use of clinical judgement in the decision to discharge suspected overdose patients, although caution is required, as there were a number of adverse events missed with both clinical judgement and the clinical decision tool.

## Case Resolution
Using your clinical judgement and the HOUR tool, you discharge the patient home with a prescription for a naloxone kit and offer a referral to an addiction counsellor.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have just had an opioid overdose and you feel better because we gave you a drug that blocks the effects (naloxone). If you feel like you are getting worse, you should return immediately. We would prefer if you go home with someone who can keep an eye on you for a period of time. If you would like to be referred to a detox program, we can arrange for you to meet with someone. We are also giving you a prescription for a naloxone kit to take home.
