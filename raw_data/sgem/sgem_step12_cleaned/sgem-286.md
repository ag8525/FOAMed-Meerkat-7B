## Summary
The document discusses the effectiveness of N95 respirators compared to medical masks in preventing influenza and flu-like illnesses among healthcare workers. A randomized clinical trial (ResPECT) found no significant difference in the incidence of laboratory-confirmed influenza between those wearing N95 respirators and those wearing medical masks. The study, conducted over four years with 2,862 participants, highlighted that adherence to mask use was self-reported, which could affect the results. The authors concluded that N95 masks do not offer a statistical advantage over medical masks in clinical settings. The document emphasizes the importance of vaccination, hand hygiene, and mask use around patients with suspected respiratory illnesses as effective preventive measures.

## Case
With the potential global impact of the coronavirus (COVID-19) and our rural emergency departments (ED) having an extremely low compliance rate for N95 mask fit testing, our ED administration sends an urgent request for everyone to have N95 mask testing as soon as possible (ASAP). The urgent email also requests shaving facial hair. You wonder about the evidence supporting the initiative and if there is any recent evidence surrounding N95 masks usage for preventing health care workers from getting acute respiratory illnesses.

## Background
The 2009 H1N1 pandemic prompted hospitals to fit healthcare workers with N95 masks, believed to be more effective than surgical masks in preventing small particle transmission. However, it was unclear if this would reduce viral respiratory infections in healthcare settings. As the H1N1 transmission resembled that of seasonal influenza, the use of surgical masks was reinstated in most settings. With the emergence of potential coronavirus outbreaks, preventive measures have gained importance. Studies on mask efficacy include a 2009 non-inferiority trial by Loeb et al., which found surgical masks non-inferior to N95 respirators for flu prevention among Ontario nurses. MacIntyre et al. conducted a 2009 cluster RCT indicating low mask adherence in households, though mask wearers showed reduced clinical infections. A 2011 study by MacIntyre et al. in China demonstrated a significant reduction in respiratory illnesses with N95 masks, though it may have been underpowered. A 2016 systematic review by Smith et al. concluded that while N95 masks showed a protective advantage in labs, there was insufficient data to confirm their superiority over surgical masks in clinical settings.

## Clinical Point
Are N95 masks superior in preventing flu or flu-like illnesses in hospital workers compared to medical masks?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There appears to be no statistical advantage to the N95 respiratory mask over surgical or medical mask for hospital employees in preventing flu or flu-like illnesses.

## Case Resolution
You respond to the email from the nursing administration to follow hospital guidelines, continue N95 mask testing, and to follow hospital policies. However, I think it is important to stress to all staff to get their flu shot, consistently wash their hands, stop touching their face, and wear a mask if a patient has suspected or confirmed respiratory illness.

## Clinical Application
This study will increase my use of a mask (medical mask or N95) with all suspected patients arriving at an ED or outpatient setting. We should also focus on things that have been demonstrated to have a benefit like vaccination and good hand washing.

**What Do I Tell the Staff?**
Get immunized against influenza, wash your hands well and often, try not to touch your face, and wear a mask when around patients who have suspected or confirmed respiratory illness.
