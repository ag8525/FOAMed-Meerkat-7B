## Summary
Pediatric cervical spine injuries (CSI) are rare but critical to diagnose accurately to prevent severe consequences. The document discusses the challenges in using Clinical Decision Rules (CDRs) for detecting CSIs in children following blunt trauma, highlighting the lack of a dedicated, accurate CDR for pediatric patients. Existing CDRs, such as PECARN, Canadian C-spine Rule, and NEXUS, show high sensitivity but low specificity, leading to potential overuse of imaging and unnecessary radiation exposure. The systematic review of 21,379 children indicates insufficient evidence to recommend one CDR over another, emphasizing the need for well-designed studies to evaluate their accuracy. Clinical judgment remains crucial, as CDRs may not outperform clinician assessment, particularly in diverse clinical settings. The case resolution demonstrates the application of clinical assessment over CDRs, with the decision to remove the C-collar based on the absence of neurological deficits and neck pain.

## Case
A 4-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department (ED) by Emergency Medical Services (EMS) after falling from a tall tree. The fall was witnessed by his family. They tell you that he is going through a Spiderman phase and tries to climb everything. They saw him slip off the tree and landed in the grass below. He did not have any loss of consciousness. EMS placed him in a C-collar and brought him to you. On examination, you only notice a few scrapes, but he is cradling his left arm and complaining that it hurts. There looks to be an obvious deformity of his forearm. The family members ask you, “he seems uncomfortable in the neck collar, can we remove it? Do you think he injured his neck?”

## Background
Pediatric cervical spine injuries (CSI) are uncommon but can lead to severe outcomes if not properly identified. Diagnostic imaging for CSI typically involves X-rays or CT scans, especially in acute scenarios, though these methods pose a risk of radiation exposure to children. Clinical Decision Rules (CDRs) are employed to guide clinicians in making informed decisions, aiming to reduce unnecessary imaging while ensuring significant injuries are detected. However, there is no standardized protocol for diagnosing CSI in children following blunt trauma, leading to variability in practice depending on the clinician, institution, and resources. Unlike adults, for whom CDRs like the Canadian C-spine Rule and NEXUS criteria exist, there is no specific, reliable CDR for pediatric patients.

## Clinical Point
Which triage tools or Clinical Decision Rules (CDRs) are most effective for detecting cervical spine injuries in pediatric trauma patients?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There are many CDRs for pediatric CSI. There is no conclusive evidence to recommend one over another.

## Case Resolution
You examine the child after he receives some medication for his arm pain. He can obey all your commands. He has no neurological deficits. As you palpate his C-spine, he denies any midline tenderness. You carefully remove his C-collar and assess the range of movement of his neck. He has full range of motion and no pain, so you do not obtain any additional imaging at this time.

## Clinical Application
Dr. Ward is polytheistic – takes the PECARN retrospective factors (diving, high-risk MVC, predisposing condition, altered mental status, torso injury, neck pain, torticollis, neuro deficits) + neck tenderness on the exam (NEXUS) – if none of those factors are present then the collar comes off.
It is easy enough to point out why each of these CDRs has challenges – estimates not very precise, lower specificity etc. but there is reason to believe that they are better than clinical gestalt, and if you are waiting on a perfect rule before being willing to adopt it, you might be waiting a long time…

**What Do I Tell the Patient/Family?**
I’m sorry your child fell out of the tree. Despite the fall, he looks pretty good. He is not having neck pain currently, and I am not finding anything concerning on his neurological examination. I think we can take the C-collar off right now. We will need to get some X-rays of his arm to see if it is fractured.
