## Summary
The document discusses a case of a 71-year-old patient with uncomplicated left-sided diverticulitis discharged without antibiotics, leading to conflict with the primary care provider. It highlights a systematic review on conflict in emergency medicine, identifying task and relationship issues as common sources of conflict in the ED, such as disagreements on clinical decisions and interpersonal miscommunications. Factors contributing to conflict include individual inexperience, team biases, and systemic pressures like high workload. Strategies to mitigate conflict include communication training, standardized protocols, and fostering interdepartmental relationships. The case resolution involved creating a protocol for diverticulitis management to align with recent guidelines, emphasizing the importance of collaborative decision-making and conflict resolution in clinical practice.

## Case
A 71-year-old patient with a history of hypertension and well-controlled diabetes mellitus without organ involvement presents with left lower abdominal pain, afebrile, blood pressure 138/70 mm Hg, heart rate 82 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation on room air 99%. They are afebrile and tolerate oral intake. The emergency department (ED) evaluation reveals an unremarkable chemistry panel with normal renal function and a white blood cell count of 10,000. An abdominal pelvic CT scan demonstrates uncomplicated left-sided diverticulitis. The patient is feeling well enough to go home and you discharge the patient to home without antibiotics and ask them to follow up with their primary care provider or return if they get worse. In follow-up, the patient’s primary care provider is upset that the patient was not started on antibiotics.

## Background
Workplace conflict in healthcare is defined as a process arising from perceived differences and opposition regarding interests, beliefs, or values, often involving task and relationship issues. In the healthcare setting, particularly in emergency departments (ED), conflicts are exacerbated by factors such as incompatible personal motivations, high workload, stress, role ambiguity, and poor leadership, which can impede teamwork and decision-making, thereby compromising patient safety and potentially leading to moral injury. Conflicts in the ED are frequently linked to clinical decision-making and actions, contributing to adverse patient events and access block issues. While individual studies have explored various conflict factors, comprehensive reviews specific to the ED are lacking. Understanding the individual, team-level, and systemic contributors to conflict among ED clinicians is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate these conflicts.

## Clinical Point
What drives conflict in emergency medicine and are there strategies to reduce conflict?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Conflict in the ED may arise through several different pathways. Bias, burnout, culture, and local protocols for treatment and disposition may help mitigate conflict.

## Case Resolution
The emergency physician works with the department, gastroenterology, primary care, and surgery to create buy-in on a diverticulitis protocol that adheres to recent guidelines recommending the select use of antibiotics in uncomplicated diverticulitis.

## Clinical Application
Conflict may arise from personal, team and institutional level factors. Departments could consider ways to create a culture of shared respect, information sharing, and examining where local conflict occurs to create tailored solutions.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Many physicians may disagree with patient care or disposition treatments based on expertise, experience, understanding of the evidence, as well as other operational features. In many cases, there are several reasonable treatment pathways and clinicians may just be more comfortable with one or the other. We are working to address systemic issues.
