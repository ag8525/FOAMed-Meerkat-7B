## Summary
The document discusses the increased emergency department (ED) utilization by youth before and after sustaining firearm injuries, highlighting a potential for early intervention. The retrospective cohort study by Kemal et al. analyzed youth aged 10 to 19 years with firearm injuries in 2019, revealing that 12.8% had ED visits in the 90 days prior and 22.1% in the 90 days following the injury. The study found that many of these visits were related to mental health and assault, with a significant portion of post-injury visits involving recurrent trauma. The authors suggest that general EDs, which often lack pediatric-specific resources, should implement firearm injury prevention and intervention strategies. However, the effectiveness of such interventions remains unproven, necessitating further research. The case resolution emphasizes the importance of recognizing patterns of ED visits as predictors of future behavior, prompting the initiation of a violence intervention program targeting at-risk youth.

## Case
Your non-US emergency department (ED) has recently been shaken by the attendance of a teenager with a gunshot injury. Subsequent investigation has found he attended a different hospital in the region six weeks ago with a stab wound. The team wonders if that attendance was an opportunity to intervene.

## Background
Firearm injuries have become the leading cause of death among youth in the United States, overtaking motor vehicle collisions. While emergency medicine effectively addresses the immediate clinical management of gunshot wounds, there is a need to explore pre- and post-emergency department (ED) visit interventions. The ED often acts as the primary healthcare contact for youth exposed to community violence, and patterns of ED use, such as visits for mental health crises or minor injuries, may offer opportunities for early identification and prevention of firearm injuries. Survivors of firearm injuries are at increased risk for repeat injuries, psychological trauma, and mortality, highlighting the importance of understanding post-injury healthcare utilization. This is particularly crucial in general EDs, which may lack pediatric-specific resources, potentially leading to missed intervention opportunities.

## Clinical Point
Do youth with firearm injuries have increased emergency department utilization before and after their injury compared to their peers?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Firearm-injured youth frequently present to the ED both before and after injury, representing potential missed opportunities for prevention and follow-up care.

## Case Resolution
The second ED visit wasn’t entirely unpredictable. The best predictor of future behavior is often past behavior. Recognizing this pattern, your hospital begins piloting a violence intervention program targeting youth with prior ED visits for assault or behavioral concerns. The plan is to collect data and see if the intervention has a positive impact.

## Clinical Application
ED providers could use visit history to flag high-risk youth and advocate for violence intervention referrals, social work consults, or follow-up care plans. However, there is a lack of evidence that such interventions would be effective. Further studies are needed to test various interventions, especially in non-pediatric EDs.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have had a couple of visits to the ED recently. We are worried about you. Are you interested in talking to someone about the violence in your life?
