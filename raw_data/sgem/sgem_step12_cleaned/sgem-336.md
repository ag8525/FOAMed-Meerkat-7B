## Summary
The document discusses the role of hypothermia in post-cardiac arrest care, particularly following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) with ventricular fibrillation. Initial studies in 2002 suggested hypothermia improved neurological outcomes, but subsequent trials, including the TTM and TTM2 trials, found no significant benefit of hypothermia over normothermia in reducing mortality or improving functional outcomes at six months. The TTM2 trial, which compared hypothermia (33°C) to normothermia (≤37.5°C), showed no difference in death rates or poor functional outcomes, with hypothermia associated with higher rates of arrhythmias causing hemodynamic compromise. The findings suggest that hypothermia should not be prioritized in post-arrest care, emphasizing the need for further research to determine the necessity of any temperature management. The document highlights the importance of skepticism and evidence-based practice in adopting medical interventions.

## Case
A 58-year-old man collapsed in front of his family. When paramedics arrived, they found him to be in cardiac arrest, with ventricular fibrillation on the monitor. Paramedics managed to get return of spontaneous circulation after a single defibrillation, but the patient is still comatose on arrival. The charge nurse turns to you and asks: should I grab the ice packs?

## Background
Hypothermia, a treatment involving the reduction of body temperature, became a key component of post-cardiac arrest care following two influential trials in 2002. The first trial by Bernard et al. involved 77 patients with ventricular fibrillation who achieved return of spontaneous circulation but remained comatose, showing improved neurological outcomes in the hypothermia group compared to normothermia. However, the trial's methodology was criticized for lack of true randomization and blinding, and its statistical significance was questioned. The second trial, the Hypothermia After Cardiac Arrest (HACA) study, involved 273 patients and demonstrated a 14% absolute reduction in mortality and better neurological outcomes with hypothermia. Despite these promising results, the trials' limitations led to skepticism within the medical community. Consequently, the larger Target Temperature Management (TTM) trial in 2013 compared hypothermia targets of 33°C and 36°C, finding no significant difference in mortality or neurological outcomes, thus questioning the superiority of hypothermia over normothermia. This prompted the subsequent TTM2 trial to further investigate the efficacy of hypothermia in post-arrest care.

## Clinical Point
Does hypothermia result in improved survival after cardiac arrest as compared to controlled normothermia?

## SGEM Bottom Line
The combination of the TTM and TTM2 trials make it clear that hypothermia does not result in better outcomes for post-arrest patients. It remains to be seen whether there is any benefit from targeted normothermia.

## Case Resolution
Based on these new findings, you spend your time searching for an underlying cause of this patient’s cardiac arrest and optimizing the many other aspects of high-quality supportive care, but don’t worry too much about the patient’s temperature.

## Clinical Application
There does not appear to be any value in inducing hypothermia in the emergency department for our comatose post-arrest patients.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
The patient is comatose. However, when talking to their family, I might say: You may have heard of something called hypothermia, where in the past we have tried to cool patients down after having a cardiac arrest. However, the latest evidence suggests that hypothermia is unhelpful, so we can focus our attention on other more important aspects of your loved one’s care.
