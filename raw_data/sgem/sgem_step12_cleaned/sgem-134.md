## Summary
In the context of acute, non-traumatic headaches suggestive of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), the traditional diagnostic approach of non-contrast CT followed by lumbar puncture (LP) is scrutinized. A study involving 2,248 patients with negative CT scans revealed that LPs have a low diagnostic yield for SAH, with only 4% positive results and a number needed to tap (NNTap) of 250 to identify one aneurysm. The Ottawa SAH Tool, a clinical decision aid, may reduce missed SAH diagnoses but requires further validation. The study highlights the limitations of LP, including inconclusive results and potential complications, suggesting that modern multi-slice CT scans may suffice in ruling out SAH, thus questioning the necessity of routine LPs in this setting.

## Case Scenario
A 34-year-old woman presents with acute onset of headache peaking in 30 minutes with no recent trauma, focal deficits, and a normal neurologic examination.

## Background
Headaches account for approximately 2% of emergency department visits, with 1-3% of these cases resulting in a diagnosis of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). SAH is often misdiagnosed, with about 5% of cases initially overlooked, partly due to the absence of neurological deficits in 50% of presentations. The Ottawa SAH Tool, developed by Dr. Jeff Perry and colleagues, aims to improve diagnostic accuracy by using six variables to determine the necessity of a CT scan, potentially reducing the miss rate of SAH to nearly zero, though it requires further validation. Traditionally, SAH diagnosis involves a non-contrast CT scan followed by a lumbar puncture (LP), but Dr. David Newman has challenged the necessity of LPs after a negative CT scan, highlighting that 700 LPs are needed to identify one SAH requiring intervention, raising questions about the procedure's routine use.

## Clinical Point
In emergency department acute headache patients, how frequently does LP diagnose SAH after an unremarkable CT scan?

## SGEM Bottom Line
In this study, one patient would be diagnosed with SAH out of every 250 patients receiving an LP who presented to the emergency department with a headache that did not have their bleed identified on CT scan.

## Case Resolution
You are clinically concerned and get a non-contrast CT head that is negative. You discuss the risks and benefits of an LP with the patient. A shared decision is made with the patient not to do an LP. She is discharged home with appropriate analgesia. She is to return to the emergency department if she develops focal neurologic symptoms, pain increases, loss of consciousness, seizure, or is otherwise worried.

## Clinical Application
Contrary to classic teaching, LP in the setting of acute, non-traumatic headache of <12 hours duration concerning for SAH rarely yields the elusive cerebral aneurysm diagnosis and is often falsely positive or inconclusive.

**What do I tell my patient?**
Sudden onset headaches are clinically concerning for SAH, which can have devastating long-term consequences if misdiagnosed. Unfortunately, migraine headaches outnumber SAH headaches 50:1 in ED settings and do not benefit from CT-imaging of your brain, evaluation of your cerebrospinal fluid, or surgery. Modern CT scanners are very good at identifying blood in your brain from a SAH, if the CT is obtained within 12-hours of when the headache began. Older research indicated that only 9/10 SAH were detected by a CT so evaluation of fluid around your brain (CSF) was recommended to be completely certain that SAH was not the cause of the headache. Newer research indicates that CT scans almost never miss the diagnosis of SAH and that 250 lumbar punctures (LP) are needed to identify one cerebral aneurysm that CT missed. LPs are not benign procedures and can cause post-LP headaches, infections, nerve damage, and bleeding around your spinal cord.
