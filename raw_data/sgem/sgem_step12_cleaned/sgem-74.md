## Summary
The document discusses the evaluation of a 54-year-old man with shoulder pain, focusing on the likelihood of rotator cuff disease (RCD), a prevalent cause of shoulder pain. The systematic review by Hermans et al. in JAMA evaluates diagnostic tests for RCD, highlighting the painful arc test and external rotation resistance test as accurate for detecting RCD, while the external and internal rotation lag tests are most accurate for diagnosing full-thickness tears. The review notes a high prevalence of RCD in referred populations, suggesting potential verification bias and limited generalizability to emergency department settings. Management typically involves rest, ice, NSAIDs, and physical therapy, with imaging and specialist referral as needed. The study emphasizes the importance of clinical examination skills in diagnosing RCD, despite the limitations in external validity.

## Case Scenario
A 54-year-old man presents with increasing pain in his right shoulder.

## Point
Does this patient with shoulder pain have rotator cuff disease?

## Background
Shoulder pain is the third most common musculoskeletal reason for medical consultation, with 4.5 million visits annually in the USA, predominantly due to rotator cuff disease. This condition significantly affects quality of life, particularly when involving the dominant arm. The rotator cuff comprises the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis muscles, each innervated by specific nerves and responsible for various arm movements. Patients typically experience pain, functional loss, reduced range of motion, weakness, stiffness, crepitus, and sleep disturbances. Standard treatment includes rest, ice, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and physical therapy, which may extend over three months, with surgery being necessary in only a minority of cases.

## Bottom Line
Take an x-ray if you are concerned about bone injury or involvement. Conduct an examination you are confident in performing. Treat the patient's pain. Arrange for appropriate follow-up.

## Clinical Application
This study will not change my clinical practice. I will get an x-ray if concerned about bone injury or involvement. Conduct my typical shoulder examination without the specialized tests. Provide appropriate pain medication. Arrange for imaging studies depending on my clinical concern and resources (US vs. MRI). Suggest follow-up with a primary care physician or specialist depending on local practice patterns.

You can also consider referring these types of patients to your friendly neighborhood physiotherapist. Remember that it can take three months to treat rotator cuff pathology with this treatment modality.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It appears you may have rotator cuff disease. Rest your arm, apply ice, and take some NSAIDs for the pain. Follow-up with your primary care doctor in the next week or so. If it is still sore, they may suggest getting an US here or sending you to the city for an MRI. Your doctor may suggest seeing a physiotherapist if it is not improving quickly.
