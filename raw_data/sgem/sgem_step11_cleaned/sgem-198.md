**Summary:**
A study comparing intravenous push versus slow infusion of low-dose ketamine for moderate to severe pain in the emergency department found that administering ketamine over 15 minutes significantly reduces the incidence of side effects such as feelings of unreality and sedation, without compromising analgesic efficacy. The trial involved 48 patients and demonstrated that slow infusion resulted in fewer adverse effects compared to the traditional IV push method, with no difference in pain relief or need for additional analgesia. This suggests that slow infusion of ketamine is a preferable method for pain management in the ED, offering effective analgesia while minimizing undesirable side effects. The study highlights the importance of considering administration techniques to optimize patient comfort and safety in acute pain management.

**Case:**
A 54-year old female patient with acute back pain comes to your emergency department for her fourth visit in seven days for recurring pain. She was lifting something heavy and felt a “pop” in her back. She denies bowel or bladder issues, saddle paresthesia, lower extremity weakness, but does feel radicular pain down both of her legs. She has been given intravenous hydromorphone, diazepam, and ketorolac without relief of her symptoms. You decide to give her low dose ketamine, as this has worked in the past.

**Background:**
Low back pain is a prevalent and complex issue in emergency departments, often benign but challenging due to the need to rule out serious conditions like epidural abscesses, osteomyelitis, and pathological fractures. Physicians face difficulties with patient demands for imaging and concerns about opioid misuse. Red flag systems, such as TUNA FISH, help identify patients at risk for serious conditions, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Guidelines from ACEP and CAEP advise against routine lumbar imaging without red flags and recommend opioids only for severe pain unresponsive to NSAIDs or acetaminophen, with prescriptions limited in dose and duration. Managing patient expectations is crucial, as pain may persist beyond the emergency visit. Ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is gaining attention for its analgesic properties and opioid-sparing effects, though it can cause adverse effects like nausea and dizziness, which may be mitigated by slow administration.

**Clinical Point:**
Does increasing the duration of the ketamine from IV push (3 – 5 min) to a slow infusion (10 – 15 min) mitigate some of the untoward side effects, while maintaining analgesic efficacy?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Slowing down the rate of low-dose IV ketamine infusion to 15 minutes significantly reduces rates of the feeling of unreality and sedation with no difference in analgesic efficacy when compared to IV push over 3 – 5 minutes.

**Case Resolution:**
You explain to your patient that giving ketamine over a longer period of time should decrease the feelings of unreality she may have experienced in the past while still helping to improve her pain. Your patient is given IV low-dose ketamine over 15 minutes without any of the untoward side effects and almost complete resolution of her pain.

**Clinical Application:**
Low dose ketamine of 0.3mg/kg, mixed into 100 ml of Normal Saline given over slow infusion (15 minutes) has a decreased side effect (i.e hallucinations or dizziness) and equal analgesic profile when compared to IV push (5 minutes) low dose ketamine.

**What do I tell my patient?**
By slowing down the infusion of ketamine we can still relieve your pain, and potentially mitigate some of the bad side effects of this medication.
