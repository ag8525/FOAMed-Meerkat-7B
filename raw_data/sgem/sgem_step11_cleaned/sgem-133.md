**Summary:**
A 53-year-old woman presented with atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, confirmed by an irregularly irregular heart rate and EKG findings. The clinical focus is on the management of atrial fibrillation, particularly the choice between beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers for rate control. The study referenced compared diltiazem and metoprolol, finding diltiazem more effective in achieving a heart rate of less than 100 bpm within 30 minutes without increased adverse effects. Despite limitations such as sample size and dosing differences, the study supports the use of diltiazem for rapid rate control in the emergency setting. The patient was successfully managed with diltiazem, achieving rate control, and was discharged with aspirin due to low stroke risk.

**Case:**
A 53-year-old woman with no past medical history presents to the emergency department with palpitations for four days. She says she has felt fatigued and a bit short of breath. Vitals reveal a blood pressure of 153/72 and a heart rate of 137 beats per minute. On physical examination, you notice that her heart rate is irregularly irregular and a 12-lead EKG confirms that the patient is in atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response.

**Background:**
Atrial fibrillation is a prevalent dysrhythmia encountered in the Emergency Department, with atrial flutter being less common but managed similarly. The management of recent onset atrial fibrillation is debated, particularly regarding the decision to cardiovert or maintain the arrhythmia. The Ottawa Aggressive Atrial Fibrillation Protocol is effective for new onset cases but not applicable for symptoms persisting beyond four days. For chronic atrial fibrillation or cases with unknown onset and rapid ventricular response, standard emergency management includes rate control and initiation of anticoagulation therapy. Beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers are commonly used for rate control, though there is limited high-quality evidence to determine the superiority of one over the other.

**Clinical Point:**
In patients with atrial fibrillation and rapid ventricular response, what agent, beta blocker or calcium channel blocker, will obtain rate control the fastest?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The best available evidence shows that diltiazem will achieve more rapid rate control in patients with atrial fibrillation than metoprolol.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient was given 0.25 mg/kg of diltiazem as a slow push and 10 minutes after the push, her rate came down to 93 bpm. She was started on oral diltiazem to continue her rate control. The emergency physician performed a CHADS-VASC score on the patient and she was found to be very low risk for stroke. Therefore, she was started on aspirin alone and discharged home with follow-up with a cardiologist in two days.

**Clinical Application:**
Although the evidence is sub-optimal and this study has some flaws, this set of data defends emergency physicians use of diltiazem for rate control in atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It appears that the cause of your symptoms is that your heart is in an irregular rhythm called atrial fibrillation. Your heart rate is very high and so we need to give you medicine to reduce it. This will also make you feel more comfortable. We have two major medication choices but the best available evidence indicates that a medication called diltiazem will get you rate controlled the fastest.
