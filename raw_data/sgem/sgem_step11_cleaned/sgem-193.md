**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of sweet solutions, particularly sucrose, as an effective analgesic for neonates undergoing painful procedures. It highlights the risk of oligoanalgesia in pediatric patients and emphasizes that sucrose has been shown to reduce procedural pain without serious side effects, as supported by multiple studies and systematic reviews. The evidence for sucrose's efficacy became statistically significant in the early 2000s, and it is now considered unethical to conduct placebo-controlled trials given the established benefits. The document underscores the importance of using sucrose to minimize pain in newborns and advises against using codeine in children under 12 years. The clinical application involves administering sucrose to neonates before procedures to alleviate pain, and families are informed about its benefits in a relatable manner.

**Case:**
A 12-day old girl presents with fever and is otherwise well. You are planning to begin a full septic workup including some painful procedures including bloodwork. You wonder whether there is a way to mitigate the painful experience for the child.

**Background:**
Painful procedures are prevalent in the neonatal period, and there is growing evidence of long-term neurodevelopmental harm from pain in preterm infants. Oligoanalgesia, or inadequate pain control, is a concern, particularly in pediatric patients. Pain management in this group can be addressed through pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic methods, though codeine is contraindicated for children under 12 years. Sucrose has been identified as an effective non-pharmacologic treatment for reducing procedural pain in infants, with no serious side effects, as supported by a Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis and a randomized clinical trial.

**Clinical Point:**
1) What is the current evidence for the use of sucrose in neonatal infants undergoing painful procedures?  2) Using a Cumulative meta-analysis (CMA), when did the evidence for the use of sucrose become statistically significant?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
When performing painful procedures on newborns use a sweet solution to minimize the pain experience. If you are doing research on sweet solutions, please do not have your comparison group get placebo or no treatment, it’s unethical at this point.

**Case Resolution:**
In this case, prior to performing the bloodwork, catheter urine, and lumbar puncture, the child is given sucrose. Although there is some crying noted, the child appears mostly comfortable during the process.

**Clinical Application:**
In newborns experiencing painful procedures, sweet solutions, like sucrose, should be given to minimize the pain experience.

**What do I tell my patient?**
I tell the families that we use sucrose to minimize the pain their baby will experience. I often tell them it’s like giving them the world’s best piece of chocolate before poking them with a needle. They might be so distracted by the chocolate that they won’t feel the pain as much.
