**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of septic shock, focusing on the early administration of norepinephrine to achieve shock control. The CENSER trial evaluated whether early norepinephrine administration in adult patients with septic shock could lead to earlier shock resolution, defined as achieving a sustained mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 65 mmHg with adequate tissue perfusion. The study found that early norepinephrine significantly increased shock control within six hours compared to placebo, although it did not improve patient-oriented outcomes such as 28-day mortality. The trial highlighted the potential of norepinephrine to alter monitor-oriented outcomes like MAP and lactate clearance but emphasized the need for further research to assess its impact on long-term patient outcomes. Despite these findings, the trial did not change clinical practice significantly, as the primary focus remains on treating hypotension effectively in the emergency setting.

**Case:**
It’s another day in your emergency department (ED). Six hours into your shift, you finish dispo’ing the *“really quick sign-out”* from the night before. The triage nurse places a 61-year-old man with fever, hypotension, and cough into the smallest room in the ED. You scan through the EMR and see the blood pressure is 60/40. Being an astute emergency physician, you surmise that this value is one number column short of normal. It’s uncomfortably low – is it time to start a norepinephrine infusion?

**Background:**
Sepsis is a critical medical condition characterized by a systemic inflammatory response to infection, leading to severe sepsis and septic shock, which are associated with high morbidity and mortality. The landmark study by Dr. Emanuel Rivers introduced early goal-directed therapy for treating severe sepsis and septic shock, emphasizing the importance of restoring end-organ perfusion. Traditionally, this involved administering intravenous crystalloids to counteract hypovolemia, but over-resuscitation with fluids has been found to negatively affect outcomes. Consequently, early administration of norepinephrine has been proposed to improve end-organ perfusion. Clinical trials often use monitor-oriented outcomes (MOOs), such as mean arterial pressure, to evaluate the effectiveness of vasoactive infusions, although these do not always correlate with improved patient-oriented outcomes (POOs). Despite efforts to reduce the duration of shock, this has not consistently resulted in better clinical outcomes.

**Clinical Point:**
Does starting norepinephrine earlier in septic shock lead to earlier shock control?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Early norepinephrine can change some MOOs (MAP, lactate, urinary output) but does not seem to change any POOs (in-hospital or 28-day mortality) in adult patients with septic shock.

**Case Resolution:**
While a bag of isotonic crystalloid of choice is being infused through one peripheral IV, fixed-dose norepinephrine at 0.05 ug/kg/min is started through the other. The patient is then taken to the intensive care unit where he makes a full recovery after three days of treatment for his community-acquired pneumonia.

**Clinical Application:**
This trial does not change anything for me. Hypotension is an emergency and as emergency physicians, we are good at treating it. This was a well-designed trial that should generate tempered enthusiasm for early norepinephrine while further trials are being performed. The ongoing CLOVERS trial will hopefully shed some light on many of the hypothesis-generating questions that the secondary outcomes of this trial left us with.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have pneumonia. This is an infection in your lungs. It is making your blood pressure dangerously low. We can increase your blood pressure by giving you some IV fluids. We can also raise your blood pressure with a medicine called norepinephrine. This medicine can be turned up to keep your blood pressure closer to normal while we give the antibiotics time to work.
