**Summary:**
The document discusses a case of a 3-year-old boy with suspected sepsis, highlighting the use of the newly developed Phoenix Criteria for diagnosing pediatric sepsis and septic shock. The Phoenix Criteria, which focus on respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation, and neurologic systems, offer improved diagnostic performance over the previous IPSCC criteria, particularly in diverse resource settings. The criteria demonstrated high sensitivity and positive predictive value for in-hospital mortality and early death or ECMO. The case resolution involved treating the child for presumed sepsis with BiPAP and empiric antibiotics, followed by intubation and ICU transfer due to declining mental status. The Phoenix Criteria are not intended for screening but for identifying life-threatening organ dysfunction due to infection.

**Case:**
A 3-year-old boy arrives at the emergency department (ED) with a high fever, rapid breathing, and lethargy. His parents state that he has had a fever and cough for the past three days. He tested positive for influenza two days ago but seems to be getting worse. On exam, he has crackles in his right lung field. His pulse oximeter reads 88% on room air. His heart rate is elevated. He looks sleepy but is clinging to his parents. A medical trainee you are working with asks, *“He looks really sick. Is this pneumonia or could this be sepsis?”*

**Background:**
Pediatric sepsis is a critical global health issue, responsible for approximately 3.3 million deaths each year. The condition was traditionally diagnosed using the 2005 International Pediatric Sepsis Consensus Conference (IPSCC) criteria, which, despite being based on expert consensus, faced criticism for low specificity and limited adaptability across various resource settings. The Phoenix Criteria have been introduced as a more evidence-based approach, integrating data from both high and low-resource environments to improve the diagnosis of pediatric sepsis and septic shock.

**Clinical Point:**
How accurately can a new clinical decision rule (The Phoenix Sepsis Score) diagnose pediatric sepsis and septic shock in hospitalized children within the first 24 hours?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The Phoenix Sepsis score offers a new and improved evidence-based method of defining sepsis and septic shock in children.

**Case Resolution:**
You tell the trainee that the patient looks very ill. You suspect the possibility of a superimposed bacterial pneumonia in the setting of influenza, but his sleepiness is concerning for possible sepsis. He is initially placed on BiPAP and receives empiric antibiotics, but his mental status continues to decline. He is intubated and transferred to the intensive care unit for further care.

**What Do I Tell My Student:**
This patient looks very sick. It may be pneumonia, but his ill-appearance and sleepiness make me concerned that he may also be septic.
