**Summary:**
A 54-year-old female presented to the ED with abdominal pain and diarrhea, suspected to be an adverse drug event (ADE) from recent amoxicillin/clavulanate use, confirmed by a positive C. difficile stool assay. The case highlights the importance of recognizing ADEs, which are a significant cause of ED visits and can often be overlooked by physicians. A study validated clinical decision rules that can help identify patients at high risk for ADEs, showing that these tools, when used by clinical pharmacists, can effectively identify over 90% of ADEs. The study found that age, opioid, antihypertensive, and antibiotic use, recent medication changes, and the number of prescriptions were strongly associated with ADEs. The implementation of these decision rules could optimize the allocation of clinical pharmacists' expertise in the ED, improving patient outcomes by ensuring timely identification and management of ADEs.

**Case:**
A 54-year-old female presents to the emergency department (ED) with abdominal pain and profuse non-bloody diarrhea for the past 24 hours. Her vital signs are within normal limits and she is tolerating oral fluids. There have been no recent suspicious meals or water sources, camping, spelunking, sick contacts and she does not have any birds or reptiles at home. She takes no regular medications; however, she did start amoxicillin/clavulanate (Clavulin or Augmentin) for a sinus infection three days ago.

**Background:**
Preventable adverse drug events (ADEs) are significant contributors to emergency department (ED) visits, hospitalizations, and mortality, with medications being the leading cause of deaths related to medical care. ADEs account for one in nine ED visits, yet 20-50% of these events go unrecognized by emergency and inpatient physicians, leading to continued inappropriate medication use and increased morbidity and healthcare resource utilization. Clinical pharmacists, who specialize in medication management, are more adept at identifying medication-related issues in ED presentations than physicians, but their availability is limited, and routine medication reviews for all ED patients are impractical. There is a need for evidence-based criteria to better identify and treat ADEs, ensuring that high-risk patients receive evaluation by clinical pharmacists to improve outcomes.

**Clinical Point:**
Can a clinical decision rule (tool) accurately identify patients presenting to the ED with adverse drug events?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Use of a clinical decision rule (tool) by clinical pharmacists and ED physicians can help to identify patients at risk for adverse drug events in the ED.

**Case Resolution:**
Based on her recent initiation of antibiotics, you suspect this diarrhea may be due to an adverse drug event. You send stool cultures for C. difficile and tell the patient to stop the amoxicillin/clavulanate (Clavulin or Augmentin) as it is not needed for sinusitis anyway. The next day her stool assay is positive for C. difficile, and you call the patient to start her on appropriate therapy.

**Clinical Application:**
Clinical pharmacists can be a great resource to the emergency department. These two tools look promising, especially Rule #1, to micro allocate this important scarce medical resources to those patients at higher risk of ADEs. It would be great to have another impact analysis of these tools before implementing them widely to ensure that they have a net patient-oriented benefit.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have experienced an adverse drug reaction from the antibiotic used to treat your sinus infection. Antibiotics can disrupt the normal bacteria in your gut and this can sometimes result in the development of an infection called C. difficile. We will stop your other antibiotic and need to treat this infection with a different drug as it can become serious if left untreated.
