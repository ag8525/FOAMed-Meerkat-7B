**Summary:**
The document discusses the inadequacy of the "cardiac box" as a predictive tool for cardiac injury from gunshot wounds (GSWs) to the thorax. The study, based on autopsy data from Fulton County, Georgia, found no significant difference in the risk of cardiac injury between GSWs inside versus outside the cardiac box, challenging the traditional reliance on this anatomical concept. The study highlights the limitations of using autopsy data, including death bias and lack of power calculation, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to living patients. Clinically, the document emphasizes that physicians should maintain a high suspicion for cardiac injury in all thoracic GSWs, regardless of their location relative to the cardiac box, and underscores the importance of rapid assessment and intervention following Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocols.

**Case:**
You receive a call on the Biocom for an incoming Type A trauma, three minutes out. The patient is an adult male with a gunshot wound to the chest, and they’re combative with emergency medical services (EMS). Upon arrival in the emergency department (ED), the patient is incoherently speaking, has a pulse of 135 beats per minute, blood pressure of 85/50 mm Hg, and an obvious open wound in their left mid-axillary line at the level of the nipple.

**Background:**
Penetrating trauma, particularly from gunshot wounds, is a significant health concern in the United States, causing approximately 30,000 deaths annually and constituting about 10% of all trauma cases, with higher rates at certain trauma centers. At Truman Medical Center, gunshot wounds account for around 19% of traumas. Prompt identification and management of cardiac injuries from gunshot wounds are critical, leading to the development of risk stratification tools like the "cardiac box," a three-dimensional area defined by anatomical landmarks where cardiac injury risk is highest. However, the concept of the cardiac box, originally based on studies of stab wounds, may not fully apply to gunshot wounds due to their high kinetic energy, which can cause cardiac injuries from entrance wounds anywhere on the torso. Despite this, the cardiac box influences clinical perceptions and communication, particularly in non-trauma centers, potentially leading to underestimation of injury severity if wounds are outside this area.

**Clinical Point:**
Are the anatomic borders of the cardiac box adequate to predict cardiac injury from gunshot wounds?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Cardiac injury from a gunshot wound within the “cardiac box” is low but being outside the cardiac box does not rule out a possible cardiac injury.

**Case Resolution:**
Following ATLS, you and your trauma team rapidly work your way through the ABCs. The patient is turned, and no additional wounds are noted so the team rapidly proceeds to the operating room for a thoracotomy.

**Clinical Application:**
Patients with gunshot wounds to the chest must prompt the physician to suspect an underlying cardiac injury. Obviously, the patient’s overall appearance will increase suspicion of a clinically significant injury of any type, and unfortunately, the cardiac box is not a useful tool for evaluating these injuries.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your injury appears severe, and we’re going to take you back to the operating room as soon as possible. There’s a chance this gunshot injured your heart and if that happened, it needs to be taken care of right now.
