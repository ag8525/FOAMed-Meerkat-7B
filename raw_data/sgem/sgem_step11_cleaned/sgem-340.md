**Summary:**
In the management of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) with shock-refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, the administration route of antiarrhythmic medications such as amiodarone and lidocaine (intravenous [IV] vs. intraosseous [IO]) does not significantly impact survival to hospital discharge or favorable neurologic outcomes. The ALPS trial and subsequent analyses suggest that while IV administration of amiodarone showed a statistically significant benefit over placebo, no such benefit was observed with IO administration. Despite these findings, the evidence remains insufficient to prioritize IV over IO access, especially given the practical challenges in pre-hospital settings. The focus should remain on interventions with proven benefits, such as high-quality CPR and defibrillation, rather than the specific route of drug administration. EMS providers are encouraged to use IO access when IV access is not readily achievable, as the overall impact on patient outcomes is minimal.

**Case:**
An EMS crew arrives to your emergency department (ED) with a 58-year-old female who suffered a witnessed ventricular fibrillation (VF) out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). They performed high-quality CPR and shocked the patient twice before giving amiodarone via intraosseous (IO). After giving hand off the medic tells you she had difficulty finding intravenous (IV) access and went straight to an IO. She wonders if she should have spent more time on scene trying to get the IV versus the tibial IO she has in place.

**Background:**
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical emergency with significant morbidity and mortality, necessitating effective pre-hospital interventions. The ALPS randomized control trial, published in NEJM in 2016, evaluated the efficacy of amiodarone versus lidocaine in adult OHCA patients with refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, concluding that neither drug provided a clinically significant benefit. A critical appraisal of an observational study by Kawano et al. in 2018 indicated that intraosseous (IO) access was associated with poorer neurologic outcomes compared to intravenous (IV) access, though potential confounders in the study suggest caution in interpretation. Recent trends in pre-hospital care emphasize reducing cognitive load by prioritizing interventions with proven outcomes, such as favoring supraglottic airway devices over intubation and considering IO access to allow providers to concentrate on more impactful treatments.

**Clinical Point:**
Does it matter if you give antiarrhythmic medications via IV or IO route in OHCA?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We suggest focusing on the treatments that have demonstrated to provide a patient-oriented benefit (high-quality CPR and defibrillation) in adult OHCA rather than those treatments that do not have high-quality evidence to support their use (type of airway, vascular access, vasopressors, or antiarrhythmic drugs).

**Case Resolution:**
You tell the paramedic that she was right to focus on high-quality CPR and defibrillation and you give an extra kudos for placing a supraglottic device on scene. When it comes to IV versus IO it is reasonable to place an IO if IV access is not practical. At this time the evidence to support IV over IO is weak and EMS providers should continue to focus on the interventions that matter most.

**Clinical Application:**
We should continue to decrease cognitive load when possible. Given the complexity of OHCA is it reasonable to give ACLS medications through the IV or IO route knowing it is unlikely that the drugs or route of administration will make a clinical difference. It is fine to use the IO route first if the IV route is not easily obtained.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We would tell her family members that she had a cardiac arrest. CPR was done and shocks were given to her heart. She also received some IV medications. Her heart is beating now, and she is in good hands with the hospital critical care team.
