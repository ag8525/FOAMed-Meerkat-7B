**Summary:**
In the management of acute agitation in emergency department patients, droperidol has been found to be more effective and associated with fewer episodes of respiratory depression compared to lorazepam and ziprasidone, according to a randomized double-blind trial. The study involved 115 participants, primarily with alcohol intoxication, and demonstrated that 64% of patients receiving droperidol achieved adequate sedation at 15 minutes, compared to 25-35% for ziprasidone and 29% for lorazepam. Secondary outcomes indicated that lorazepam was associated with a higher need for additional sedation and more respiratory depression. Despite previous supply issues, droperidol is recommended as a preferred option for parenteral sedation in agitated patients, with verbal de-escalation techniques also being initially applied.

**Case:**
You are sitting minding your own business charting on shift when you become aware of shouting and banging from your ambulance bay. On investigating, you find a collection of nursing, EMS, and hospital security personnel surrounding an obviously agitated patient with blood on his head who is attempting to punch them. The nurse wants to know what medications he can get to chemically restrain the patient.

**Background:**
Excited delirium is a condition characterized by severe agitation and confusion, often associated with psychosis. The clinical significance of excited delirium includes its potential for causing harm to the patient and others, necessitating effective management strategies. A systematic review highlighted the inadequate evidence base for most pharmacological treatments for this condition. Haloperidol has been identified as an effective treatment for agitation due to psychosis, though it is associated with common side effects. Droperidol, another treatment option for acute severe agitation, was widely used in Australasia but faced a decline in use in the United States from 2013 to 2019 due to an FDA Black Box warning and supply issues, prompting the investigation and use of alternative agents.

**Clinical Point:**
In patients needing parenteral sedation for acute agitation, is droperidol, ziprasidone, or lorazepam intramuscularly most effective and safe?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Consider droperidol as a therapeutic option for agitated patients requiring parenteral sedation.

**Case Resolution:**
You ask the nurse to prepare for parenteral sedation with droperidol while you initially apply your techniques of verbal de-escalation.

**Clinical Application:**
In my UK practice, droperidol still isn’t widely available, so I will reach for haloperidol, but if droperidol becomes available, I am likely to use it.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
When you were first brought in, you were very distressed and a threat to yourself and others. We gave you an injection of sedative so we could treat you safely with a low risk of causing side-effects.
