**Summary:**
In the case of a 62-year-old man presenting with lethargy, hypotension, and signs of a urinary tract infection, the clinical focus is on fluid resuscitation in critically ill patients. The document discusses the ongoing debate between using normal saline and balanced crystalloid solutions like Plasma-Lyte 148 for fluid resuscitation. Recent trials, including the PLUS trial, have shown no significant difference in 90-day mortality between the two fluids in critically ill adults, suggesting that either can be used safely in the absence of brain injury. The patient in the case was treated with normal saline and antibiotics, and despite initial hypotension, responded well to treatment and was discharged home. The clinical application emphasizes that in the absence of significant brain injury, either fluid type can be used without impacting major patient-centered outcomes.

**Case:**
A 62-year-old man is brought in by EMS from home with lethargy and hypotension. Chest x-ray is clear, labs are remarkable for a leukocytosis of 16,000 with left shift; exam is notable for left flank pain and costovertebral tenderness. Straight catheter urinalysis is grossly cloudy, and pyuria is present on microscopy. Blood pressure is 85/50 mmHg. You wonder which IV fluid should you order?

**Background:**
The debate over the optimal intravenous fluid for volume resuscitation in critically ill patients has focused on the choice between 0.9% sodium chloride, known as "abnormal saline," and balanced crystalloid solutions like lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte 148, which have a chloride composition closer to plasma. Albumin is known to be detrimental to patients with brain injuries, and hydroxyethyl starch solutions are linked to kidney injury and increased mortality. Concerns about 0.9% saline include potential kidney injury due to its high chloride content. The SMART and SALT-ED trials indicated a slight advantage of balanced crystalloids in reducing Major Adverse Kidney Events within 30 days. However, the BaSICS trial, conducted in Brazilian ICUs, found no significant difference in 90-day survival between saline and Plasma-Lyte, regardless of infusion rate, although secondary outcomes suggested worse SOFA scores in the balanced crystalloid group. The PLUS trial from Australia and New Zealand further contributes to this ongoing discussion.

**Clinical Point:**
Is the 90-day mortality in critically ill adult patients lower with the use of Plasma-Lyte 148, a balanced crystalloid solution, for fluid resuscitation and therapy, than with the use of normal saline?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Plasma-Lyte is not routinely necessary for fluid resuscitation in critically ill adults in the ICU.

**Case Resolution:**
You cover the patient with piperacillin/tazobactam and resuscitate him with a total of 2 liters of normal saline. He remains hypotensive and point of care ultrasound reveals a normal ejection fraction and a plump, adynamic inferior vena cava. He is started on norepinephrine and admitted to the intensive care unit. Urine culture grows pan-sensitive E.coli and antibiotics are narrowed. He is discharged home well on hospital day three.

**Clinical Application:**
Critically ill emergency department patients without significant brain injury can be safely fluid resuscitated with either balanced crystalloids or normal saline without a large difference in patient-centered outcomes.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have very low blood pressure caused by your body’s response to a bad infection. We are treating your infection with antibiotics. We are going to give you some IV fluids to try to raise your pressure, but we may have to use some medicines to keep it up for some time, and take care of you in the intensive care unit.
