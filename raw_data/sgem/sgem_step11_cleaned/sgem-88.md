**Summary:**
The Ottawa Aggressive Protocol is a structured approach for managing recent-onset atrial fibrillation or flutter in the emergency department, emphasizing rapid cardioversion and discharge. In a cohort of 660 patients, the protocol demonstrated a high conversion rate to sinus rhythm, with 58.3% achieving conversion with procainamide and 91.7% with electrical cardioversion, resulting in 96.8% of patients being discharged home. The protocol was associated with a low adverse event rate of 7.6% and no thromboembolic events, torsades de pointes, or deaths. The median length of stay was 4.9 hours, highlighting the protocol's efficiency. Despite its effectiveness, concerns about thromboembolic risks persist, particularly in the U.S., where there is hesitancy to perform cardioversion due to potential embolic complications. The study underscores the need for further research to refine cardioversion timing and anticoagulation strategies, while also emphasizing the importance of shared decision-making in evidence-based medicine.

**Case Scenario:**
A 35-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with palpitations. She states that she woke up this morning and went for a run and began feeling her heart race. She stopped and rested but her heart rate wouldn’t come down and it felt irregular. This all started about 2 hours ago. Her vitals are unremarkable except that her heart rate is 140 bpm and irregular.

**Point:**
What is the effectiveness and safety of the Ottawa Aggressive Protocol to perform rapid cardioversion and discharge of patients with these arrhythmias?

**Background:**
Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a prevalent dysrhythmia frequently encountered in emergency departments, characterized by symptoms such as increased heart rates, chest pain, and weakness. The clinical management of AF involves a longstanding debate between rate control and rhythm control strategies, with significant variability in practice. A study by Dr. Ian Stiell and colleagues in 2011 highlighted this variability in Canada, where rhythm control was chosen for 42-85% of patients, and electrical cardioversion was used in 7-69% of cases. In the United States, there is apprehension regarding cardioversion due to the risk of thromboembolism, leading to a preference for rate control and subsequent cardiology evaluation. Stiell's research aimed to demonstrate the effectiveness and safety of their management protocol amidst these differing practices.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The Ottawa Aggressive Protocol appears to be highly effective in converting patients with recent onset atrial fibrillation or flutter back to sinus rhythm. In this cohort of 660 patients, there were no thromboembolic events and the relapse rate was 8.6% at 7 days.

**Case Resolution:**
You discuss the options with your patient and she elects for chemical cardioversion. After the administration of procainamide, she is still in atrial fibrillation. She then elects for electrical cardioversion. You perform procedural sedation and convert the patient with 150J biphasic and she converts to sinus rhythm. 1 hour after the procedure, you discharge her home for follow-up with your local cardiologist.
