**Summary:**
A 54-year-old diabetic female presented to the ED with symptoms suggestive of retinal detachment, including floaters, flashing lights, and blurry vision. Ocular point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is highlighted as a valuable diagnostic tool for retinal detachment, offering high specificity but variable sensitivity, potentially due to operator training rather than technological limitations. The systematic review and meta-analysis discussed found POCUS to have high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.988) but noted moderate heterogeneity and potential biases such as partial verification and differential verification biases. The study emphasizes the need for improved training protocols to enhance sensitivity and suggests that while POCUS can confirm retinal detachment, caution is advised in ruling it out without further ophthalmologic consultation. The case resolution involved diagnosing a retinal detachment with a "macula on" status, prompting urgent ophthalmology referral to preserve vision.

**Case:**
A 54-year-old diabetic female presents to your emergency department (ED) complaining of floaters, flashing lights, and blurry vision. She has no pain and no history of trauma. She noticed that she couldn’t read her newspaper, like there was a wall of light between her left eye and the words on paper. She presents to the ED looking for answers.

The vision in her right eye is 20/40 and vision in the left eye is 20/50. The left eye field of vision is significant for floaters and decreased capacity to see medially. She has no afferent pupillary deficit and she has no obvious cranial nerve deficits. Her eye is not red, and her cornea is not hazy. The rest of her examination is unremarkable.

**Background:**
Ocular complaints constitute 3-4% of emergency department (ED) visits, with most being benign; however, 20% require further evaluation and referral to preserve vision, involving conditions like uveitis, macular degeneration, and retinal detachment. Retinal detachment is clinically significant due to potential interventions that can prevent or restore vision, necessitating referral to a retina specialist. Diagnosis of posterior chamber pathologies traditionally relies on direct and indirect ophthalmoscopy, but these skills are often lacking among non-ophthalmologists, leading to underutilization in EDs. The FOTO-ED study highlighted this gap, showing that ED physicians performed fundoscopy in only 14% of cases, missing diagnoses in all 33 patients examined. Limited ophthalmology coverage in EDs, especially in rural areas, exacerbates the issue, though tele-ophthalmology services are emerging. Direct ophthalmoscopy has poor diagnostic accuracy, and even indirect ophthalmoscopy requires skilled operators. Ocular ultrasound, introduced in the 1950s and gaining traction in emergency medicine over the past 17 years, offers a more accessible diagnostic tool for posterior chamber pathologies, with studies indicating high sensitivity and specificity. Despite requiring skill acquisition, ocular ultrasound is portable, allows bedside examination, and facilitates immediate feedback, making it a promising alternative to traditional ophthalmoscopy.

**Clinical Point:**
What are the testing characteristics of ocular point of care ultrasound when attempting to diagnose retinal detachment among a group of patients presenting with vision complaints?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Early on in your POCUS training if you identify a retinal detachment make the call. Be wary if you don’t see any pathology and make sure the patient has immediate consultation or immediate follow-up. As you progress in your POCUS training you may be more confident with cases that you can rule out.

**Case Resolution:**
You make the diagnosis of a retinal detachment, but you are concerned the macula remains intact (Mac On) because the tear does not reach the optic nerve. You consult ophthalmology who schedules the patient for a procedure to preserve vision.

**Clinical Application:**
Ocular POCUS is highly specific meaning it’s excellent if the pathology is readily visible. However, more research is needed to determine if its unacceptable sensitivity is due to lack of training or limitations of ultrasound technology.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
I typically use the “movie screen” analogy and say, imagine you’re at the theatre looking up at your favorite movie and part of the screen peels off. If it’s a large part of the screen then the movie is going to look all blurry and wavy. This is an emergency and I may have you see the eye surgeon right away. If I look into your eye and it seems like the screen is flat, then I’m going to ask you to see an eye doctor in the next two to three days because sometimes it appears like it’s glued tightly but a small corner of the screen has come undone. That small corner can get pretty big over time.

If you have no eye doctor or no good way of seeing an eye doctor in the next two to three days I may look with my ultrasound and, regardless of what I see, I will ask them to look into your eye so at least they start following your symptoms because preserving vision is an important piece of a happy life and would be worth the wait.
