**Summary:**
This document discusses the management of ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) in rural settings, emphasizing the critical time from first medical contact (FMC) to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The study highlights that rural patients with STEMI have a fourfold increase in in-hospital mortality if PCI is delayed beyond 90 minutes. The retrospective cohort study of 365 rural patients found that timely PCI within 90 minutes significantly reduces mortality, with a negative predictive value of 98.6% for in-hospital death. The study underscores the importance of minimizing prehospital and emergency department delays to improve outcomes, suggesting that rural healthcare systems need optimization to meet recommended time benchmarks or consider alternative therapies like thrombolysis when timely PCI is not feasible. The findings also point to potential confounders such as patient comorbidities and the need for adjustments for clustering effects in the analysis.

**Case:**
A 72-year-old man with a history of high blood pressure and diabetes calls emergency medical services (EMS) for chest pressure and dyspnea that started 1 hour ago. Upon EMS arrival, they find the patient is sweaty with normal vital signs. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrates ST elevations in leads II, III, and aVF with ST depressions in leads I and aVL and the team begins transport to the nearest percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) capable hospital.

**Background:**
ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) is a critical cardiac condition requiring prompt intervention to restore blood flow, typically through percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or systemic thrombolysis. Current guidelines emphasize a target time of 90 minutes or less from first medical contact (FMC) to PCI, with systemic thrombolysis recommended if this time exceeds 120 minutes. A study involving 101 STEMI patients from rural emergency departments revealed median times of 6 minutes for door-to-ECG, 8 minutes for door-to-physician, and 27 minutes for door-to-needle (DTN), with 58% receiving thrombolytics within 30 minutes. The study highlights the challenges faced by rural hospitals, which often lack PCI facilities, leading to extended transportation times and potential delays in treatment. Regional systems of care aim to expedite the recognition and reperfusion of STEMI patients, but the specific pre-PCI steps contributing to delays remain unclear.

**Clinical Point:**
Is there an association between in-hospital mortality and time between first medical contact and primary percutaneous coronary intervention in rural patients who present with a STEMI?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Higher in-hospital mortality for rural STEMI patients is associated with longer time from first medical contact to percutaneous coronary intervention.

**Case Resolution:**
EMS activates the local STEMI pathway immediately the patient is given aspirin and nitroglycerin and transported to the nearest PCI-capable hospital.

**Clinical Application:**
Decreasing unnecessary delays in the prehospital and emergency department settings are important in the care of patients with occlusive myocardial infarction. There is likely potential in rural settings to optimize care to meet recommended benchmarks or provide alternative therapy when necessary.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You are having a heart attack. We are bypassing the closest hospital and transporting you to the nearest hospital that will be able to relieve the blockage in your heart. Things will move very quickly to take the best care of you.
