**Summary:**
A 41-year-old man presents with acute low back pain without red flags, raising the Point of acupuncture's efficacy as an alternative to pharmacologic treatment. Low back pain is a common emergency department presentation, often managed with acetaminophen, NSAIDs, muscle relaxants, or opioids, though these have limited success and potential side effects. A study by Cohen et al. evaluated acupuncture versus pharmacotherapy for pain relief, finding no significant difference in pain reduction between groups, suggesting a strong placebo effect for acupuncture. The study's limitations include lack of blinding, absence of a sham control, and potential selection bias. The SGEM concludes there is no high-quality evidence supporting acupuncture's effectiveness for acute pain in the emergency setting, and the patient is advised on the limited benefits of acupuncture and the potential placebo effect, with a recommendation to use acetaminophen and remain active.

**Case:**
A 41-year-old man presents to the emergency department with an acute onset of back pain. He was putting some groceries into the car and felt something pull in his lower back. He has no *“red flags”* on your history and physical examination. He is worried about taking pain pills and wants to know if acupuncture would work?

**Background:**
Low back pain is a prevalent reason for emergency department visits in the United States, accounting for approximately 2.4% or 2.7 million visits annually, with most cases being benign but challenging to manage. Oligoanalgesia, or inadequate pain control, remains a significant issue. Various treatments, including acetaminophen, muscle relaxants, NSAIDs, and benzodiazepines, have shown limited success. Opioids, while effective for severe pain, pose risks of addiction and side effects, leading to guidelines recommending their use only for severe, disabling pain unresponsive to other treatments. In light of the opioid epidemic, alternative treatments like acupuncture have been considered. Acupuncture, a component of Traditional Chinese Medicine, involves inserting needles to restore the flow of chi/qi, though research has not provided high-quality evidence of its efficacy, with studies indicating a strong placebo effect.

**Clinical Point:**
Can acupuncture provide pain relief equivalent to pharmacologic treatment in the emergency department?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There is no high-quality evidence that acupuncture works for patients presenting to the emergency department with back pain, ankle sprains, or migraines.

**Case Resolution:**
You offer him acetaminophen to treat his back pain but set his expectations. He is encouraged to keep active and told the benefit observed with acupuncture is most likely a placebo effect. If he develops any red flag signs or symptoms, he should return to the emergency department.

**Clinical Application:**
There is still no high-quality evidence that acupuncture is effective for any medical condition.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
There are no great treatments for back pain. A short course of opioids can work, but can have serious problems (side effects and risk of addiction).
