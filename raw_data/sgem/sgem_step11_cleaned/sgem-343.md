**Summary:**
The study by McLean et al. investigates the prevalence of interphysician weight bias, focusing on implicit, explicit, and professional biases among physicians in North America. Utilizing adapted survey instruments, including the Implicit Association Test and the Anti-fat Attitudes Questionnaire, the study found a significant presence of implicit weight bias (IWB) among physicians, with 87% of participants demonstrating bias against colleagues with obesity. Explicit weight bias (EWB) and professional weight bias (PWB) were also present, with positive correlations between these biases and factors such as male gender and increased age. The study highlights the need for implicit bias training to foster a more inclusive medical workplace, as these biases can impact professional relationships and patient care. The findings underscore the importance of addressing weight bias in the medical community to improve team dynamics and healthcare outcomes.

**Case:**
You are working in the emergency department (ED) with the new resident, one of whom is overweight. You overhear his colleagues wonder where he went, chuckling, and one of them comments that *“he probably went for second breakfast.”* Realizing that these residents are making fun of their colleague’s weight, you decide to address the issue.

**Background:**
Bias in medicine refers to systematic deviations from the truth that can affect research outcomes and clinical practice. Common biases include selection bias, where non-random subject selection skews study results, and publication bias, where studies with positive outcomes are more likely to be published. Cognitive biases such as anchoring bias, base-rate neglect, and hindsight bias also impact medical decision-making. Biases related to age, gender, socioeconomic status, and race are prevalent, with the gender pay gap being a notable example, as highlighted by a study showing female academic emergency medicine physicians earn significantly less than their male counterparts. The September 2021 issue of Academic Emergency Medicine focuses on biases in emergency medicine, including racial, ethnic, and gender disparities, and highlights the underexplored area of weight bias, which affects interactions between physicians and patients, as well as among physicians themselves.

**Clinical Point:**

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Forms of intraphysician bias exist, including implicit, explicit, and professional bias. It is important to recognize these biases to understand how to overcome them and keep negative impacts on patient care and physician to physician relationships from occurring.

**Case Resolution:**
You approach the group of residents and explain that you overheard their comments. You explain that making fun of or otherwise shaming their colleague threatens their relationships with him and could negatively impact their ability to work as a team to care effectively for patients.
