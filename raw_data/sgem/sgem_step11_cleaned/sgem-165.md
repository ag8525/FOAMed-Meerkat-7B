**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of procedural sedation in pediatric emergency department patients, particularly focusing on the relevance of nil per os (NPO) status. It highlights a study by Beach et al., which analyzed 139,142 sedation encounters and found no statistical association between NPO status and major complications, including aspiration. The study suggests that pre-procedural fasting does not reduce the risk of adverse events, supporting the ACEP's 2013 guidelines that do not recommend delaying sedation based on fasting time. The document emphasizes the importance of clinical judgment and informed discussions with families, as well as the potential limitations of applying these findings universally, particularly in settings without high-performance sedation teams. The case resolution demonstrates the practical application of these findings, where a decision was made to proceed with sedation without delay, resulting in a successful outcome.

**Case:**
A 5-year-old girl was bit in the face by a neighbor’s dog. She has a complex lower lip laceration that extends beyond the vermillion border with a flap deformity. You believe it will require repair by an oral and maxillofacial surgeon in your emergency department and that the patient will require procedural sedation. The patient had been eating a turkey sandwich one hour ago and you consider how long to leave her NPO prior to the sedation.

**Background:**
Procedural sedation is a common practice in emergency departments for pediatric cases such as laceration repair, incision and drainage, closed reduction of fractures, and radiographic studies. The American Society of Anesthesiologists' 2011 guidelines suggest fasting times before elective procedures requiring sedation, but these do not necessarily apply to emergency care. The ACEP Clinical Policy from 2013 provides a Level B recommendation against delaying procedural sedation in emergency settings based on fasting time, as pre-procedural fasting has not been shown to reduce the risk of emesis or aspiration during procedural sedation and analgesia.

**Clinical Point:**
Do we need to delay procedural sedation of pediatric emergency department patients based on their NPO status?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It is reasonable not to delay procedural sedation in a pediatric emergency department patients based on their NPO status.

**Case Resolution:**
You have an informed discussion with the family regarding their daughter’s injury and the risks of sedation. You make a joint decision to proceed with the sedation and have the oral and maxillofacial surgeon perform the laceration repair. The child undergoes the sedation without complications and upon awakening, asks to go home.

**Clinical Application:**
It will always be difficult to risk stratify rare conditions, but this paper offers support to those in favor of more rapid initiation of procedural sedation, regardless of NPO status. I am a little more tacit with adoption of this paper into practice. I support the findings of the paper, but the study was limited for some of the reasons we discussed. The paper does support ACEP’s clinical guidelines and adds to the available evidence to support procedural sedation independent of NPO status. However, as always, you must use your judgement.

**What do I tell the patient?**
Complications from procedural sedation are uncommon but something we constantly prepare for. A common thought in medicine is that there is increased risk of one of these complications if sedation is started after recent food or drink ingestion. A study of a large group of children recently demonstrated no increased risk based on food or drink ingestion. The American College of Emergency Physician says emergency doctors should not delay procedures based on fasting time. With your permission, we’d like to begin the sedation now to repair your daughter’s injury.
