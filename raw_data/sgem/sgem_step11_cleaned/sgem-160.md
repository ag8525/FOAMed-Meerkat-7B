**Summary:**
In this clinical scenario, a 53-year-old woman with a history of hypertension presents with chest pain, and initial evaluations including ECG and high sensitivity troponin T (hs-cTnT) are normal. The document discusses the utility of hs-cTnT in ruling out acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in patients without ECG ischemia, highlighting a study where a single hs-cTnT below the detection limit, combined with a non-ischemic ECG, showed a sensitivity of 99.1% and a negative predictive value of 99.6% for AMI. The study suggests that this approach could safely exclude AMI in a significant portion of patients, potentially reducing unnecessary admissions. However, the document emphasizes the importance of clinical judgment and patient preferences in decision-making, acknowledging a small residual risk of missed AMI and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) within 30 days. The case resolution involves offering the patient the option of further testing or discharge, with informed consent about the low but non-zero risk of adverse outcomes.

**Case:**
A 53-year-old woman presents to your emergency department a little less than two hours after her anterior, squeezing chest pain hits its peak. She has a history of hypertension, but is otherwise healthy. Thanks to a medical directive, when you sit down with the patient, she already has a normal ECG and a high sensitivity troponin T (hs-cTnT) test that has come back below the limit of detection. She is feeling a lot better and would like to go home.

**Background:**
Chest pain in the emergency department necessitates the exclusion of life-threatening conditions, with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) being a primary concern. Despite the lack of a universally accepted diagnostic algorithm for ACS, serial ECGs and various biomarkers are commonly employed, with high sensitivity troponin (hs-cTnT) tests gaining prominence. A single hs-cTnT test below the detection threshold has shown a sensitivity of 97.4% and a specificity of 42.4% for ruling out myocardial infarction. When combined with a non-ischemic ECG, the sensitivity increases to 98.3% with a negative predictive value of 99.8%, as demonstrated in a large retrospective study. However, many studies on this approach are limited by their small size, single-center focus, and retrospective nature.

**Clinical Point:**
Can high sensitivity troponin T (hs-cTnT) levels below the limit of detection on arrival in the emergency department be used to safely exclude acute myocardial infarction in patients with no ECG ischemia?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
hs-cTnT is a new reality for some emergency physicians. We need to know how to use this test correctly to safely evaluate patients presenting with symptoms of ACS.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is offered serial troponins and ECGs but decides to go home knowing there is no *“zero risk”.*

**Clinical Application**: Use of hs-cTnT will all depend on your comfort level, patients’ preferences, and your current practice environment.

**What do I tell my patient?**
We cannot find any evidence of a heart attack. Your ECG is normal. Your blood work using a new highly sensitive test is also normal. However, this does not mean you did not have a heart attack. There is a very small risk (less than 1%) that it could be missed on the ECG and blood test. It also does not mean you do not have heart disease. It is possible you could have a major adverse event (including death) in the next 30 days. That possibility is also very low but not zero percent. With your test results, less than 1/500 people die in the next 30 days, but 1% of people have what we call a major event – mostly meaning they have to come back to the hospital to have a heart intervention like a stent or a surgery. Would you like to stay for further testing in 1-3 hours or go home now?
