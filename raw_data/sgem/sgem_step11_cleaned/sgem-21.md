**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of a 72-year-old man who experienced a witnessed cardiac arrest and was resuscitated from ventricular fibrillation. The focus is on the use of therapeutic hypothermia post-cardiac arrest, specifically whether pre-hospital cooling by paramedics improves outcomes. Two key studies from 2002 demonstrated neuroprotective benefits of hypothermia, with a calculated NNT of 6 for improved outcomes. However, a 2010 randomized controlled trial found no significant benefit of pre-hospital cooling with ice-cold fluids compared to in-hospital cooling, with a risk ratio of 0.90 (P=0.43). The study was underpowered due to early termination, and while it did not support pre-hospital cooling, it highlighted the need for further research, particularly in settings with longer transport times. The case concludes with the patient being cooled upon hospital arrival and achieving a favorable neurological outcome.

**Case:**
A 72-year-old man has a witnessed cardiac arrest while watching his grandson’s hockey game. Bystander CPR is started, and he is shocked out of ventricular fibrillation using an automatic external defibrillator (AED). EMS arrives and finds the patient with stable vital signs but unconscious. The paramedic calls the base hospital and asks if they should start cooling en route.

**Background:**
Mild therapeutic hypothermia is a neuroprotective intervention following cardiac arrest resuscitation, as evidenced by two randomized control trials published in 2002. The first trial, involving 273 participants, demonstrated a significant improvement in neurological outcomes at six months when using a cooled air mattress, with 55% of patients showing good outcomes compared to 39% in the control group. A smaller Australian study with 77 participants reported a good neurologic outcome at hospital discharge in 49% of patients versus 26% in the control group. Dr. David Newman calculated a number needed to treat (NNT) of 6 for this intervention. The Cochrane Collaboration's 2012 review supports the use of conventional cooling methods to induce mild therapeutic hypothermia, affirming its efficacy in improving survival and neurological outcomes post-cardiac arrest, aligning with the International Resuscitation Guidelines.

**Point:**
Does pre-hospital therapeutic hypothermia improve patient outcomes after successful resuscitation?

**Bottom Line:**
Scoop and run, no cooling required in the field.

**Case Conclusion:**
The patient was not cooled in the field but on arrival. He was admitted and one week later he was the 1 in 6 person to walk out of the ICU neurologically intact.
