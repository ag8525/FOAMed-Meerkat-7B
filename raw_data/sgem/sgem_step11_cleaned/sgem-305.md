**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) in patients with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), focusing on the CRASH-3 trial and a subsequent study by Rowell et al. The CRASH-3 trial found no significant mortality benefit of TXA in TBI patients, although a subgroup with GCS 9 to 15 and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) showed potential benefit. Rowell et al.'s study evaluated pre-hospital TXA administration and found no significant improvement in 6-month neurologic outcomes compared to placebo. The study highlighted issues such as survival bias, limitations of the Glasgow Coma Scale, and the small number of patients with ICH, which may have diluted potential benefits. The conclusion is that routine pre-hospital TXA use in isolated TBI is not recommended due to insufficient evidence of efficacy.

**Case:**
A 42-year-old helmeted bicycle rider is involved in an accident where he hits his head on the ground. At the time of emergency medical services (EMS) arrival, the patient is alert but seems a bit confused. The accident was within one hour of injury and his Glasgow Coma Scale (GSC) score was 12. Vital signs show a slight tachycardia but otherwise normal. Pupils were both equal and reactive and he doesn’t appear to have any other traumatic injuries, or focal neurologic deficits. Other injuries appear minimal with some abrasions from the fall.

**Background:**
The CRASH-2 trial demonstrated a 1.5% mortality benefit for patients with traumatic hemorrhage treated with tranexamic acid (TXA), establishing it as a standard treatment in many settings. However, the trial excluded patients with significant head injuries, leaving the effect of TXA on this group unclear. The subsequent CRASH-3 trial, published in 2019, investigated TXA use in patients with traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) characterized by a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 12 or lower or any intracranial bleed on CT scan, treated within 3 hours of injury. The trial found no statistical superiority of TXA over placebo for reducing head injury-related deaths within 28 days. Nonetheless, a subgroup analysis indicated a mortality benefit for patients with a GCS score of 9 to 15 and intracranial hemorrhage on baseline CT, though caution is advised in interpreting these findings.

**Clinical Point:**
Does pre-hospital administration of TXA to patients with moderate or severe traumatic brain injury improve neurologic outcomes at 6 months?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The routine use of TXA in the pre-hospital setting for patients with isolated moderate or severe TBI cannot be recommended at this time.

**Case Resolution:**
After evaluation of the patient, it is decided to not administer TXA prehospitally as the patient has an isolated TBI, no other traumatic injuries that would benefit from TXA, and the lack of robust evidence to support its use in TBI in the out-of-hospital setting.

**Clinical Application:**
This trial of TXA vs placebo given within 2 hours of injury, in the out-of-hospital setting, in patients with moderate or severe traumatic brain injury (GCS ≤ 12) combined with the results of the CRASH-3 and the TICH-2 trial, we have no high-quality evidence to support the routine use of out-of-hospital TXA in patients suspected of having ICH.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have had a head injury. We would like to admit you to the hospital for observation, neurologic checks, and ensure there are no changes over the next 24 to 48 hours. The CT scan we performed did not show any bleeding in the brain.
