**Summary:**
In the case of an 18-month-old male with a suspected skull fracture following a fall, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is highlighted as a valuable tool in the emergency department to assess for skull fractures, especially in pediatric patients where minimizing radiation exposure is crucial. The PECARN pediatric CT decision rules guide the use of CT scans, but POCUS offers a radiation-free alternative with high specificity for detecting skull fractures, as demonstrated by a study showing sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 97%. While POCUS can effectively rule-in or rule-out fractures, it should be used in conjunction with clinical decision rules like PECARN, as serious intracranial injuries can occur without fractures. The study emphasizes the importance of ultrasound training for clinicians and suggests that while POCUS is a useful adjunct, it should not replace CT scans when intracranial injury is suspected. The case resolution involved using POCUS to rule out a fracture, allowing for safe observation and discharge without CT, aligning with the goal of reducing unnecessary radiation exposure in children.

**Case:**
An 18-month-old male presents to your small emergency department having a witnessed fall off a couch and hit his head on a hardwood floor. He threw up once and cried immediately at the scene. There was no loss of consciousness. The parents are concerned about a serious head injury, particularly with the large hematoma, but understandably are not thrilled with the idea of the radiation exposure of a CT, need for sedation, and transport to another centre for the CT scan. In the emergency department, he is alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15 and has no neurological deficits. Remembering your PECARN pediatric CT decision rules, you note that for children less than two years old a CT of the head is recommended if Glasgow Coma Scale<15, altered mental status, or palpable skull fracture. You would also consider a CT if non-frontal scalp hematoma, loss of consciousness greater than five seconds, severe mechanism, or abnormal activity. This child looks great except for a large frontal hematoma and you want to be able to send him home but you know that the presence of a skull fracture increases the risk of an associated intracranial injury. Honesty being the best policy, you acknowledge your lack of confidence in your ability to palpate a skull fracture and that forehead is kind of swollen.

**Background:**
Point of care ultrasound (POCUS) is increasingly utilized for fracture detection due to its rapid execution, suitability for unstable patients, and repeatability, particularly in fracture reduction scenarios. Clinicians have demonstrated good accuracy with POCUS for various fractures, and it is comparable or superior to traditional imaging methods like plain films and bone scans for certain flat bone fractures, such as the sternum. In pediatric head injuries, reducing ionizing radiation exposure is crucial, and while decision rules like PECARN help minimize CT scans, skull fractures significantly elevate the risk of intracranial injury. Skull X-rays are often difficult to interpret and miss fractures, and clinical exams are not reliably accurate, with significant fracture detection even in low-risk groups. POCUS, which avoids ionizing radiation and is child-friendly, has shown promise in pediatric skull fracture detection, with studies reporting sensitivities between 82% and 100% and specificities from 94% to 100%.

**Clinical Point:**
Is ultrasound in the emergency department useful to rule-in or rule-out skull fractures in children?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Ultrasound is a useful adjunct for detecting skull fractures and further risk stratifying minor head injuries when used along with a clinical decision rule like PECARN. However, serious intracranial injuries can occur without fracture and the sensitivity of ultrasound for fracture is not yet sufficient to use it as the sole method for detecting injury and making discharge decisions.

**Case Resolution:**
You could order a skull Xray but you know they are hard to interpret, involve some radiation, will miss a significant number of fractures, and don’t provide any information about intracranial injury. Instead, you decide to use your department’s portable ultrasound machine to look for a fracture and combine this with your clinical decision rules. With Mom holding her son in her lap, you gently scan over and around the region of the frontal hematoma. There is no fracture visible so, along with PECARN rule support, you are happy to observe the child for a few hours in the ED and send him home with clear discharge instructions for the parents. They are reassured by your examination, seeing their son’s intact skull on the ultrasound and are content to avoid doing a CT unless his clinical picture changes later.

**Clinical Application:**
Point-of-care ultrasound, in the hands of competent physicians, appears to be a viable option to rule-in and rule-out skull fractures in children. One concern, from a clinical standpoint, is that often children with enough findings to merit concern about a skull fracture, have enough clinical findings to warrant a CT scan to rule-out intra-cranial pathology. Also, the incidence of pathology below a skull fracture in children is high, so finding one on ultrasound may merit further investigation with CT scanning. Judicious use of radiation is encouraged, as there is evolving evidence of the long-term risks to mortality and development for children exposed to ionizing radiation.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We can use an ultrasound device to check and see if your son has a skull fracture. This can help me decide if he is at risk of having a more serious injury and needs to get a CT scan.
