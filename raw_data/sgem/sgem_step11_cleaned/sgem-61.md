**Summary:**
In the management of acute upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, a restrictive transfusion strategy (initiating transfusion at hemoglobin levels <7g/dl) is favored over a liberal strategy (<9g/dl) due to improved outcomes, including reduced mortality and re-bleeding rates. The study by Villanueva et al. demonstrated that a restrictive approach significantly decreased 45-day mortality (5% vs. 9%) and further bleeding (10% vs. 16%) compared to a liberal strategy, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 25 and 17, respectively. Despite potential biases due to exclusion criteria and lack of blinding, the findings align with previous studies suggesting that conservative transfusion is beneficial, particularly in non-massive bleeds. Clinically, this supports a more conservative transfusion approach in patients with acute upper GI bleeds, reserving transfusions for those with hemoglobin levels below 7g/dl, while ensuring early endoscopic evaluation and appropriate medical management.

**Case Scenario:**
A 62-year-old man arrives with hematemesis. BP 112/72, HR 84 and Hgb is 82 (8.2g/dl).

**Point:**
Which is better, a conservative or liberal transfusion strategy for upper GI bleeds?

**Background:**
The medical condition discussed is hypovolemic anemia, a common emergency department presentation characterized by high morbidity and mortality. Transfusions are considered lifesaving in cases of massive bleeding; however, their use in less severe cases is controversial. Observational studies and small controlled trials indicate that transfusions may be detrimental in patients with hypovolemic anemia, particularly if the bleeding source is related to portal hypertension, as this can lead to a rebound increase in portal pressure and a heightened risk of re-bleeding. The TRICC trial, a multicenter, randomized, controlled clinical study, provides significant insights into transfusion requirements in critical care settings.

**Case Resolution:**
You group and cross him for 2 units of blood, hang one unit of blood in the emergency department, give pantoprazole 40mg IV and consult GI service.

**Clinical Application:**
I will probably be less likely to provide blood to patients with non-massive, acute upper GI bleeds with initial hemoglobins of >7g/dl.

**What do I tell my patients?**
Sometimes giving blood can cause harm and not be helpful. We are going to see what your hemoglobin is before starting a transfusion. If it is really low you will need some blood but if it is not too low we will discuss the case with the admitting specialist to make sure we are giving you the best care.
