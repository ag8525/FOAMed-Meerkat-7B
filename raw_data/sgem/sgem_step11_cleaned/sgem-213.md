**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) in children, focusing on the efficacy and safety of the upside-down position as a modified Valsalva maneuver. SVT is a common arrhythmia in children, and vagal maneuvers are the first-line non-invasive treatment. The study compared the upside-down position to the standard Valsalva maneuver, finding no statistically significant difference in cardioversion rates, although the upside-down position showed a trend towards greater effectiveness. The study was small, with 24 participants, and lacked sufficient power to assess safety, as no adverse events were reported. The authors' conclusion that the upside-down maneuver is safe and effective is disputed due to the study's limitations, including selection bias and inadequate monitoring for adverse events. The SGEM bottom line suggests that further research is needed before recommending the upside-down maneuver as a standard treatment for SVT in children.

**Case**
: A seven-year-old girl presents to your emergency department complaining of palpitations. On exam, she appears anxious and begs you not to give "that drug that makes my heart stop like that last doctor did." You know vagal maneuvers are first-line, but there’s variation in techniques. As the patient already tried breathing out of her clenched nose, you wonder if there is another safe method you can try prior to medications.

**Background:**
Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is the most common arrhythmia in children, with a prevalence ranging from 1 in 250 to 1 in 1,000. It often presents early in life, with 40% of cases occurring in the first month and over 50% within the first year. Vagal maneuvers are considered the first-line treatment due to their non-invasive nature and potential for rapid resolution of SVT episodes. Common techniques include carotid sinus massage, the mammalian dive reflex, and the Valsalva maneuver.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The upside-down maneuver exists, but further research is needed to determine if it has efficacy in children with SVT.

**Case Resolution:**
You attempt a standard vagal maneuver with the patient, which fails. The patient then undergoes chemical cardioversion with adenosine successfully.

**Clinical Application**: There is no clinical application for this upside-down modified Valsalva maneuver unless future research demonstrates its safety and efficacy.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You inform the family that the upside-down technique is still under investigation and has not been demonstrated to be better than usual treatment. However, they can always discuss the modified Valsalva maneuver with their cardiologist at their next follow-up.
