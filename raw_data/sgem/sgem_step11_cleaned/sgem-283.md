**Summary:**
The document discusses the clinical evaluation and management of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) in patients presenting with acute headaches in the emergency department, focusing on the Ottawa SAH Rule and the 6-hour CT Rule. The Ottawa SAH Rule, while highly sensitive, has low specificity, suggesting it may lead to increased downstream testing if applied broadly. The 6-hour CT Rule, with a sensitivity of 95%, is not infallible but can reduce unnecessary lumbar punctures when a CT scan is performed within six hours of symptom onset. The study highlights the importance of shared decision-making and the need for further validation of these rules in diverse clinical settings. The case resolution emphasizes the use of both CT and lumbar puncture to rule out SAH, underscoring the critical role of these diagnostic tools in managing high-risk headache presentations.

**Case:**
A 48-year-old male presents to your emergency department with a sudden onset headache, which started about one-hour prior to arrival. The headache is severe in quality and the patient does not have a history of similar headaches in the past. It is associated with nausea, vomiting, and photophobia.

**Background:**
Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a critical cause of headaches that can present in the emergency department, necessitating early diagnosis to prevent life-threatening complications. The Ottawa SAH Rule is a clinical decision tool designed to aid in diagnosing SAH, though it requires further external validation and impact analysis before widespread adoption. A significant diagnostic controversy involves whether a lumbar puncture is necessary after a negative CT scan performed within six hours of symptom onset. The clinical significance of SAH lies in its potential for severe morbidity and mortality if not promptly identified and managed.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the clinical impact of the Ottawa SAH Rule and the 6-hour CT Rule compared to standard care when implemented in six emergency departments across Canada?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The Ottawa SAH Rule is highly sensitive but has a very poor specificity. It is unclear how it performs against unstructured clinical judgment or in non-urban tertiary care teaching hospitals. While the 6-hr CT Rule has a high sensitivity, it is not 100%.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient underwent a non-contrast head CT which was negative for a SAH. The patient presented within 6-hrs of symptom onset and in this case, the non-contrast CT is highly sensitive but not perfect for ruling out SAH.
A shared decision-making discussion was had with the patient and his wife and it was determined given the high-risk nature of his symptoms he would undergo an LP. The LP was negative for red blood cells or xanthochromia, ruling out a SAH as the cause of his headache.

**Clinical Application:**
The Ottawa SAH Rule and 6-hr CT Rule can be incorporated into the current diagnostic pathways for the workup of SAH as long as their respective weaknesses are well understood and taken into account when utilizing them.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We were concerned your headache could have been from a bleed in your brain. This is called a subarachnoid hemorrhage. It can be due to an aneurysm from a weakness in an artery. The scan of your head and the spinal tap showed no evidence of a bleed. The risk that your headache is due to an aneurysm in your brain is essentially zero. We will make sure you have adequate pain control and then send you home to follow up with your primary care physician.
