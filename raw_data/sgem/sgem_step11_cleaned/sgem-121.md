**Summary:**
A 24-year-old male experienced a primary anterior shoulder dislocation, a common injury in young active men, particularly during activities with a wide range of motion. The traditional management involves immobilization in internal rotation, but recent studies, such as those by Itoi et al., suggest that external rotation may reduce recurrence rates, especially in patients under 30. However, conflicting evidence exists, with studies like Liavaag et al. and Whelan et al. showing no significant benefit of external over internal rotation. A 2014 study by Heidari et al. indicated that immobilization in abduction and external rotation significantly reduced recurrence rates compared to internal rotation. Despite these findings, the optimal immobilization position remains uncertain, and shared decision-making with the patient is recommended, considering the potential benefits and practical challenges of each method. The patient was ultimately placed in an internal rotation sling with plans for follow-up and physiotherapy.

**Case:**
24 year-old man is goofing around on the Memorial Day long weekend at the beach. He falls and dislocates his shoulder for the first time. An examination shows it is an isolated injury and x-rays demonstrate an anterior dislocation of his shoulder without fracture. Procedural sedation is performed with no complications. Post procedure image shows a reduced shoulder joint. You are getting ready to immobilize him and wonder whether it would be best in external or internal rotation.

**Background:**
The shoulder joint, known for its extensive range of motion, is prone to various injuries, including dislocation, with anterior dislocations being the most common. Young active men are particularly susceptible to shoulder dislocations. Traditionally, treatment for primary anterior shoulder dislocation involves immobilization in a sling with the arm in internal rotation and adduction, although this method has a high recurrence rate, especially among younger individuals. A significant debate in the literature arose when studies by Itoi et al. suggested that immobilization in external rotation could reduce recurrence rates compared to the conventional internal rotation method, particularly benefiting patients aged 30 or younger. However, Liavaag et al. contradicted these findings, reporting no reduction in recurrence rates with external rotation immobilization. Subsequent studies in 2014 further explored this issue but reached differing conclusions, highlighting ongoing uncertainty in the optimal immobilization strategy post-dislocation.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the best position to immobilize someone after a primary shoulder dislocation?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We do not know what is the best position for primary anterior shoulder dislocations to be immobilized.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is placed in a standard internal rotation immobilization sling. He is provided with specific instruction to wear the sling for 3-4 weeks and then return for re-assessment. He will then be started on a course of physiotherapy to restore range of motion, strength, and function.

**Clinical Application:**
This clinical situation makes an excellent opportunity for shared decision-making. This means collaborating with the patient about the two reasonable options. Provide information about the traditional internal rotation vs. the external rotation immobilization. Reassure the patient there is no right or wrong Point and whatever they decide will be fine.

**What do I tell my patient?**
We have put your shoulder back in the joint. There is a high chance it can pop out again. You need to have it immobilized for the next month and then start physiotherapy. Traditionally we have put people in a sling. Some research suggests having your arm sticking out to the side could be better. Other researchers have said the opposite. The new way may turn out to be better but it is a big device and can be awkward. What do you want to do?
