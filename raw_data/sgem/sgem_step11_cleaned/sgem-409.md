**Summary:**
The systematic review and meta-analysis by Hanula et al. evaluated the efficacy of oseltamivir in preventing hospitalization among outpatients with influenza, concluding that oseltamivir does not significantly reduce hospitalization risk but is associated with increased gastrointestinal adverse events such as nausea and vomiting. Despite recommendations from WHO, CDC, and IDSA, the evidence does not support routine use of oseltamivir for this purpose, as the treatment effect was not clinically significant, and the studies included showed moderate-certainty evidence with potential biases. The analysis suggests that while oseltamivir may slightly shorten the duration of influenza symptoms, it does not justify its use to prevent hospitalization, especially given the associated side effects and lack of high-quality evidence supporting its efficacy in high-risk populations.

**Case:**
A 57-year-old woman with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type-2 diabetes mellitus presents to the emergency department (ED) with fever, cough, myalgias, headache, and congestion. It’s flu season and you’ve already seen 15 people with the same symptoms. Her vitals look good: Temperature 38.5C, heart rate 102 beats/minute, blood pressure 143/88, and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. She is breathing comfortably without any increased work of breathing. You send off COVID and flu swabs and the results come back as positive for influenza. She looks well enough to go home but asks if she should be prescribed oseltamivir (Tamiflu) because some of her friends have been given it recently by their doctors.

**Background:**
Oseltamivir, approved by the FDA in 1999, is an antiviral medication used to treat influenza, with initial trials funded by Roche. It has been associated with adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. The Cochrane collaboration's 2014 review indicated that oseltamivir reduces the time to symptom alleviation by approximately 17 hours in adults but is accompanied by side effects. Despite these concerns, organizations like WHO, IDSA, and CDC recommend its use, especially for patients at high risk of severe outcomes. A 2020 Lancet RCT involving over 3,000 patients showed that oseltamivir shortened recovery by about one day compared to controls, with increased nausea and vomiting. Subgroup analysis suggested greater benefits for older, sicker patients, though such findings are considered hypothesis-generating. An observational study in JAMA Pediatrics 2022 highlighted the lack of high-quality evidence for routine oseltamivir use in hospitalized children with suspected influenza.

**Clinical Point:**
Is oseltamivir effective in preventing hospitalization from influenza in adults and adolescent outpatients?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Same as it ever was…we cannot recommend the routine use of oseltamivir to reduce the risk of hospitalization amongst outpatients diagnosed with influenza.

**Case Resolution:**
You explain to your patient that the data tells us that she may have a slightly shorter course of influenza if she takes oseltamivir but that it won’t prevent the need for hospitalization. Additionally, it comes with an increased risk of nausea, vomiting, and neuropsychiatric effects.

Based on this, the patient doesn’t feel like it would be advisable to take the drug. She agrees to schedule close follow-up with her family physician and, in the meantime, order up some chicken soup.

**Clinical Application:**
Despite the recommendations from WHO, CDC, and IDSA, it seems the benefit of oseltamivir is minimal, and we don’t see a reason to make it a standard part of your treatment approach.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have the flu. While there is a medication that may shorten your illness by less than one day it will not prevent you from being hospitalized and it comes with some unpleasant side effects like nausea, vomiting, and will cost you about $20.
