**Summary:**
In this case, a 44-year-old male with suspected abdominal and pelvic trauma following a motor vehicle collision is treated with tranexamic acid (TXA) as per hospital protocol. TXA, a synthetic lysine derivative, stabilizes clots by inhibiting fibrinolysis, and while it has shown mixed results in various conditions, the CRASH-2 trial demonstrated a 1.5% absolute mortality reduction in trauma patients. However, a retrospective study by Erramouspe et al. at an American Level 1 Trauma Center found similar mortality rates but a higher incidence of thromboembolic events compared to CRASH-2, highlighting concerns about thromboembolism risk with TXA use. Despite these findings, TXA remains a part of the treatment protocol for trauma patients, with the understanding that further research is needed to better understand thromboembolic risks.

**Case:**
A 44-year-old male presents to your level 1 trauma center by EMS after a motor vehicle collision. He is hypotensive and tachycardic. You suspect abdomen and pelvic trauma and calculate his injury severity score (ISS) to be 22. Your hospital protocol is to give tranexamic acid (TXA) 1g IV over 10 minutes followed by a 1g infusion over eight hours. You wonder what his overall chance of dying or developing a thromboembolic event when treated with TXA.

**Background:**
Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a synthetic lysine derivative that inhibits fibrinolysis to stabilize clots and control bleeding. Its clinical significance varies across conditions; it is effective for epistaxis and shows potential for post-tonsillectomy bleeding and hemoptysis, but its benefits in postpartum hemorrhage, hemorrhagic strokes, and gastrointestinal bleeds remain uncertain. The CRASH-2 trial demonstrated a 1.5% absolute mortality reduction in adult trauma patients, with a number needed to treat of 67, while the CRASH-3 trial found no significant mortality benefit for isolated head trauma, though a secondary analysis suggested potential benefit. Both trials faced limitations in external validity due to the predominance of study sites in middle to low-income countries, which may affect the generalizability of results to higher-income settings.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the mortality and thromboembolic events in adult trauma patients receiving TXA at an American Level 1 Trauma Center?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The evidence supports the use of TXA in the treatment of adult patients with blunt trauma, but the increased risk of thromboembolism is concerning.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is intubated; his pelvis is placed in a binder and you start your hospital’s massive transfusion protocol. A FAST exam is positive, and the surgeons start debating whether to get more advanced imaging or take the patient directly to the operating room for an exploratory laparotomy. You step out of the room and make a mental note to look up the patient tomorrow on your next shift.

**Clinical Application:**
TXA has an absolute mortality benefit of 1.5% in CRASH-2. This new retrospective study will not change my practice but does increase our concern about thromboembolic events.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It looks like you have internal bleeding. We are going to give you blood products as well as a medicine called TXA. This can help stop the bleeding and improve your chance of survival. There is a low risk of increasing blood clotting. The surgeons will talk to you more about taking you to the operating room.
