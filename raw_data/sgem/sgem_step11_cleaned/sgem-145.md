**Summary:**
A 23-year-old woman presents with a corneal abrasion, a common emergency department condition characterized by significant pain due to the cornea's high innervation. The case explores the use of topical anesthetics like proparacaine and tetracaine for pain management, which, despite historical concerns about delayed healing and corneal toxicity, have shown promise in recent studies when used in dilute forms for short durations. A systematic review of six studies indicates effective pain control without adverse outcomes if used for 48 hours with physician follow-up. However, the safety of outpatient use remains inconclusive due to the small size of the studies and the need for larger trials. The current practice involves prescribing diluted anesthetic drops with strict follow-up to mitigate risks, challenging the traditional avoidance of these agents in outpatient settings.

**Case:**
A 23-year-old healthy woman presents with right eye pain. She states she felt a foreign body sensation yesterday and then overnight, it became painful. Her visual acuity is 20/20 bilaterally and she doesn’t wear corrective lenses. Instillation of tetracaine drops results in complete resolution of the pain. On your slit lamp examination, you see a small corneal abrasion outside of the visual axis with no evidence of ulceration and no foreign body. You update the patient’s tetanus, prescribe her an antibiotic drop and prepare to discharge her when she approaches you and states, *“the pain is starting to come back. Can I have that bottle of medicine you used before to take home with me?”*

**Background:**
Corneal abrasions, affecting approximately 10% of eye-related emergency department visits, are common and can cause significant pain due to the cornea's high innervation. Pain management is a primary goal in treating these injuries. Although topical ophthalmologic anesthetics, first used in 1818 with a cocaine derivative, effectively block nerve conduction, their use is limited due to potential complications. These include delayed healing from mitosis inhibition, decreased corneal sensation risking unnoticed ulcer progression, and direct corneal toxicity with prolonged use, leading to increased corneal thickness, opacification, stromal infiltration, and epithelial defects. Consequently, major Emergency Medicine textbooks, such as Rosen’s and Tintinalli’s, advise against using topical anesthetics for outpatient management of corneal abrasions, despite their efficacy in pain reduction.

**Clinical Point:**
Can emergency department patients with simple corneal abrasions be safely discharged home with a prescription for a topical anesthetic drop?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The best evidence that we currently have demonstrates that dilute topical anesthetic drops of either proparacaine or tetracaine are safe for use in ED patients with simple corneal abrasions to provide analgesia. The studies are small but the data contained in them is far superior to the case series published 50 years ago which led to the dogma that using them is dangerous.

**Case Resolution (Kara):**
You discuss the options with the patient for analgesia. She prefers to not take any opiate medications and opts for a short course of dilute topical tetracaine. You schedule her a follow-up appointment with an ophthalmologist in two days.

**What do I tell my patients (Kara):**
You have a corneal abrasion, which is a scratch on the surface of your eye. This scratch can be quite painful. There are various options available to help control your pain once you leave the Emergency Department, including topical eye drops and oral pain medications. The advantage of the eye drops is that their effects are limited to the eye, so they cause less body-wide side effects compared to the oral medications. While we don’t have robust evidence regarding their use and further research is needed, recent studies suggest that they are effective and probably safe, as long as you use them as prescribed and for a short amount of time. Furthermore, it is very important that you follow up with an ophthalmologist within two days for your eye to be re-checked.
