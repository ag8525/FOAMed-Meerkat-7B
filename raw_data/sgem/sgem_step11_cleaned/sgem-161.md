**Summary:**
In a prospective, multicenter cohort study, Racine et al. investigated the delayed complications and functional outcomes of patients with isolated sternal fractures discharged from the emergency department. The study found that 12.5% of these patients developed a delayed hemothorax within 14 days, although none required drainage, suggesting limited clinical significance. Patients with isolated sternal fractures experienced more significant physical disability at 30 and 90 days compared to those with other minor thoracic injuries. The study emphasizes the importance of adequate pain management and follow-up for these patients, as sternal fractures are associated with prolonged pain and potential functional impairment. Despite the small sample size and limitations in follow-up, the findings highlight the need for patient education on potential complications and the importance of returning to the emergency department if symptoms worsen.

**Case:**
A 49-year-old male presents to the emergency department after being in a motor vehicle collision. You diagnose him with an isolated sternal fracture on X-ray. Specifically, there are no rib fractures or lung abnormalities identified. He also has a normal ECG and troponin. As you are preparing to discharge him, he asks if there are any potential complications to expect from the injury, and how long he should expect to have pain?

**Background:**
Sternal fractures, typically resulting from significant blunt thoracic trauma, occur in 0.33% to 3.7% of trauma patients, with the higher incidence noted in motor vehicle accident admissions. The prognosis of sternal fractures is heavily influenced by the severity of accompanying injuries, complications, and pre-existing comorbidities. Isolated sternal fractures, however, have been shown to be manageable on an outpatient basis, allowing for safe discharge from the emergency department following appropriate evaluation, which includes cardiac biomarkers and ECG. The clinical approach to isolated sternal fractures has evolved, reducing the necessity for hospital admission. Nonetheless, delayed hemothorax remains a notable complication in minor thoracic injuries discharged from emergency care, posing a risk for poor functional outcomes.

**Clinical Point:**
What are the complications and outcomes of patients with isolated sternal fracture discharged from the emergency department compared to those with other minor thoracic injury?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Patients discharged home with isolated sternal fractures have a risk of delayed hemothorax. These are painful injuries, and patients should be provided with adequate analgesia and follow-up.

**Case Resolution:**
He is discharged from the emergency department with adequate pain control, warned about when to return, and set expectations on how long he might expect to have pain.

**Clinical Application:**
We should be sure to inform patients with sternal fractures of reasons to return to the emergency department and adequately manage pain for these patients.

**What do I tell my patient?**
About one in eight patients with a sternal fracture will develop bleeding in the chest in the next two weeks. If you develop increasing shortness of breath, chest pain, fever, or are otherwise concerned, please come back to the emergency department.

You can take acetaminophen and/or an NSAID for the pain. I will also give you a prescription for an opiate to be used only if needed. You can expect to have some pain and difficulty with physical function for weeks or even months.
