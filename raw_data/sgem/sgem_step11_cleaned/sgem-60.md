**Summary:**
A randomized, double-blind multicenter trial evaluated the efficacy of nitrous oxide (N2O) versus medical air for prehospital analgesia in adults with moderate traumatic acute pain. The study involved 60 adult patients and demonstrated that 67% of those receiving N2O reported significant pain relief (pain score of 3 or less) within 15 minutes, compared to 27% in the medical air group. The trial highlighted N2O's rapid onset and effectiveness, with minimal adverse events reported. However, the study focused on efficacy rather than safety, and its findings are not directly applicable to emergency department settings. Further research is needed to establish N2O's safety and potential as a standard analgesic option in emergency care.

**Case Scenario:**
You are working at "The Hut" (Ski Patrol Hut) and a 46-year-old snowboarder comes in after falling. He has an obvious deformity of his right wrist. He is neurovascularly intact but in moderate pain. It is a 45-minute transfer from the ski hill to the emergency department.

**Background:**
Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a prehospital analgesic characterized by its safety, non-invasive administration, rapid onset and offset, effectiveness, and reversibility, particularly noted in pediatric studies. Despite these favorable properties, there is a lack of high-quality research evaluating its use in adult patients within emergency department settings.

**Case Resolution:**
You recognize the patient is in pain. He is provided with 50% nitrous oxide, splinted, and transferred. Within 15 minutes his pain has decreased significantly. He arrives at the emergency department 45 minutes later feeling more comfortable and an x-ray confirms a distal radius fracture.

**Clinical Application:**
If future studies demonstrate safety and efficiency, N2O could be added to analgesic options in the prehospital setting.

**What do I tell my patients?**
We do not have access to nitrous oxide (laughing gas) in the adult ED, although many children’s hospitals are using it. Early research in adults indicates that nitrous oxide could be an effective and safe strategy to acutely reduce pain without an IV, but we need to study this medication a bit more. Your dentist’s chair is still the likeliest place you’ll experience laughing gas.
