**Summary:**
In the context of managing intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in patients on factor Xa inhibitors, the study by Connolly et al. evaluates the efficacy of andexanet alfa compared to usual care, primarily involving prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). The trial found that andexanet alfa was more effective in controlling hematoma expansion but was associated with a higher incidence of thrombotic events, including ischemic stroke. Despite reducing anti-factor Xa activity significantly, the use of andexanet alfa did not translate into improved patient-centered outcomes such as survival or reduced neurological disability. The study's limitations, including early termination and potential conflicts of interest, raise concerns about the robustness of the findings. Consequently, the routine use of andexanet alfa is not recommended over PCC due to its high cost, lack of significant patient-oriented benefits, and increased risk of thrombotic complications.

**Case:**
A 65-year-old man is brought into the emergency department (ED) by emergency medical services (EMS) after his family saw him slump over at the dinner table. He was confused, slurring his speech, and had trouble moving his right side. Initial evaluation by medics revealed right arm weakness, a right facial droop, and decreased responsiveness. When he arrives at your ED, the family tells you he was doing fine until dinner. A code stroke is activated, and a CT head shows a left basal ganglia hemorrhage with no vascular lesions on CT angiography. Vital signs show a blood pressure (BP) of 190/110, heart rate (HR) 117 and irregularly irregular, respiratory rate (RR) 18, SpO2 99% on room air. You ask the patient’s family about any other medical conditions, and they report he has high blood pressure and atrial fibrillation. He takes metoprolol and apixaban and last took his medications that morning about eight hours before. As you begin to lower his blood pressure, you start thinking about reversal agents for his anticoagulation.

**Background:**
Hemorrhagic stroke, specifically intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), requires careful management of airway, blood pressure, and coagulopathy. Airway management is crucial for patients with compromised neurological function. Blood pressure control is debated, with trials like INTERACT-2 and ATACH-2 showing no significant benefit from intensive lowering, while INTERACT-3 suggests improved outcomes with goal-directed care. Coagulopathy reversal is essential for patients on anticoagulants to prevent hemorrhage expansion, though evidence on its impact on mortality and neurological outcomes is mixed. Warfarin reversal can be achieved with recombinant factor VIIa, while direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) like dabigatran, apixaban, and rivaroxaban require specific strategies such as hemodialysis, activated charcoal, or prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). The 2022 AHA/ASA guidelines recommend rapid blood pressure lowering and specific reversal agents like 4-factor PCC for vitamin K antagonists and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors, though the latter's high cost and safety concerns limit its use. PCC remains a preferred option for DOAC reversal due to its effectiveness and cost-efficiency.

**Case Resolution:**
Your hospital does not have andexanet alfa on the formulary, so you administer the appropriate dose of 4-factor PCC. The patient was admitted to the neurointensive care unit, and his repeat CT scan was unchanged.

**Clinical Application:**
The potential benefit of this drug is a disease-oriented outcome (DOO) and not a patient-oriented outcome (POO). There was also potential harm with a statistically significant increase in thrombotic events. Add that to the price of this drug and it does not look like a superior option to PCC.
