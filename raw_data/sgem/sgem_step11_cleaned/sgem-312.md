**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of oseltamivir (Tamiflu) for treating influenza-like illness (ILI) in primary care, highlighting a randomized controlled trial that found oseltamivir reduced recovery time by about one day compared to usual care. However, the trial's open-label design and subjective primary outcome raise concerns about bias, suggesting the observed benefit may be due to a placebo effect. The study also reported increased nausea and vomiting in the oseltamivir group, consistent with previous findings of adverse effects. Despite some authors' conflicts of interest with Roche, the evidence does not support routine oseltamivir use for ILI, emphasizing the importance of flu vaccination instead. The summary advises against oseltamivir, recommending symptomatic treatment and flu vaccination as preventive measures.

**Case:**
A 45-year-old female presents to her primary care clinician complaining of fever, sore throat, and muscle aches. She did not get a flu shot this year. You diagnose her with an influenza-like illness (ILI). She wants to know if taking an anti-viral like oseltamivir (Tamiflu) will help?

**Background:**
Oseltamivir, a neuraminidase inhibitor, was initially approved for the treatment and prevention of influenza based on unpublished trials, leading to significant controversy regarding its efficacy and safety. Subsequent reviews, including a comprehensive Cochrane review, revealed that oseltamivir does not effectively prevent influenza complications or serve as a reliable prophylactic measure, and it presents more harms than previously reported, such as increased nausea, vomiting, neuropsychiatric events, and headaches. The drug's approval and widespread use were influenced by conflicts of interest, as studies with financial ties to the pharmaceutical industry often reported more favorable outcomes. The evidence indicates that oseltamivir only marginally reduces the duration of symptoms by less than a day and does not significantly affect hospital admission rates, highlighting the need for careful consideration of conflicts of interest in medical research.

**Clinical Point:**
Does oseltamivir improve time to recovery in patients presenting to their primary care clinician with an influenza-like illnesses?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We recommend getting a flu shot and not using oseltamivir.

**Case Resolution:**
You provide her with some general advice about symptomatic treatment of an ILI. She is encouraged to stay home, wash her hands often, and cover her mouth when she coughs. If her condition gets worse (e.g., difficulty breathing, develops a rash, has a seizure) or is otherwise worried she can contact the office or consider going to the emergency department.

**Clinical Application:**
We still do not have any high-quality evidence that supports the routine use of oseltamivir in the treatment of influenza or influenza-like illnesses.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Oseltamivir is unlikely to make you better quicker and can cause nausea, vomiting, and other side effects. I recommend against using it. Next year, think about getting the flu shot.
