**Summary:**
The AIRWAYS-2 trial compared the use of supraglottic airway (SGA) devices to endotracheal intubation (ETI) in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and found that SGA was non-inferior to ETI regarding neurological outcomes at 30 days. The study involved 9,296 patients and showed no significant difference in primary outcomes between the two groups, with a slightly higher initial ventilation success rate in the SGA group. Despite a lower first-pass success rate for ETI, the trial suggests that airway management strategy may not significantly impact patient outcomes compared to the critical importance of early defibrillation and high-quality CPR. The study highlights that while SGA devices are easier and faster to use, they do not necessarily improve patient outcomes, emphasizing the need for paramedics to focus on proven interventions like defibrillation and CPR.

**Case:**
EMS arrive to your emergency department with a 68-year-old man post cardiac arrest patient. They had a difficult time getting a definitive airway pre-hospital. It required multiple attempts which caused several prolonged interruptions in CPR. After the patient is stabilized the medic asks you how he can improve his airway management skills during a cardiac arrest as it was difficult to intubate during compressions. What should you tell him?

**Background:**
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical condition where the heart stops functioning outside of a hospital setting, necessitating immediate intervention to improve survival rates. High-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and early defibrillation are crucial for patient survival, while there is no evidence supporting the benefit of epinephrine, other advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) drugs, pre-hospital therapeutic hypothermia, or mechanical CPR in improving patient outcomes. Endotracheal intubation has traditionally been the standard airway management technique for OHCA, but its efficacy is now being questioned due to the technical skill and proficiency required, which may be challenging to achieve in pre-hospital environments.

**Clinical Point:**
Are superglottic airway devices non-inferior to endotracheal intubation in OHCA with regards to neurologic outcome?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
In adults with OHCA, key factors for survival with good neurological outcome are early defibrillation and high-quality CPR. Airway strategies do not seem to be as important.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient goes on to have a poor neurologic outcome like most patients with OHCA.

**Clinical Application:**
Using a supraglottic airway device is a reasonable management option in adult patients with OHCA. This could cognitively unload paramedics and allow them to concentrate on those things that have been demonstrated to improve patient-oriented outcomes like early defibrillation and high-quality CPR.

**What Do I Tell the Medic?**
You tell the medic that we do not have good evidence on what is the best strategy for airway management in these cases. Inserting a supraglottic airway is easier, faster and more successful. However, it does not appear to result in better patient outcomes. Don’t get distracted with the airway and focus on early defibrillation and high-quality CPR.
