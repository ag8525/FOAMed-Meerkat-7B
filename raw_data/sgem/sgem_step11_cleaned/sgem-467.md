**Summary:**
The document discusses the evaluation and management of pediatric cervical spine (c-spine) injuries following blunt trauma, emphasizing the use of the PECARN clinical decision rule (CDR) to guide imaging decisions. Pediatric c-spine injuries, though rare, require careful assessment due to their potential severity. The PECARN CDR, validated through a large multicenter study, stratifies risk into high, intermediate, and no risk categories, recommending CT scans for high-risk cases and clinical clearance for low-risk cases, thereby reducing unnecessary radiation exposure. The study demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, supporting its use in emergency settings to minimize unnecessary imaging while ensuring accurate diagnosis. In the case presented, the child was clinically cleared without imaging, illustrating the application of the PECARN tool in practice.

**Case:**
An 8-year-old girl is brought in by EMS after a car accident. She was seat belted, sitting in the backseat of the family’s car when they were hit from the side by another vehicle that ran a red light. The airbags deployed, and the car spun a few times. When EMS arrived on the scene, they found both parents unconscious and the girl appeared slightly dazed and confused.
EMS places her in a cervical collar and brings her to the emergency department (ED). On your examination, you see she is scared but answering questions appropriately. She does have some abrasions from her seatbelt and complains of pain around her ankle. The rest of her exam is unremarkable.
After your evaluation, you are informed that her grandmother has arrived to be with the girl as her other family members are being treated. She looks at the contraption on the girl’s neck and asks you, *“Is she okay? Is something wrong with her neck? Does she need an X-ray or CT scan?”*

**Background:**
Pediatric cervical spine injuries, accounting for 1-3% of blunt trauma cases, are rare but potentially severe, often resulting from motor vehicle accidents, falls, sports injuries, or physical abuse. The anatomical differences in children, such as larger head size, predispose them to upper cervical spine injuries. Diagnosing these injuries is challenging due to difficulties in obtaining accurate histories and cooperation from young patients. While CT and X-rays are commonly used for detection, their overuse poses a risk due to ionizing radiation exposure, particularly harmful to developing tissues. To address this, clinical decision rules (CDRs) have been developed to guide imaging decisions, minimizing unnecessary exposure. The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) has created a widely recognized CDR, based on multicenter studies, which identifies critical risk factors like altered mental status and focal neurological deficits. This rule has shown high sensitivity in detecting injuries while reducing unnecessary imaging, thus balancing the need for accurate diagnosis with the imperative to limit radiation exposure.

**Clinical Point:**
Can the new PECARN clinical prediction rule (tool) guide imaging decisions in detecting pediatric cervical spine injuries without missing significant cervical spine injuries (CSI)?​

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The new PECARN cervical spine prediction tool helps risk stratify children with C-spine injury and guide imaging.

**Case Resolution:**
After you assess the child, you do not note anything remarkable in her primary survey. Her secondary survey is only notable for a small abrasion on her forehead and shoulder. You ask someone to hold her head still as you prepare to examine her neck. You tell her very clearly, *“I am going to ask you if you are having any pain around the area that I am touching. Do not nod or shake your head. Just Point yes and no.”* After noting that she does not have any tenderness to palpation of her neck and testing the range of motion of her neck, you clinically clear her C-collar and avoid additional imaging.

**Clinical Application:**
The new PECARN CDR for cervical spine trauma in children can help guide imaging decisions. Remember, we refer to these as clinical decision “*tools*” rather than rules. Considering all three pillars of evidence-based medicine, the scientific literature, clinical judgement, and patient/family values and preferences.

**What Do I Tell the Patient’s Grandmother?**
I understand that you are worried about your granddaughter. We examined her closely. She is acting appropriately and answering questions. She is not having any pain when she moves her neck or when we touch her neck. We try to balance the possible harms and benefits when thinking about whether we need to do X-rays or CT scans on children because we want to minimize radiation exposure. Based on her reassuring exam, I have low suspicion that she has a serious injury to her neck.
