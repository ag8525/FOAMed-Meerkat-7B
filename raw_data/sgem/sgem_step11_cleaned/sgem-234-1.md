**Summary:**
In this clinical scenario, a 64-year-old woman with type-2 diabetes and a GFR of 50 required a CTPA to rule out pulmonary embolism, raising concerns about contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN). The document discusses the PRESERVE trial, which evaluated the efficacy of intravenous sodium bicarbonate, sodium chloride, and oral acetylcysteine in preventing acute kidney injury (AKI) and adverse outcomes in high-risk patients undergoing angiography. The trial concluded that there was no benefit of these interventions over placebo in preventing death, dialysis, or persistent kidney function decline at 90 days, nor in preventing contrast-associated AKI. The study's findings suggest that the risk of AKI from modern contrast agents is minimal, and the potential harm from delayed diagnosis outweighs the risk of CIN. Consequently, the patient underwent the CTPA without prophylactic measures, revealing no pulmonary embolism but a possible early pneumonia, underscoring the importance of proceeding with necessary diagnostic imaging despite renal concerns.

**Case:**
A 64-year-old woman with type-2 diabetes presents to the emergency department with chest pain and some shortness of breath. The acute coronary syndrome work-up is negative, but she is Well’s high and needs a CTPA to rule out a pulmonary embolism. Her GFR is 50, and you are wondering if the contrast needed for the CT will cause an acute kidney injury (AKI) and if so, can you do anything to mitigate causing an AKI?

**Background:**
Contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN) is a condition historically associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) following the administration of contrast material during CT scans, particularly when high osmolar contrast agents were used. The clinical significance of CIN has been debated, as early non-controlled studies suggested a link between contrast use and AKI. However, with the current use of iso-osmolar or low osmolar contrast agents, recent observational studies indicate that AKI related to contrast is not prevalent. The definition of CIN typically involves a rise in serum creatinine by 25% or an absolute increase of 0.3 to 0.5 mg/dL within three days post-contrast administration, though these are laboratory-based outcomes rather than direct patient-oriented outcomes such as mortality or the need for dialysis.

**Clinical Point:**
Do intravenous sodium bicarbonate or sodium chloride with oral acetylcysteine or placebo prevent acute kidney injury and major adverse outcomes in high-risk patients undergoing angiography?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The risk of AKI from CT contrast is not as great as it was thought to be, and it might not even exist. The risk of missed or delayed diagnosis likely outweighs any from the exposure in a patient who requires a contrast CT study.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient has the CTPA study done without receiving bicarb or NAC, and no pulmonary embolism was identified. There was a small suspected pneumonia but requires clinical correlation.

**Clinical Application:**
If a contrast-enhanced CT study is needed for patient management, you should get the scan even in high-risk patients.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
There is no sign of a heart attack or blood clot with the CT scan. However, we did pick up a possible early pneumonia on the CT scan. I have written a prescription for antibiotics. See your PCP next week and come back to the ED if you develop a rash, your shortness of breath gets worse, or you are worried.
