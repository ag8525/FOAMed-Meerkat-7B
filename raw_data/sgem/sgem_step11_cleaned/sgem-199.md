**Summary:**
In this clinical case, a 22-year-old male with poorly controlled epilepsy presented with convulsive status epilepticus, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation after multiple seizures. The management included administration of midazolam and fosphenytoin, with consideration of therapeutic hypothermia. However, evidence from the TTM trial and a study by Legriel et al. indicates that therapeutic hypothermia does not significantly improve neurological outcomes in convulsive status epilepticus compared to standard care. The study, involving 268 patients, showed no significant difference in the primary outcome of a good neurological outcome at 90 days or in secondary outcomes such as mortality and progression to refractory status epilepticus. The findings suggest that therapeutic hypothermia does not confer additional benefit in the ICU setting for these patients, aligning with the American Epilepsy Society's guidelines on acute management.

**Case:**
Johnny is a 22-year-old male patient who presents to the emergency department via EMS with a seizure. He is known to have poorly controlled epilepsy. It was a witnessed clonic-tonic seizure that stopped but he did not wake up. The paramedics report another seizure on route to the hospital. He arrives with normal vital signs, normal finger stick blood glucose and a Glasgow Coma Scale of 3. He then has another seizure in the department. You quickly assess him in the resuscitation room, the team attempts intravenous (IV) access and collects labs while you give 10mg of midazolam intramuscularly and the seizures seem to stop. His oxygen saturation begins to drop and you decide to intubate and mechanically ventilate. He is now stabilized, fosphenytoin is being infused, you are getting ready to ship him to the neuro-ICU and wonder if cooling him down would help.

**Background:**
Therapeutic hypothermia has not shown benefit in pre-hospital settings, and the TTM trial indicated no significant difference in outcomes between maintaining body temperatures at 33°C versus 36°C. Status epilepticus, previously defined as a seizure lasting 30 minutes or more, is now defined as a single seizure lasting over five minutes or two or more seizures within five minutes without recovery between them. The American Epilepsy Society recommends intramuscular midazolam for adults without IV access during acute management of convulsive status epilepticus, with no significant difference in effectiveness between intravenous lorazepam and diazepam. Although cooling the brain during seizures is hypothesized to be protective, there is insufficient evidence supporting therapeutic hypothermia as neuroprotective in ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, in-hospital cardiac arrest, or traumatic brain injury.

**Clinical Point:**
Does induced therapeutic hypothermia improve neurologic outcomes in patients with convulsive status epilepticus?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Therapeutic hypothermia does not appear to have benefit for adult patients admitted to the ICU with convulsive status epilepticus.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is intubated, sedated and has been stabilized. Your works is done and the patient is transferred to the Neuro-ICU for further management.

**Clinical Application:**
This is another study failing to demonstrate a patient important benefit with therapeutic hypothermia.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You can’t talk to these patients because they are intubated. I would however, tell the family that Johnny had a bad seizure and needed to be sedated and intubated. Cooling down the body has been tried to help protect the brain from more damage. Unfortunately, it has not shown to help. Johnny is going to be transferred to the Neuro-ICU where they will take good care of him.
