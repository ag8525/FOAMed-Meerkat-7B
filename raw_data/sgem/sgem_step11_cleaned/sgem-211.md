**Summary:**
In this clinical case, a 40-year-old male with a history of migraines seeks advice on preventing recurrence after successful acute treatment in the emergency department. The document discusses the prevalence and economic impact of migraines, highlighting various acute treatment options, including IV metoclopramide and dexamethasone, which has shown efficacy in preventing early recurrence. A study on acupuncture for migraine prophylaxis is reviewed, indicating a statistically significant but clinically questionable reduction in migraine frequency with true acupuncture compared to sham and no treatment. However, the study's validity is questioned due to potential selection bias, unblinded providers and patients, and the use of a strawman comparison. The conclusion is that acupuncture lacks evidence for preventing migraine recurrence in patients without aura, and dexamethasone is recommended for short-term prevention.

**Case:**
40-year-old male appears with what he describes as his typical migraine that has failed his usual home therapies. In the emergency department after six hours and multiple medications, the patient’s pain is finally under control. While being discharged he asks if there is anything you can offer to prevent headaches from coming back. He states: *“I have to fly to Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine next week on a sales call and don’t want to have the headache return while I am there.”*

**Background:**
Migraine is a prevalent neurological condition affecting over 10% of the population, with a higher incidence in women (18%) compared to men (6%), leading to substantial economic burdens due to direct medical costs estimated at $17 billion annually and indirect costs of $15 billion primarily from lost productivity. In emergency settings, treatment options for migraines are varied, indicating a lack of universally effective solutions. A quiet, dark environment for rest is often beneficial, while intravenous metoclopramide is a recommended pharmacological intervention. Droperidol, though effective, is less available, and opioids may be necessary for severe cases. Dexamethasone, administered parenterally at doses ≥15mg, has been shown in randomized trials to reduce early migraine recurrences with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 9 and no significant side effects. Acupuncture has been explored for migraine prevention, with a 2016 Cochrane review indicating a small but statistically significant effect over sham interventions, though the evidence quality was moderate with some heterogeneity.

**Clinical Point:**
Does treating patients who have migraines without aura with acupuncture prevent the re-occurrence of migraine headaches?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
This study does not provide any evidence of the efficacy of acupuncture to prevent the re-occurrence of migraine headache in patients without aura.

**Case Resolution:**
You offer him a dose of dexamethasone to prevent his headache from re-occurring while away in China.

**Clinical Application:**
Acupuncture for migraine prevention does not have a clinical application in the emergency department.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
I’m glad we were able to effectively treat your headache with medication. If you want to prevent the headache from re-occurring in the short term on your trip to China, I would recommend a dose of dexamethasone.
