**Summary:**
A 35-year-old male presents with symptoms suggestive of uncomplicated acute appendicitis, confirmed by an Alvarado Score of 7 and ultrasound findings. The traditional treatment for acute appendicitis has been appendectomy, with laparoscopic procedures showing high safety and low complication rates. Recent studies have explored the use of antibiotics as a non-operative treatment for uncomplicated cases, with mixed results. Varadhan et al. found antibiotics to be effective and safe, while Kirby et al. reported fewer major complications with appendectomy. The diagnostic uncertainty of appendicitis complicates the evaluation of antibiotic efficacy, as surgical confirmation via pathology is definitive. Shared decision-making is crucial, as both treatment options have potential risks and benefits. The clinician, favoring surgical intervention due to its lower complication rate, recommends a laparoscopic appendectomy to the patient.

**Case:**
A 35-year-old man presents to the emergency room with right lower quadrant pain for approximately 18 hours. You assess the patient and find that his Alvarado Score is 7. You then ask for an ultrasound, as his body mass index is in the normal range. The ultrasound shows that he has an enlarged appendix >6mm that is not compressible and there is no intraperitoneal fluid present. You make a presumptive diagnosis of uncomplicated acute appendicitis. You relay this finding to the patient and he turns to you and asks, *“Hey doc, I heard that you can treat appendicitis with antibiotics now. Is that true?”*

**Background:**
Acute appendicitis is a condition characterized by inflammation of the appendix, traditionally treated with an appendectomy since its description by Charles McBurney in 1889. Laparoscopic appendectomy has been shown to be a safe procedure, with a study by Omar et al. (2008) reporting no deaths among over 230,000 UK patients under 49 years old, and Kluiber et al. (1996) noting a 2-5% incidence of post-operative intra-abdominal wound infection. There is ongoing debate about the potential for treating uncomplicated acute appendicitis with antibiotics, drawing parallels to the successful antibiotic treatment of diverticulitis. However, recent meta-analyses have yielded conflicting conclusions on this approach, highlighting the complexities of evidence-based medicine.

**Clinical Point:**
Are antibiotics in non-complicated acute appendicitis an effective and safe alternative to appendectomy?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Because of the diagnostic uncertainty of appendicitis, non-operative treatment of appendicitis (NOTA) will always be a very difficult thing to study. You will never know for sure in the antibiotics arm whether you were actually treating appendicitis. You will only know this in the surgical arm in which there is a pathology diagnosis. As a general practitioner with enhanced surgical skills, I would be concerned about treating suspected appendicitis with antibiotics because should it fail, this could lead to increased morbidity.

**Case Resolution:**
You look skeptically at the patient. Being a person who routinely does surgery for acute appendicitis, your pre-conceived bias takes over and you say that yes, there have been some studies recently that have shown promise in this regard, but there also have been studies that demonstrate potential harm. Therefore, you stick with what you feel comfortable with and offer the patient a laparoscopic appendectomy as it has the lowest complication rate in regard to surgical management of acute appendicitis. In addition, it also has been shown to significantly reduce the negative appendectomy rate.

**What Do I Tell the Patient:**
There are some studies showing antibiotics can be effective in the treatment of acute uncomplicated appendicitis. However, it’s complicated, because there are other studies that show the opposite to be true. There are risks no matter what treatment you choose. I think the data still supports doing surgery right away but ultimately the decision is yours.
