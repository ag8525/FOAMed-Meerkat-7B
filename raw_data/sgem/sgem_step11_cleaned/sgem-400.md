**Summary:**
The document discusses the incidence and characteristics of sports-related sudden cardiac arrest (Sr-SCA) in female athletes, highlighting a significantly lower occurrence compared to male athletes. The study, based on data from three large European registries, found that Sr-SCA incidence was 0.19 per million women per year versus 2.63 per million men per year. Despite similar management and survival rates post-event, the underlying causes differ, with women more likely to experience Sr-SCA due to dysrhythmias, such as long QT syndrome and channelopathies, rather than structural heart disease or coronary artery disease, which are more common in men. The findings suggest that preparticipation screening strategies should consider these sex-based differences, focusing on ECG and family history for women rather than structural assessments like echocardiograms. The study emphasizes the importance of timely bystander CPR and AED use in improving survival outcomes.

**Background:**
Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a rare but critical event that can occur in athletes during physical activity, with a higher incidence reported in male athletes compared to females. This disparity may be attributed to males' participation in more physically demanding sports and a higher likelihood of genetic predispositions to heart conditions. Despite the lower incidence in female athletes, SCA remains a significant risk, necessitating cardiovascular screening for females engaged in high-intensity sports to detect potential underlying heart conditions. The increasing participation of females in sports highlights the need for more research to understand sex-based differences in SCA risk and improve prevention strategies.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Sr-SCA is less common in women and more likely related to dysrhythmia than structural abnormalities and CAD.

**Clinical Application:**
Differences in underlying mechanisms responsible for Sr-SCA in both men and women can be used to improve public health policy, in particular screening of athletes. And as evidenced from recent newsworthy events, timely bystander CPR and automated external defibrillator application works.

**What Do I Tell a Patient?**
If seeing a patient with sports-related syncope or near syncope, I would let them know I am concerned, look closely at their ECG and ask specific questions about their family history. Then I would tell them I am referring them to a cardiologist.
