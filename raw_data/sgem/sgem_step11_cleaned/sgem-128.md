**Summary:**
The document discusses the evaluation of a 53-year-old man with acute chest pain, focusing on the use of high-sensitivity troponin T assays for diagnosing acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in the emergency department. The study by Reichlin et al. explores a one-hour algorithm using high-sensitivity troponin T and ECG to rule in or rule out AMI, showing that 60% of patients could be ruled out and 16% ruled in for AMI with high sensitivity and specificity. However, the study faces limitations such as potential biases, imprecision of the assay, and risk of over-testing due to low specificity. The authors conclude that while the protocol accelerates management, external validation is needed before widespread adoption. The case resolution involved shared decision-making, with the patient staying for six hours of observation, resulting in negative troponin tests and no ischemic changes on ECG, allowing for safe discharge. The document highlights the potential and challenges of using high-sensitivity troponin assays in clinical practice.

**Case:**
A 53 year-old man presents with left sided chest pain that started 90 minutes ago. The pain radiates to his left arm and he has nausea without vomiting. The pain began while he was doing yard work and he has never had pain like this before. His initial ECG is unremarkable.

**Background:**
Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a critical condition characterized by the interruption of blood flow to the heart, with ST-elevated myocardial infarction (STEMI) representing about 5% of acute chest pain cases, making it relatively straightforward to diagnose and manage. The remaining 95% of chest pain cases require differentiation between AMI and other causes, where cardiac biomarkers are essential. Historically, serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), now known as aspartate aminotransferase (AST), was used, but since the late 1990s, the preferred marker has shifted from CK-MB to troponin. Current troponin assays, however, have a limitation in that they take 3-4 hours to rise, delaying the diagnosis of Non-STEMI and necessitating prolonged monitoring. High sensitivity troponin assays, now widely used in emergency departments, offer increased sensitivity but reduced specificity compared to traditional assays. These high sensitivity assays may facilitate the quicker and safer discharge of patients, potentially reducing emergency department overcrowding and patient anxiety.

**Clinical Point:**
Is it possible to rule in/rule out acute myocardial infarction in the emergency department with high sensitivity troponin T and ECG in one hour?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Due to aforementioned biases and other factors mentioned, a one-hour protocol utilizing high sensitivity troponin T cannot be recommended at this time. External validation of this protocol along with a more explicit discussion of how the diagnosis of AMI is arrived at might allow for a rapid rule out in the future.

**Case Resolution:**
Shared decision making was done with the patient. After discussion with the patient, he agreed to stay in the emergency department for six hours. His troponin at zero and six hours were negative, and serial ECGs showed no ischemic changes, and his pain had completely resolved. He was discharged home with close follow up with his primary care physician and strict return precautions.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
There is a new blood test that checks for very early damage to your heart. If you are having a heart attack, this test often picks up early signs of heart injury. However, the test is so sensitive that it may suggest you are having a heart attack even if you aren’t having a heart attack. In order to be as sure as we can be, we will need to get multiple heart tracings and do more than one blood test.
