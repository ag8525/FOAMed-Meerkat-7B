**Summary:**
Transient global amnesia (TGA) is an acute neurological disorder characterized by sudden onset anterograde memory loss, typically resolving within 24 hours, and is often precipitated by stressors or physical exertion. Diagnosis is clinical, based on criteria including witnessed attack, clear anterograde amnesia, and absence of other neurological symptoms. Differential diagnoses include transient epileptic amnesia, transient ischemic attack, and stroke. While diffusion-weighted MRI (DW-MRI) can be used to support diagnosis, its sensitivity is low within the first 12 hours of symptom onset, increasing thereafter, making early DW-MRI a low-yield intervention. Management involves observation and reassurance, with follow-up care as needed.

**Case:**
A 65-year-old man presents to your emergency department with his wife. She tells you that he woke up normally this morning, but after breakfast he began asking the same questions repetitively and was amnestic to the Point, seemingly unable to form new memories. He remained completely awake and alert and otherwise appeared well. There was no history of recent trauma, infectious symptoms, or any other illness.

**Background:**
Transient global amnesia (TGA) is an idiopathic acute neurological disorder characterized by sudden onset anterograde memory loss, typically affecting individuals aged 50 to 70. Clinically significant due to its abrupt presentation, TGA has an incidence of 23.5 per 100,000 people annually and is more prevalent among migraine sufferers. The condition is often triggered by stressors such as physical or emotional stress, pain, or specific activities like the Valsalva maneuver. Symptoms resolve within 24 hours, and diagnosis is based on criteria including witnessed attack, clear anterograde amnesia, absence of other neurological symptoms, and no recent head injury or epileptic features. Differential diagnosis involves excluding conditions like transient epileptic amnesia, transient ischemic attack, and stroke, with diagnostic workup potentially involving laboratory tests, EEGs, ECGs, echocardiograms, and neuroimaging.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the sensitivity of diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI) as a function of time from symptom onset compared to clinical diagnosis of TGA?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Urgent DW-MRI for patients meeting standard diagnostic criteria for TGA is a low yield intervention.

**Case Resolution:**
You perform a good history, followed by a directed physical examination. You then observe him over a period of hours in the emergency department and he slowly begins to form new memories. You perform an unenhanced CT head and basic bloodwork which is normal. He is discharged home with his wife and will follow up with a neurologist as an outpatient.

**Clinical Application:**
We do not need to request urgent DW-MRIs in patients who meet clinical diagnostic criteria for TGA.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have something called transient global amnesia or TGA. This condition is not a stroke or seizure or other dangerous disease and will mostly likely resolve over 24 hours. You should have no long-lasting effects from this. You also explain this to the patient’s caregiver and provide a written handout from the Mayo Clinic explaining the same. Should you develop any other neurologic symptoms such as weakness, sensory changes, speech abnormalities or confusion, or are worried you should return to the emergency department immediately.
