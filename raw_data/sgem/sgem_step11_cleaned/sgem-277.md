**Summary:**
The document discusses the application of the pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm for diagnosing suspected pulmonary embolism (PE) in pregnant women, highlighting its potential to safely rule out PE and reduce the need for CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA). The YEARS algorithm, which incorporates D-dimer testing and clinical criteria, was evaluated in a study involving 498 pregnant women, showing that PE could be excluded in 96% of cases at baseline, with only one deep vein thrombosis (DVT) identified during follow-up. The study found that CTPA could be avoided in 39% of patients, though biases such as incorporation and verification biases were noted. The authors conclude that while the algorithm shows promise, it requires external validation before widespread clinical implementation. The clinical application suggests cautious optimism, advising against unnecessary imaging in the absence of strong clinical suspicion of PE.

**Case:**
A 32-year-old female, G1P0, who is 22 weeks pregnant, presents to your local emergency department with the chief complaint of shortness of breath. She states that for the last one week she has had progressive shortness of breath on exertion. She denies any chest pain, fever, cough, or leg swelling. She has no history of venous thromboembolic (VTE) disease like deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE). But recently she drove seven hours from London, Ontario to Montreal, Quebec. She is worried about the possibility of a PE. She is otherwise healthy, takes only prenatal vitamins, and has no allergies. She is terrified about any radiation exposure in pregnancy and has read on Google that there is a blood test you can order to rule out PE.

**Background:**
Venous thromboembolism (VTE), specifically pulmonary embolism (PE), is a significant clinical condition with an incidence of 1.72 cases per 1,000 deliveries and accounts for approximately one maternal death per 100,000 deliveries. The YEARS criteria, developed to aid in the diagnosis of acute PE, involves assessing clinical signs of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), hemoptysis, and whether PE is the most likely diagnosis, alongside D-dimer testing. If no YEARS criteria are present and the D-dimer is below 1,000 ng/ml, PE is excluded; otherwise, a CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is required. If one or more YEARS criteria are present and the D-dimer is below 500 ng/ml, PE is excluded; otherwise, CTPA is needed. A study by Kabrhel et al. compared the YEARS criteria to standard care, finding that YEARS reduced imaging by 67% but had a lower sensitivity (92.9%) compared to standard care (97.6%). The original YEARS study excluded pregnant women, complicating PE diagnosis in pregnancy due to overlapping symptoms and the risks of diagnostic radiation, which can increase breast cancer risk by 1.5% in young women.

**Clinical Point:**
Can the YEARS algorithm, which utilizes the D-dimer test, be used in pregnant women to rule out the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm has the potential to safely rule out PE and decrease CTPA studies but requires external validation.

**Case Resolution:**
Your clinical gestalt is she does not have a VTE and you do not work her up for a PE.

**Clinical Application:**
The data on the pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm is encouraging but should go through external validation prior to implementation.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You tell her that based on over 20 years of experience you do not think she has a blood clot in her lung. You explain that the blood test she googled is called a d-dimer. While it is good for ruling out blood clots it can be falsely elevated. This can lead to unnecessary CT scans with radiation that she is terrified about being exposed to. You provide reassurance and tell her to return to the ED if she gets increasing shortness of breath, develops chest pain, starts coughing up blood, one leg swells up, or she is worried.
