**Summary:**
In the case of a 65-year-old man with a mild ischemic stroke, the clinical decision revolves around whether to prescribe aspirin alone or in combination with ticagrelor. While aspirin is a standard treatment to prevent recurrent strokes, recent trials have shown mixed results regarding dual antiplatelet therapy. The combination of aspirin and clopidogrel has not consistently reduced stroke recurrence and has increased bleeding risks. Ticagrelor, a direct-acting antiplatelet, when combined with aspirin, showed a reduction in stroke or death within 30 days compared to aspirin alone, but with a higher incidence of severe bleeding and no significant improvement in disability outcomes. Given these findings, the recommendation is to discharge the patient on aspirin alone, with a follow-up in a TIA/Stroke Clinic, emphasizing the importance of risk assessment and shared decision-making in managing antiplatelet therapy.

**Case:**
A 65-year-old man with a history of well-controlled hypertension presents to the emergency department and is diagnosed with a mild stroke (NIHSS score 3). He is a non-smoker, not diabetic, and has never had a stroke before. The only medicine he takes is an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor. You are wondering if he should be discharged on just aspirin or aspirin plus another antiplatelet agent like ticagrelor.

**Background:**
Acute ischemic strokes (AIS) are a leading cause of disability and the third most common cause of death. Aspirin is commonly used to prevent recurrent strokes in patients who have experienced AIS or transient ischemic attacks (TIA), which occur in 5-10% of patients shortly after the initial event. The combination of aspirin and clopidogrel has shown mixed results; a trial by Benavente et al indicated no reduction in recurrent strokes and increased bleeding risk, leading to early termination. Conversely, a trial by Wang et al found that the combination was more effective than aspirin alone in reducing stroke risk within 90 days without increasing hemorrhage risk. Another trial by Johnston et al was stopped early due to increased hemorrhage risk with combination therapy despite a lower risk of ischemic events. Clopidogrel's efficacy is uncertain in certain populations due to genetic variations affecting its activation. Ticagrelor, which does not require metabolic activation, showed no benefit over aspirin alone in preventing cardiovascular events, and its combination with aspirin for stroke prevention remains understudied.

**Clinical Point:**
Is the combination of ticagrelor and aspirin superior to aspirin alone in reducing the risk of subsequent stroke or death among patients with acute non-cardioembolic cerebral ischemia?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Ticagrelor in combination with aspirin cannot be routinely recommended at this time. The decrease in subsequent strokes comes at a cost of increased serious bleeding and no increase in a good neurologic outcome. A risk assessment and shared decision-making is encouraged.

**Case Resolution:**
You discharge the patient home with aspirin alone with an Urgent TIA/Stroke Clinic follow-up in the next 24 to 48 hours.

**Clinical Application:**
With antiplatelet drugs and anticoagulants, there is always a trade-off. While adding another antiplatelet drug to aspirin has the potential to increase efficacy, it also increases the potential harm. I will be more likely to prescribe dual therapy for patients who are at high risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) and low bleeding risk while less likely to prescribe dual therapy for patients at low risk of CAD and high risk of bleeding.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have had a mild stroke. I would recommend taking a low dose of aspirin a day. This can lower your risk of having another stroke. We could also add another drug. It can lower your risk of having another stroke even more. However, it can increase your risk of having a serious bleed. The combination of the two drugs has not been shown to improve your function and is more expensive.
