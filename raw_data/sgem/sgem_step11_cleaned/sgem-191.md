**Summary:**
In a study examining the efficacy of supervised physiotherapy for acute ankle sprains in adults, it was found that adding physiotherapy to usual care did not result in a clinically significant improvement in recovery outcomes. The randomized controlled trial included 504 patients with Grade I/II ankle sprains, comparing early supervised physiotherapy with standard care, which involved basic home management instructions. The primary outcome, excellent recovery at three months, showed no significant difference between the physiotherapy group (43%) and the usual care group (37%), with an absolute difference of 6% (95% CI -3% to 15%, p=0.26). Secondary outcomes at one and six months also showed no significant differences. The study highlighted potential biases, such as selection bias and lack of blinding, and noted that the physiotherapy protocol did not include bracing or manual therapies. The conclusion was that physiotherapy referral for simple ankle sprains in a general emergency department population is not supported by current evidence, although it may be beneficial in specific populations like athletes.

**Case:**
A 43-year-old male presents to your local rural emergency department Monday morning after rolling his ankle during his usual weekend pickup basketball game. He is Ottawa Ankle Rule positive but the x-ray shows no fracture.

**Background:**
The Ottawa Ankle Rules are a highly validated clinical decision instrument designed to safely rule out ankle fractures, applicable to patients as young as five years old. Developed by Dr. Ian Stiell's team in Ottawa, these rules are significant in emergency medicine due to the high prevalence of ankle sprains, which frequently lead to emergency department visits. While ankle sprains are common, the acute management of simple sprains (Grade I and II) lacks well-defined clinical standards, including the role of supervised physiotherapy. Some studies, such as those by Hupperets et al. and Janssen et al., suggest benefits of proprioceptive training and bracing in specific populations, like athletes, but these findings may not be directly applicable to the general emergency department population.

**Clinical Point:**
Does a supervised physiotherapy program result in improved recovery from acute ankle sprains compared to usual care in adult patients presenting to an emergency department or urgent care clinic?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The current evidence does not support the referral of adult patients presenting to the emergency department or urgent care clinic with a simple ankle sprain for physiotherapy.

**Case Resolution:**
The weekend warrior is diagnosed with a Grade I sprain and is given instructions for usual ankle sprain care.

**Clinical Application:**
This is a good study to suggest that for the general population presenting to the emergency department or urgent care center, outpatient physiotherapy is unlikely to provide benefit. However, we do have some evidence of benefit demonstrated in other studies looking at athletes. So, it all depends. In some cases, further assessment by a sports medicine physician and referral to physiotherapy may be reasonable.

**What do I tell my patient?**
Just roll with it…you have sprained your ankle. This should heal over time. It might take you a little longer than when you were a teenager. Get some rest, we will put on a tensor bandage to remind you to behave yourself and you can take some over-the-counter pain medicine if you want. Most people have an excellent recovery but it can take months. We do not have any evidence that physiotherapy can speed up your recovery. However, if your pain is getting worse, your function is going down or you are concerned then get in to see your family physician.
