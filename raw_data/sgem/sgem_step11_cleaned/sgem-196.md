**Summary:**
A recent study explored the efficacy of haloperidol as an adjunctive treatment for gastroparesis symptoms in the emergency department, focusing on its impact on abdominal pain and nausea. The randomized control trial included 33 patients, revealing that haloperidol significantly reduced pain and nausea compared to placebo, with pain scores dropping from 8.5 to 3.1 and nausea scores from 4.5 to 1.8. The study also noted fewer hospital admissions in the haloperidol group. Despite the small sample size, the findings suggest haloperidol could be a beneficial addition to conventional therapy for gastroparesis, though further research is needed to confirm safety and efficacy across broader patient populations.

**Case:**
Your charge nurse approaches as you finish charting on the trauma patient who was just transferred out. "Mrs. G. is back again, vomiting and screaming in pain. This is the third time this month, and nothing ever seems to help. Is there anything we can do for her?" Mrs. G is a 37-year-old female with gastroparesis secondary to diabetes. You know her well, and none of the usual anti-emetics seem to help her symptoms. While inwardly wishing you worked in a country where droperidol was available, you wonder whether there is any new research to guide your management.

**Background:**
Gastroparesis, also known as delayed gastric emptying, is a condition characterized by symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, early satiety, and postprandial fullness, resulting from the paralysis of the stomach. It is most commonly idiopathic, but known causes include diabetes and surgical interventions. The condition poses significant challenges due to limited effective treatments. The only FDA-approved medication for gastroparesis is metoclopramide, which acts as a dopamine receptor blocker with antiemetic and prokinetic properties. Other drugs explored for treatment include serotonin 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron), histamine antagonists (meclizine and promethazine), serotonin 5-HT4 receptor agonists (cisapride), and motilin receptor agonists (erythromycin). Haloperidol, an antipsychotic that blocks dopamine receptors, has been used off-label for nausea and vomiting in various settings. A retrospective study, HUGS, indicated that haloperidol reduced hospital admissions and opioid use in diabetic gastroparesis patients, though no randomized controlled trials had previously assessed its efficacy for nausea and vomiting in gastroparesis until recently.

**Clinical Point:**
Does haloperidol, when added to conventional therapy, decrease abdominal pain and nausea at one hour in patients presenting to the emergency department with known gastroparesis?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Consider adding 5mg IV haloperidol as an adjunct treatment to patients presenting to the emergency department with abdominal pain and nausea from their known gastroparesis.

**Case Resolution:**
You discuss this new trial with your patient, and after a shared decision-making conversation, you decide to try haloperidol as part of their symptom control strategy.

**Clinical Application:**
Adding this first, small randomized control trial to the large clinical experience, we think it is reasonable to consider trying haloperidol as an adjunct to treat gastroparesis.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
As you know, the symptoms from gastroparesis can be very difficult to manage. There is a new research paper saying a medication called haloperidol could help. This medicine has been successfully used for years to treat post-operative patients and cancer patients with nausea and vomiting. I am not saying you have cancer. What I am saying is you have tried all these other medications that have not worked. This recent small study on haloperidol suggests it could work for you. I can see you are suffering and want to try something to help.
