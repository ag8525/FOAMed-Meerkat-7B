**Summary:**
A 43-year-old male presented with chest wall pain and shortness of breath following an ATV accident, revealing multiple right-sided rib fractures and a 500cc hemothorax. A multicenter randomized trial by Kulvatunyou et al. compared the efficacy of small (14 Fr) pigtail catheters (PC) versus large (28-32 Fr) chest tubes (LBCT) for draining traumatic hemothorax in hemodynamically stable patients. The study found that PCs were non-inferior to LBCTs in terms of failure rates (11% vs 13%, p=0.74) and were associated with better patient-reported insertion perception experiences. Despite the trial's early termination and potential selection bias, the findings suggest that PCs are a viable alternative to LBCTs, offering similar efficacy with potentially greater patient comfort. The study supports the use of PCs for stable patients, although further research is needed for unstable cases.

**Case:**
A 43-year-old male presents to your emergency department (ED) the day after being involved in an all-terrain vehicle (ATV) accident. He reports he was riding his ATV along an embankment when it rolled, landing on top of him briefly. He did not seek medical attention at the time of the incident, but he has had persistent chest wall pain and worsening shortness of breath since yesterday evening. He is hemodynamically stable, oxygen saturation is 91% on room air, physical exam reveals ecchymosis and tenderness over the right chest wall with diminished right sided lung sounds. CT scans reveal multiple right sided rib fractures and a hemothorax estimated to measure 500cc with no additional injuries.

**Background:**
Chest tubes are medical devices used to drain air, blood, or fluid from the pleural space, particularly in conditions such as pneumothorax and hemothorax. The placement of chest tubes in the "triangle of safety" is crucial, regardless of whether they are positioned high or low. In cases of spontaneous pneumothorax, conservative management has been shown to be non-inferior to chest tube insertion for large first-time occurrences. For tension pneumothorax, the traditional site for needle decompression, the second intercostal space-midclavicular line, is not necessarily thicker than the fourth/fifth intercostal space-anterior axillary line. Traditionally, traumatic hemothorax is managed with large bore chest tubes, but there is growing evidence supporting the use of smaller, percutaneously inserted chest tubes or pigtail catheters for effective drainage of pleural effusions, pneumothoraces, and hemothorax.

**Clinical Point:**
Are small (14fr) pigtail catheters as effective as large (28-32 fr) chest tubes for the treatment of hemodynamically stable patients with traumatic hemothorax?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It is reasonable to offer a pigtail catheter instead of a large bore chest tube for the evacuation of a traumatic hemothorax in a hemodynamically stable patient.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is consented for the placement of a PC in the ED. He is admitted to the trauma service for monitoring and pain management. The PC is removed on hospital day three and the patient is discharged on hospital day five.

**Clinical Application:**
This trial provides more information that bigger is not necessarily better means of treating traumatic hemothorax.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have a collection of blood between your chest wall and lung. This is called a hemothorax. To relieve your discomfort and prevent your breathing from becoming worse we recommend inserting a tube to drain the blood out. Traditionally this was done with a large tube. However, there’s evidence that it is effective, safe, and more comfortable for patients to use a smaller tube. This smaller tube is called a pigtail catheter. We would give you numbing medicine and some sedation to make it as comfortable as possible. You will be admitted to the hospital for monitoring and pain management while it drains. The tube will stay in place for a few days. Would you like the small tube or the big tube?
