**Summary:**
The document discusses the role of Advanced Practice Providers (APPs) in the Emergency Department (ED) and their impact on diagnostic testing and hospital admissions compared to physicians. A study by Pines et al. evaluated whether ED patients with chest or abdominal pain seen by APPs had different rates of test utilization and admissions compared to those seen by physicians. The study found that APPs and physicians had similar practice patterns, with APPs slightly less likely to order tests or admit patients. The study was observational, with potential confounding factors, and noted that APPs were supervised by physicians, which may have minimized differences. The authors concluded that care by APPs and physicians is largely similar, suggesting that APPs can be effectively integrated into ED care teams. The case resolution involved a patient diagnosed with uncomplicated diverticulitis, managed with antibiotics and discharged, illustrating typical ED management.

**Case:**
A 50-year-old male presents to the Emergency Department (ED) with left lower quadrant abdominal pain. The patient is seen by an advanced practice provider (APP). He wants to know if being seen by an APP alters his chance of diagnostic testing or hospital admission.

**Background:**
Advanced Practice Providers (APPs), including Nurse Practitioners (NPs) and Physician Assistants (PAs), have seen increased utilization in emergency departments (EDs) over the past two decades. APPs undergo approximately two years of medical training, significantly less than the traditional pathway for emergency physicians, which includes four years of medical school followed by three to four years of residency. This discrepancy in training has raised concerns about the adequacy of post-graduate training for APPs in the ED setting. In response, several professional organizations, including the American Academy of Emergency Medicine (AAEM) and the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP), have issued statements and documents addressing the role and training of APPs. A noted issue is the potential for APPs to compensate for their shorter training period by increasing the frequency of diagnostic testing.

**Clinical Point:**
Is ED evaluation by an APP associated with higher test utilization and hospitalization compared with evaluation by a physician?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
In patients presenting to the ED with chest pain and abdominal pain, physicians and APPs had similar practice patterns with test ordering and admissions rates.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient has a work-up for his abdominal pain, including labs and CT abdomen-pelvis, and is diagnosed with uncomplicated diverticulitis. He is discharged home with a prescription for appropriate antibiotics.

**Clinical Application:**
Unsure. We do not have high-quality data to inform us about the impact of APPs on the emergency department. This data comes from one large emergency department group and may lack external validity to other practice environments.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It probably will not matter whether you see the APP or the physician. Both have similar practice styles, and the APP will be supervised by an attending physician.
