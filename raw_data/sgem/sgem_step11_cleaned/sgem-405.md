**Summary:**
A meta-analysis by Kotani et al. examined the impact of etomidate as an induction agent on mortality in critically ill adults undergoing emergency intubation. The study found a high probability that etomidate increases mortality, with a number needed to harm of 31, and a risk ratio of 1.16, indicating a 16% increase in mortality compared to other agents. Etomidate was also associated with a higher incidence of adrenal insufficiency. The analysis included 11 randomized trials with 2,704 patients, but the results were influenced by potential biases, such as lack of blinding and selection bias in some trials. Despite the findings, the evidence remains moderate, and further research is needed to assess long-term outcomes and survival with good neurologic function. Clinicians are advised to consider alternative induction agents, such as ketamine, especially in patients where adrenal suppression is a concern.

**Case:**
You have a critically ill patient that you are preparing to intubate and wonder if the use of etomidate as your induction agent increases mortality.

**Background:**
Intubation is a vital procedure in emergency medicine, with the choice of induction agent being crucial, particularly in critically ill patients. Etomidate is frequently used due to its rapid onset and minimal impact on hemodynamics. However, concerns exist regarding its potential to increase the risk of organ dysfunction and mortality by inhibiting cortisol production through 11-beta-hydroxylase suppression. Multiple randomized trials have investigated the impact of etomidate on adrenal function and mortality, yielding mixed results, with some studies indicating a statistically significant increase in mortality.

**Clinical Point:**
Does etomidate used as an induction agent cause an increased mortality in critically ill adults?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It’s still uncertain if using etomidate as an induction agent decreases the patient-oriented outcome of survival with good neurologic function in critically ill patients requiring emergent endotracheal intubation.

**Case Resolution:**
As you prepare to intubate your critically ill patient, you ask the pharmacist to draw up ketamine as your induction agent.

**Clinical Application:**
Clinicians should use the induction agent they think will be the best choice for successfully intubating the patient who needs an emergent definitive airway.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Since your patient is critically ill, you tell the ICU physician that you avoided etomidate as an induction agent due to concerns about adrenal insufficiency and possible subsequent short-term increase in mortality.
