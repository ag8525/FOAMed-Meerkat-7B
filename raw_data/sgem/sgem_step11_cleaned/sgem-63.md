**Summary:**
A multicenter prospective cohort study involving 2663 patients with lacerations assessed infection risks and the relevance of the "golden period" for wound closure. The study found no significant association between infection rates and the time from injury to repair, challenging the traditional belief that wounds sutured after 6 hours are at higher risk. Key risk factors for infection included diabetes, wound contamination, length greater than 5 cm, and location on the lower extremities. The overall infection rate was low at 2.6%, and prophylactic antibiotics may be considered for high-risk wounds. The study emphasizes that simple lacerations can be managed effectively with thorough cleaning and non-sterile gloves, and that patient-oriented outcomes such as cosmetic appearance should be considered in treatment decisions.

**Case:**
An otherwise healthy 24-year-old man was out at the bar and got into a bit of a tussle at around 11pm. When he woke up the next morning he was surprised to see a 3cm laceration on the back of his right hand. He finally figures that he should have it checked out and makes it into the emergency department at noon the next day. This is more than 12 hours later.

**Background:**
The "Golden Period" for laceration repair refers to the traditional belief that wounds sutured after more than six hours are at a higher risk of infection, based on studies of bacterial colonization. However, recent evidence, though of poor quality and limited by small sample sizes and retrospective designs, has both supported and refuted this time-sensitive approach. In wound care, it is recognized that patients prioritize function and cosmetic outcomes over infection risk, and that tap water is adequate for wound irrigation. Non-sterile gloves are acceptable for simple lacerations, and epinephrine-containing local anesthetics can be used on extremities. Additionally, simple hand lacerations under 2 cm may not require sutures.

**Case Resolution:**
After examining the wound you determine that it is a simple laceration with no involvement of tendons, nerves or major blood vessels. You take a good history and learn that the patient does not have diabetes. You discuss the evidence with the patient and despite the long time from injury to presentation you both decide to go for sutures. You clean the wound thoroughly with tap water and use clean but non-sterile gloves for the repair. You inform him of about a 3% risk of infection and advise him of what to watch for and return if concerned.
