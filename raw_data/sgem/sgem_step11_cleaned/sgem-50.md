**Summary:**
The document discusses the potential benefits of a vasopressin, epinephrine, and corticosteroid (VSE) protocol for in-hospital cardiac arrest resuscitation, compared to epinephrine alone. A randomized clinical trial by Mentzelopoulos et al. demonstrated that the VSE protocol significantly improved survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological outcomes, with a survival rate of 14% versus 5% in the control group. The study was methodologically rigorous, though it noted an imbalance in the causes of cardiac arrest between groups, which could have influenced results. Despite promising findings, the study's applicability is limited to in-hospital settings, and further validation is recommended before altering ACLS protocols. The case resolution highlights the absence of the VSE protocol in the hospital, leading to standard ACLS management with poor outcomes for a patient in PEA arrest due to pneumonia.

**Case Scenario:**
While you are passing through the general ward, a code blue is announced. You find a 72-year-old man admitted for pneumonia in PEA arrest. You are the team leader. Would you use this new VSE Protocol?

**Point:**
Does a vasopressin, epinephrine, and corticosteroid (VSE) protocol for in-hospital cardiac arrest resuscitation improve survival with favorable neurological outcomes compared to epinephrine alone?

**Background:**
The OPALS Trial, conducted by Dr. Ian Steil and published in the New England Journal of Medicine, examined the efficacy of Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. The study found that while ACLS drugs may enhance survival to hospital admission, they do not significantly improve survival to hospital discharge, with an overall survival rate of 5%. In contrast, the study raises the question of whether a vasopressin, epinephrine, and corticosteroid (VSE) protocol could enhance survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological outcomes in cases of in-hospital cardiac arrest, compared to the use of epinephrine alone.

**Clinical Application:**
The results are of interest for patients experiencing cardiac arrest within the emergency department. However, most of the arrests dealt with in the emergency department happen on an out-of-hospital basis. The VSE protocol is not applicable to these cases. Traumatic cardiac arrests were not mentioned in the study and this constitutes an important proportion of cardiac arrests we deal with in the ED. The results cannot be applied to this population. This protocol requires extra nursing resources. If particularly limited, it may negatively impact the quality of the code. Even if the emergency physician decides to apply the VSE, it is not sure that the protocol will be continued during hospitalization (specifically the steroids in MI patients). At our institution, there is reluctance of some CCU staff to indiscriminately administer steroids, even after this publication.

**Bottom Line:**
The results are very interesting, but we believe it is wise to wait for a validation study (different center, different research team) before making changes to the ACLS protocol.

**Case Resolution:**
You run the code on the 72-year-old man who is only "mostly dead". If you recall, he is in a PEA arrest from pneumonia and they often do poorly. There is no VSE protocol to follow in your hospital so you do standard ACLS. He does poorly and you call the code after 20 minutes.
