**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of cutaneous abscesses, focusing on the necessity of irrigation following incision and drainage. The mainstay treatment for abscesses is incision and drainage, with debates surrounding the need for additional interventions such as packing and antibiotics. A study by Chinnock and Hendey concluded that irrigation does not reduce the need for further intervention, with a non-significant difference in outcomes between the irrigation and non-irrigation groups. The study's limitations include its single-center design, non-consecutive patient recruitment, and lack of standardization in treatment protocols. Despite these limitations, the consensus is that irrigation may not be necessary, aligning with the SGEM's conclusion that it does not improve treatment success. This information supports shared decision-making with patients to potentially omit irrigation, reducing procedural pain without compromising outcomes.

**Case:**
A 30-year-old female with a history of cutaneous abscesses comes to your emergency department stating she thinks she has another one developing on her arm. She tells you she wants to do as little as possible to treat the abscess using an incision and drainage because she hates the pain from the procedure, especially the irrigation.

**Background:**
Cutaneous abscesses are prevalent in emergency departments and primarily managed through incision and drainage. Additional management strategies may include wound culture and sensitivity, pain control, packing, and antibiotics, though the necessity of routine packing and antibiotic use is debated. Evidence, including studies by Hankin and Everett and Talan et al., suggests that routine antibiotics may not be required for simple cutaneous abscesses, even with MRSA considerations, and there is no randomized controlled trial supporting the benefit of irrigation. Treatment involves pain, time, financial costs, and contamination risks to both patients and healthcare providers.

**Clinical Point:**
Does irrigation of a cutaneous abscess after incision and drainage reduce the need for further intervention?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Irrigation of a cutaneous abscess after an initial incision and drainage is probably not necessary.

**Case Resolution:**
You discuss with your patient that there is some evidence suggesting that irrigation does not appear to improve outcomes versus not doing the irrigation. With shared decision-making, you decide not to irrigate the abscess.

**Clinical Application:**
Although abscess management varies by location and patient, irrigation of simple cutaneous abscesses may not be needed, as it does not seem to improve treatment success.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We know that incision and drainage can be painful and we want to reduce that pain as much as possible. There is new evidence that suggests irrigation of your abscess may not be beneficial and we can skip this painful part of the procedure if you would like.
