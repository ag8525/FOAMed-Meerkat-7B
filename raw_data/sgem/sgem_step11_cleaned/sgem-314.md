**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), focusing on whether intra-arrest transport or continued on-scene resuscitation is more effective for survival to hospital discharge. The study by Grunau et al. found that continued on-scene resuscitation was associated with a higher probability of survival compared to intra-arrest transport, although the observational nature of the study limits causal conclusions. The study involved adults with non-traumatic OHCA treated by EMS agencies in the USA, with exclusions for factors like DNR orders and missing data. Despite the findings, the document emphasizes the lack of high-quality evidence favoring one approach over the other, advising adherence to local protocols. Key management steps for OHCA include early recognition, EMS activation, high-quality CPR, and early defibrillation for shockable rhythms. The document also highlights potential biases and the heterogeneity of EMS systems, which may affect the generalizability of the results.

**Case:**
During a busy emergency department (ED) shift, the paramedic phone rings. On the other end of the line is one of your local crews who have responded to a 54-year-old male with a witnessed cardiac arrest. CPR is currently in progress with a single shock having been delivered. The crew is asking if they should transport the patient with resuscitation ongoing?

**Background:**
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical emergency condition characterized by the cessation of cardiac activity outside a hospital setting, requiring immediate medical intervention. The clinical significance of OHCA is underscored by its high mortality rate and the urgent need for effective emergency medical services (EMS) response. Different countries employ varying EMS models to address OHCA; the European model is physician-led and emphasizes comprehensive on-site care, whereas the North American model often prioritizes rapid transport to medical facilities. Within the United States, EMS practices vary, with some agencies transporting nearly all patients regardless of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), while others limit transport to those who achieve ROSC. The literature does not conclusively favor one approach over the other in terms of patient outcomes for adults experiencing refractory OHCA.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the association of intra-arrest transport compared to continued on-scene resuscitation in regards to survival to hospital discharge in adult patients with an OHCA?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We do not know if it is better to load and go or stay and play when treating adult patients with OHCAs.

**Case Resolution:**
You instruct the paramedic crew to continue on-scene resuscitation.

**Clinical Application:**
The most important aspects of adult OHCA care continue to be recognition of a sudden cardiac arrest, activation of EMS, early high-quality CPR, and early defibrillation for shockable rhythms.

**What Do I Tell the Paramedic?**
Follow your local protocols (stay and play or load and go). We don’t have high-quality evidence that one strategy is better than the other.
