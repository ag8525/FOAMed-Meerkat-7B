**Summary:**
In the management of acute pericarditis, the addition of colchicine to standard anti-inflammatory therapy, such as ibuprofen or aspirin, significantly reduces the recurrence or refractory symptoms of the condition, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 5. This randomized controlled trial involving 240 patients demonstrated that colchicine, administered at 0.5-1.0 mg daily for three months alongside conventional anti-inflammatories and gastrointestinal prophylaxis, effectively prolongs the time to first recurrence and decreases symptom persistence and hospitalization rates. The study highlighted that while gastrointestinal disturbances were the most common side effect, they were not significantly different between the colchicine and placebo groups. The trial was well-conducted with high adherence and no losses to follow-up, although it was underpowered to detect rare adverse effects. The findings support the use of colchicine in uncomplicated pericarditis without systemic manifestations or elevated cardiac biomarkers.

**Case Scenario:**
A 33-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with sharp retrosternal chest pain which is pleuritic in nature. Well’s low and PERC negative. ECG consistent with acute pericarditis. Other lab investigations normal.

**Point:**
Is colchicine more effective in combination than standard anti-inflammatory therapy for acute pericarditis, than standard anti-inflammatory therapy alone?

**Background:**
Pericarditis is the inflammation of the pericardium, primarily due to infectious or idiopathic causes, with less common etiologies including neoplastic, autoimmune, drugs, trauma, and radiation. Clinically significant due to its characteristic presentation of positional, pleuritic chest pain radiating to the trapezius muscles, it is exacerbated by lying flat and alleviated by sitting forward. Diagnosis involves identifying a pericardial friction rub and effusion on ultrasound, with ECG as the primary diagnostic tool showing widespread ST elevation and PR segment changes. Blood tests like WBC and CRP are non-specific, while troponin levels help assess myocardial involvement. The consensus diagnostic criteria include classic chest pain, friction rub, effusion on ultrasound, and ECG changes. Colchicine, a microtubule inhibitor derived from the autumn crocus, is used for treatment, though it has a narrow therapeutic window, posing a risk of toxicity.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Adding colchicine to usual anti-inflammatory management with GI prophylaxis prevents recurrence or refractory pericarditis symptoms with a NNT of 5. The most common side effect being diarrhea. Strict exclusion criteria limits application to uncomplicated pericarditis without systemic manifestations or evidence of elevated cardiac biomarkers (including non-specific troponitis).

**Case Resolution:**
This 33-year-old woman diagnosed with pericarditis was given a prescription for ibuprofen 600mg TID for 7 days followed by a tapering dose over 1 month, colchicine 0.5mg daily for three months, and a proton pump inhibitor for 3 months. She was also referred to cardiology for ongoing follow-up.

**Clinical Application:**
Colchicine (0.5-1.0mg) daily for three months in conjunction with conventional anti-inflammatories and GI prophylaxis can be used for acute non-suppurative pericarditis to prevent recurrence or refractory symptoms.

**What Do I Tell Patients:**
You have inflammation around your heart called pericarditis. Anti-inflammatory drugs like ibuprofen and ASA can help treat your symptoms. An additional medicine called colchicine has been shown to prevent prolonged symptoms. Colchicine has also been shown to prevent pericarditis from happening again. These two types of medications can be hard on the stomach, so we are going to give you something to protect your gut. The most common side effect of these effective pericarditis treatments is diarrhea.
