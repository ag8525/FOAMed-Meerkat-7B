**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of atrial fibrillation (AF) in the emergency department, focusing on the debate between rate control and rhythm control strategies. It highlights a study by DeMeester et al. that implemented an algorithm to reduce hospital admissions for AF patients, showing a significant decrease in admissions from 80.4% to 67.4% without increasing return visits to the emergency department. The study suggests that low-risk AF patients can be managed safely as outpatients, though identifying high-risk patients still relies on clinical judgment. The case resolution involves achieving rate control with an oral calcium channel blocker and discharging the patient with follow-up, emphasizing the potential for outpatient management to reduce costs and inconvenience for low-risk patients.

**Case:**
A 62-year-old Canadian is on vacation in up-state Michigan, and after a celebratory evening, presents to your emergency department with palpitations. "I’ve had atrial fibrillation a number of times before. Usually, they just shock me and send me home." Local practice is usually to treat rapid atrial fibrillation with a calcium channel blocker infusion and admit to the hospital. As the conversation progresses, you wonder whether it might be safe to discharge some atrial fibrillation patients home for outpatient follow-up.

**Background:**
Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a prevalent cardiac dysrhythmia characterized by rapid and irregular heartbeats, often leading to symptoms such as increased heart rate, chest pain, and weakness. The clinical management of AF in the emergency department involves a longstanding debate between rate control and rhythm control strategies. For patients with chronic AF or unknown onset time and rapid ventricular response, the standard approach includes rate control and initiation of anticoagulation therapy. Beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers, particularly diltiazem, are commonly used for rate control, with evidence suggesting diltiazem achieves more rapid rate control than metoprolol. In Canada, rhythm control is frequently employed, with the Ottawa Aggressive Protocol effectively converting recent-onset AF to sinus rhythm, resulting in high discharge rates from the emergency department. In contrast, the USA often favors rate control due to concerns about thromboembolic risks associated with cardioversion, leading to higher admission rates for cardiology evaluation.

**Clinical Point:**
Can an emergency department algorithm for atrial fibrillation management decrease the number of patients admitted to the hospital?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There are clearly patients with primary atrial fibrillation who can be managed safely as outpatients. There are no evidence-based criteria for identifying high-risk patients who require admission, so for now, we will have to rely on clinical judgment.

**Case Resolution:**
You are able to achieve rate control with an oral calcium channel blocker. The patient is asymptomatic, and your workup has not identified any high-risk features. After a shared decision-making conversation, you discharge the patient home with close follow-up with the cardiology department.

**Clinical Application:**
Hospital admissions are very expensive and inconvenient for patients. For high-risk patients, the hospital environment may help prevent bad outcomes, but for low-risk patients, we should consider outpatient management for atrial fibrillation.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have a condition called atrial fibrillation, which basically means your heart is beating fast and irregularly. The management of this condition varies, depending on where you are in the world. Seeing as you are currently feeling well, and have no risk factors for stroke, we have a few options including trying to shock your heart back into its regular rhythm or trying to slow it down. Once we have the arrhythmia controlled, we can discuss whether it would be a better option to admit you to the hospital or to go home with close follow-up.
