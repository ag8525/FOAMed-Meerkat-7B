**Summary:**
In febrile infants aged ≤60 days with a positive urinalysis indicating a urinary tract infection (UTI), the necessity of performing a lumbar puncture is debated, particularly in those aged 29-60 days. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests that lumbar puncture may be optional in these cases if inflammatory markers are normal. A study involving 7,180 infants found no cases of bacterial meningitis in infants over 28 days with positive urinalysis and normal inflammatory markers, suggesting that lumbar puncture may not be routinely required in this group. Blood work and cultures remain important, but in well-appearing infants with low-risk laboratory criteria, empiric antibiotic treatment without lumbar puncture may be appropriate, with close follow-up.

**Case:**
A 6-week-old girl is brought into the emergency department (ED) for fever of 38.5°C that started four hours prior to presentation. Her parents noted that she has been fussier today and has had feeding a little less than normal, but she’s had no other symptoms. She is otherwise healthy, full-term female who had no pre- or postnatal complications. On exam she is well-appearing, and there are no focal signs of infection. You decide to start by obtaining blood and catheterized urine for testing.
The urinalysis shows 15 WBCs, 2+ leukocyte esterase and positive nitrites. While waiting for the results of the blood tests, you tell the family the news that their child likely has a urinary tract infection. The family asks you, *“does this mean we found the source of her fever? Our son also had a fever when he was very young, and he had to get a lumbar puncture? Do we need to do a lumbar puncture for her today?”*

**Background:**
Febrile infants aged 60 days or younger are at increased risk for serious bacterial infections (SBI) such as urinary tract infections (UTI), bacteremia, and meningitis, with UTIs being the most common. Invasive bacterial infections (IBI), including bacteremia and meningitis, are critical to identify. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) has issued guidelines for managing febrile infants aged 8-60 days, recommending that lumbar puncture may be performed in infants 22 days and older with positive urinalysis but normal inflammatory markers. There is significant variability in clinical practice, and studies indicate a low prevalence of meningitis in infants with positive urinalysis, particularly in those aged 29-60 days, where the risk is estimated at 0.2%. Indiscriminate lumbar punctures can lead to complications such as sterile pleocytosis, observed in 18-24% of these infants, resulting in prolonged hospitalizations and increased use of intravenous antibiotics.

**Clinical Point:**
In a febrile infant ≤60 days with an abnormal urinalysis suggesting a UTI, do they really need a lumbar puncture or blood work?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
In well-appearing infants ≤60 days with positive UA, continue to perform blood work and blood culture. However, infants with positive UA in the second month of life may not routinely need a lumbar puncture.

**Case Resolution:**
Blood test results demonstrate that procalcitonin is <0.5 ng/dL and the ANC is <4000. You have a conversation with the parents and tell them the results of both the blood and urine testing. You review the risks and benefits of performing a lumbar puncture versus empirically treating with antibiotics. Ultimately, the parents opt to forgo the lumbar puncture and receive empiric antibiotics with the plan to follow up closely with their pediatrician the next day.

**Clinical Application:**
In well-appearing febrile infants >28 days with positive UA and meeting low-risk laboratory criteria, it may not be necessary to perform a lumbar puncture.
