**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of glucocorticoid therapy in patients with septic shock, particularly focusing on the administration of hydrocortisone. It references the ADRENAL trial, which found no significant difference in 90-day mortality between patients receiving hydrocortisone and those given a placebo. Despite some secondary outcomes suggesting faster resolution of shock and shorter ICU stays with hydrocortisone, these findings were not deemed clinically significant. The trial highlighted the importance of large sample sizes to avoid misleading results from smaller studies. Ultimately, the study concluded that glucocorticoids should not be routinely used in septic shock management due to the lack of mortality benefit and potential for adverse events. The case resolution emphasized the importance of source control in managing septic shock, as demonstrated by the patient's improvement following the identification and treatment of an obstructive kidney stone.

**Case:**
64-year-old male presents to your emergency department with worsening abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and anorexia for the past week. On presentation, he is lethargic and hypotensive. He requires control of his airway and is given a 30 cc/kg fluid bolus and started on norepinephrine. His urine analysis is consistent with a urinary tract infection. Over the course of his emergency department stay, he has escalating vasopressor requirements. After starting vasopressin, you ask yourself if you should be adding steroids for potential relative adrenal insufficiency?

**Background:**
Septic shock is a severe medical condition characterized by a significant drop in blood pressure and organ dysfunction due to infection, often associated with high morbidity and mortality. A subset of patients with septic shock may experience relative adrenal insufficiency, where their adrenal axis cannot adequately respond to stress, prompting the use of glucocorticoid therapy. However, the clinical efficacy of corticosteroids in treating septic shock remains ambiguous due to conflicting evidence from major studies. The Annane et al trial in 2002 suggested a mortality benefit for patients with relative adrenal insufficiency receiving corticosteroids, while the larger CORTICUS study in 2008 found no significant benefit compared to placebo. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in patient populations, with Annane et al focusing on a sicker cohort and CORTICUS including healthier individuals, potentially obscuring the treatment effect. Additionally, the Annane et al results might have been influenced by statistical variability and small sample size, whereas CORTICUS may reflect a regression to the mean.

**Clinical Point:**
Does the administration of a continuous infusion of hydrocortisone to a general population of patients in septic shock on mechanical ventilation improve survival?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The administration of a continuous infusion of hydrocortisone to a general population of patients in septic shock on mechanical ventilation cannot be recommended at this time.

It is hard to view these results as anything but a negative study. While there are some potential signals of benefit in the patients who received hydrocortisone, they were fleeting and ultimately had minimal influence on patient-centered outcomes. If a true benefit does exist, it is small and would require an impossibly large trial to empirically demonstrate.

Despite this, I think the results of the ADRENAL trial will paradoxically increase the use of glucocorticoids in patients with septic shock. The desire to act in the face of critical illness is strong. And the influential power of a statistically significant p-value is strong.

People should read the article called *Don’t just do something, stand there!* *The value and art of deliberate clinical inertia* (Emerg Med Australas 2018). The senior author on it is Dr. Daniel Fatovich and it is an excellent explanation of why it can be hard to avoid unnecessary tests and treatments.

When faced with a patient in refractory shock, many will continue to give corticosteroids in the hopes they may help the individual patient laying before them. It will just happen with an added degree of hand-wringing, having read the literature refuting this practice and simply want nothing to do with its conclusions.

**Case Resolution:**
Hydrocortisone was held and the patient’s shock worsened over the next few hours. A CT abdomen/pelvis was ordered because of a concern of lack of source control. The imaging identified an obstructive kidney stone with an associated pyelonephritis. Urology was consulted, and a drain was placed. Over the next 24-hours, the patient’s shock resolved, and he came off his vasopressors.

**Clinical Application:**
In an undifferentiated cohort of patients with septic shock, the addition of glucocorticoid therapy does not improve mortality.

**What Do I Tell My Patient’s Family?**
Your husband/father is fairly sick. We have resuscitated him with IV fluids and medications that will bring his blood pressure up to a safe level. Recent literature suggests that the addition of glucocorticoids does not improve outcomes when compared to standard care. Importantly, a source was identified, and we now think we have control of his infection.
