**Summary:**
A study by Young et al. in the NEJM evaluated the impact of regular intravenous acetaminophen administration on ICU-free days in febrile ICU patients with suspected infections. The randomized controlled trial included patients aged 16 and older with a temperature of 38°C or higher, excluding those with acute brain disorders, liver dysfunction, and other specific conditions. The primary outcome showed no significant difference in ICU-free days between the acetaminophen and placebo groups. Secondary outcomes, including mortality and length of stay, also showed no significant differences, although a subgroup analysis indicated a shorter ICU stay for survivors receiving acetaminophen. The study concluded that routine acetaminophen use for fever in ICU patients does not improve clinically important outcomes, suggesting its use should be reserved for patient comfort rather than routine fever management.

**Case:**
A 64-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with fever, urinary symptoms, and altered mental status. You diagnose her with sepsis with a probable urinary source. You rapidly provide empiric antibiotics and initiate fluid resuscitation. You are ready to send her up to the ICU for monitoring when your nurse asks, *“shouldn’t we give her some acetaminophen for her fever?”*

**Background:**
Fever in adult ICU patients is a contentious issue, with debates centered on its potential harm or benefit. Fever is a natural immune response that may aid in fighting infections, but it also increases metabolic stress, which could be detrimental to critically ill patients. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that fever in children is not inherently dangerous and may be beneficial, with antipyretic therapy aimed at improving comfort rather than merely reducing temperature. However, the implications for adult ICU patients remain debated, with limited high-quality evidence available to definitively guide clinical practice.

**Clinical Point:**
Does regular administration of intravenous acetaminophen in febrile ICU patients being treated for a known or suspected infection impact the number of ICU-free days?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The routine use of IV acetaminophen for the treatment of fever in ICU patients thought to be due to infection cannot be recommended at this time.

**Case Resolution:**
You explain to your patient and nursing colleague that although acetaminophen does eliminate fever, it does not seem to change any other clinically important outcomes. You offer to provide acetaminophen if fever is causing your patient any discomfort, but otherwise suggest it is not required.

**What do I tell my patient?**
Like many patients, you may have heard that fever requires treatment. Fever doesn’t seem to be harmful, and it may even be helping you fight off your infection. The best study we have so far shows that treating fever with acetaminophen does not improve your health, and therefore I don’t think it is required. However, if your fever causes you any discomfort, we can give you acetaminophen to make you feel better.
