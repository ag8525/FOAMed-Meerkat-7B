**Summary:**
A 32-year-old male construction worker presents with a minimally displaced midwaist scaphoid fracture following a fall on an outstretched hand. The clinical discussion revolves around the management of such fractures, which are common and often challenging to diagnose accurately with initial imaging. While traditional management involves cast immobilization, there is a trend towards surgical fixation to prevent non-union and facilitate quicker return to function, especially in manual laborers. A systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs found no significant difference in functional outcomes at 12 months between operative and non-operative treatments for minimally displaced scaphoid fractures, though operative management showed some benefits in grip strength and range of motion at six months. However, surgery carries a higher complication rate. The decision between surgical and conservative management should be patient-centered, considering the individual's occupational needs and preferences.

**Case:**
A 32-year-old male construction worker presents to the emergency department (ED) after falling on his right dominant hand. He has swelling in his distal radius, snuffbox tenderness, decreased range of motion and is neurovascularly intact distal to the injury. X-rays demonstrate a minimally displaced midwaist fracture of the scaphoid. He’s got a big job coming up in a couple of months and can’t work with a cast. He asks if surgery would be a better option?

**Background:**
Scaphoid fractures are the most prevalent carpal fractures encountered in emergency departments, often resulting from a fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH) or other wrist hyperextension mechanisms. Diagnosis is challenging as initial x-rays detect only 17% of cases, with follow-up x-rays identifying an additional 7%. Physical examination for snuff box tenderness is commonly used, but its reliability is moderate with significant variability. Other physical exam maneuvers and imaging modalities like bone scans, ultrasounds, and CT scans have limited accuracy, with MRI being the most reliable diagnostic tool. There is no validated clinical decision instrument for scaphoid fractures, complicating diagnosis. Most scaphoid fractures are non-displaced and treated with cast immobilization, but displaced fractures have a higher risk of non-union, potentially leading to secondary osteoarthritis. This risk has prompted a trend towards urgent surgical fixation. The decision for surgical intervention is made collaboratively between the patient and surgeon, with emergency physicians playing a role in preparing patients for these discussions.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the effectiveness of operative vs nonoperative management of un-displaced and minimally displaced (≤ 2 mm) scaphoid fractures?

**Case Resolution:**
You engage in some shared decision making with the patient. He ultimately decides to have surgery and try to get back to work sooner.

**Clinical Application:**
It all depends. This SRMA did not demonstrate a statistical benefit to surgery at one year post-op. However, what about the Bond et al article (which the meta-analysis never seems mentions) where people healed four to five weeks earlier and had an earlier return to full duty without limitations (military population) six weeks earlier.
In my practice, operative treatment was reserved for active-duty personnel with a patient centered joint decision. This was because they fit the bill of manual labor and needed use of their hand/wrist.
As a surgeon, I would have mine fixed. If I had a job where I could spend 8-12 weeks in a thumb spica cast or short arm cast, I would be fine with nonoperative treatment. There does appear to be a role for early operative treatment in select patients and this should be further studied with modern day surgical techniques (percutaneous, etc).

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have broken a bone in your wrist called the scaphoid. It is very important it heals well to prevent arthritis. There are two ways it can be fixed. One is with an operation to pin/screw the bone together. The other is with wearing a cast for two to three months with follow up to ensure there is no displacement. Both end up healing fine when you look a majority of outcomes studies. An operation is quicker but does have a few more potential risks. We need to be honest with our patients and explain to them the potential risks and benefits to both operative and nonoperative treatment.
