**Summary:**
In the pre-hospital management of acute behavioral disturbances, a retrospective cohort study by Muldowney et al. compared the efficacy and safety of midazolam versus ketamine, focusing on the need for emergent airway support. The study, involving 376 patients predominantly agitated due to substance use, found no significant difference in the requirement for advanced airway management between the two sedatives, with 12% of midazolam and 11% of ketamine patients needing intervention. Secondary outcomes, including ED intubation rates and mortality, also showed no statistical differences. Despite the study's limitations, such as potential selection and information biases inherent in its retrospective design, the findings suggest that both midazolam and ketamine are viable options for sedation in the EMS setting, with the choice depending on clinical context and provider preference. The study underscores the need for further research to refine sedation protocols in pre-hospital care.

**Case:**
You’re an experienced paramedic working a busy night shift in an urban EMS system. Dispatch sends you to a call for a 35-year-old male found acting erratically in a public park. Upon arrival, you find him disoriented, agitated, and combative. Bystanders report that he has been using methamphetamine and alcohol.
The patient is uncooperative, making verbal de-escalation ineffective. Physical restraint is needed for transport. Your EMS protocol allows for pharmacologic sedation with either midazolam (1 to 5 mg IV/IM, repeat every 2 to 5 minutes as needed) or ketamine (5 mg/kg IM, max 500 mg).
The patient is tachycardic (HR 122 bpm), hypertensive (BP 156/96 mmHg), and has a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 12. You need to act quickly for scene safety and the patient’s well-being.

**Background:**
Acutely agitated patients in the pre-hospital setting pose significant challenges for emergency medical services (EMS) due to potential underlying causes such as psychiatric disorders, substance intoxication, metabolic disturbances, traumatic brain injury, or postictal states. Effective management is crucial to prevent escalation to self-harm, harm to others, or interference with medical care. Initial management involves verbal de-escalation, but pharmacologic sedation may be necessary if these techniques fail. The choice of sedative is critical, balancing rapid sedation with the risk of adverse effects like respiratory depression and cardiovascular instability. Benzodiazepines, such as midazolam, have been traditionally used for their anxiolytic and muscle-relaxant properties, though they carry risks of respiratory depression and paradoxical agitation. Ketamine has gained favor for its rapid onset and dissociative properties while preserving airway reflexes, though it may cause emergence reactions and increased blood pressure. There is no consensus on the optimal pharmacologic approach, resulting in varied practices across EMS systems. A recent study aims to compare ketamine and midazolam in the out-of-hospital setting to provide evidence-based guidance on their efficacy and safety.

**Clinical Point:**
In prehospital patients requiring pharmacologic sedation for acute behavioural disturbance, does ketamine or midazolam result in a lower need for emergent airway support?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Both midazolam and ketamine seem like reasonable choices in managing agitated patients in the pre-hospital setting, depending on clinical context and provider preference.

**Case Resolution:**
You administer intramuscular ketamine, and the patient becomes much calmer. His vital signs are stable, maintains spontaneous respirations with no signs of airway compromise and is safely transported to the ED.

**Clinical Application:**
Given the comparable safety profiles, your decision should weigh speed of onset, route of administration, and context (i.e. it all depends).

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
When we found you, you were very agitated and possibly at risk of hurting yourself or others. For your safety and ours, we had to give you medication to calm you down. We used a sedative that works quickly and is commonly used in emergencies like yours. Thankfully, you didn’t need a breathing tube, and everything went smoothly.
