**Summary:**
Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (CHS) is characterized by cyclic vomiting and abdominal pain in chronic cannabis users, often relieved by hot showers. This case study highlights a 32-year-old male with CHS, confirmed by his heavy cannabis use and symptom exacerbation upon cannabis consumption. Diagnostic workup, including gastroscopy, was unremarkable, and standard antiemetics were ineffective. A randomized controlled trial (Ruberto et al.) compared haloperidol to ondansetron for CHS treatment, finding haloperidol superior in reducing nausea and pain at two hours post-treatment, despite potential adverse effects like akathisia and dystonia. The study's limitations include small sample size, selection bias, and early termination, necessitating further research to confirm haloperidol's efficacy. Clinicians should consider patient preferences and potential side effects when choosing between haloperidol and alternative treatments like topical capsaicin.

**Case:**
A 32-year-old male patient presents to your emergency department (ED) with severe nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain. He reports the symptoms have been continuous for 4 or 5 hours. Over-the-counter medications like acetaminophen (paracetamol) and ibuprofen have not helped. His flatmate (roommate) says he only gets relief by taking really long, hot showers.
On examination, his vital signs are normal, and he is afebrile. The abdomen exam shows no peritoneal sign and normal bowel sounds are heard. Laboratory values are unremarkable. An ultrasound does not show any free fluids or any signs of an ileus, appendicitis or gallbladder disease.
His pain and nausea are difficult to control with standard medications. You admit him to hospital and the next day he undergoes gastroscopy which is unremarkable.
In the afternoon the patient is seen by a nurse when he is smoking “weed” (cannabis) in the garden of the hospital. He admits to being a heavy cannabis user and his symptoms do seem to get worse when smoking weed. You suspect he has cannabis hyperemesis syndrome and discharge him home with the recommendation to stop smoking as much weed.

**Background:**
Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS) is a medical condition characterized by cyclical vomiting and abdominal pain associated with chronic marijuana use, first identified by Allen et al. in 2004. The syndrome is clinically significant due to its association with long-term cannabis use, often daily, and involves the overstimulation of the CB1 receptor in the gastrointestinal system, leading to reduced motility and esophageal sphincter relaxation. This results in the loss of cannabis's anti-nausea effects and persistent vomiting. Diagnosis of CHS involves specific criteria, including five major and five supportive features, emphasizing the necessity of recognizing long-term cannabis use as a key diagnostic element.

**Clinical Point:**
Can haloperidol effectively treat patients with cannabis hyperemesis syndrome?

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is offered a number of treatment options including ondansetron, haloperidol, or topical capsaicin in a shared decision model. He decides to try capsaicin first, but there is no reduction in abdominal pain. Therefore, the patient asks to be given low-dose haloperidol. His symptoms get better within two hours and he is discharged home.

**Clinical Application:**
Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome is a challenge for both the patient and clinician. Haloperidol is another treatment option with weak evidence of efficacy but does come with known potential harms. The evidence for topical capsaicin is also weak for potential benefit but does not have the same potential harms as haloperidol. Lacking clear evidence to support one method over another the patient will be asked what they would prefer.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
The amount of cannabis you are using is most likely causing your pain and vomiting. It can be hard to treat these symptoms once they have started. The best way is prevention by decreasing your cannabis use. The traditional medicines we use are not very effective. There is some weak evidence that a drug called haloperidol could work better, get you home sooner but it comes with a risk of uncommon but serious side effects. Another option would be to rub capsaicin cream on your abdomen. There is some weak data for this treatment, and it does not come with any known serious side effects (Dean et al 2020). What would you like to do?
