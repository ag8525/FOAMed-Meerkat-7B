**Summary:**
In the context of trauma patient intubation, the study by Yeatts et al. compared video laryngoscopy (VL) using the Glidescope to direct laryngoscopy (DL) and found no significant difference in mortality rates between the two methods. However, VL was associated with a longer intubation time and potentially higher mortality in patients with severe head injuries, as indicated by post-hoc analysis. Both methods achieved a first pass success rate of 80%. The study, a randomized controlled trial, highlighted the potential for selection bias due to the discretion allowed to attending physicians in patient enrollment. Despite this, the findings suggest that VL is at least as effective as DL, though it may require more time, particularly in complex cases such as those involving cervical collars. Proper pre-oxygenation is crucial to mitigate desaturation risks during the procedure.

**Case Scenario:**
You are working on a Saturday overnight shift when you get a trauma patient from a rollover motor vehicle accident. The patient is a 21-year-old male who had "just two beers" and was speeding at over 90 miles per hour (~160km/hr). He arrives at the ED combative, and it's clear he has a bad head injury along with several extremity fractures and a surgical abdomen. It’s also clear that he needs to be intubated and, as Cliff Reid likes to say, he has a lack of ketamine in his blood. You set up your equipment for the intubation and one of the nurses asks, "Are you going to use DL or the GlideScope?" You ponder this for a second as you prep your other equipment.

**Point:**
Which is better for intubation in trauma patients, video or direct laryngoscopy?

**Background:**
Emergency medicine is primarily responsible for managing acute airway situations, with emergency physicians performing 87% of intubations according to the National Emergency Airway Registry, and achieving a 95% success rate on initial attempts, predominantly using rapid sequence intubations (RSIs). Recent advancements in airway management have introduced various video laryngoscopy (VL) devices, which are increasingly replacing traditional direct laryngoscopy (DL). Studies, such as those by Levitan et al., have explored the complexities of DL versus VL, emphasizing pre-oxygenation and desaturation prevention. A randomized controlled trial by Yeatts et al. evaluated the impact of VL on trauma patient survival, comparing it to DL, with primary outcomes focusing on mortality and secondary outcomes including subgroup survival, intubation duration, desaturation, and first pass success rates.

**Case Resolution:**
You decide to use the glidescope in this case because you feel as if it might be better in this case since the patient is in a cervical collar. Knowing that the glidescope may take a little longer to pass the tube, you make sure to properly pre-oxygenate the patient with high flow oxygen with a non-rebreather mask at 30-60 liters per minute and use a nasal cannula set at 15 liters per minute kept on during your intubation attempt. You get an excellent view with the GlideScope and pass the tube on your first attempt.

**Clinical Application:**
This study supports the use of video laryngoscopy in trauma patients.

**What Do I Tell Patients:**
Video laryngoscopy is at least as good as direct laryngoscopy.
