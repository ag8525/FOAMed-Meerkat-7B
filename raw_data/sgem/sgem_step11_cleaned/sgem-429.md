**Summary:**
In pediatric oropharyngeal trauma, particularly following incidents like a child falling with an object in their mouth, the risk of serious complications such as carotid artery injury is a concern, though rare. A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies on the use of CT angiography (CTA) in these cases found that routine CTA is not recommended due to its limited impact on management and outcomes, and the associated radiation risks. The review highlighted significant practice variation and a lack of high-quality evidence guiding the use of CTA, with most studies being retrospective and prone to bias. In clinical practice, a thorough physical examination is crucial, and imaging should be reserved for specific scenarios such as active bleeding or neurological changes. Shared decision-making with the family is essential, considering the child's current well-being and the potential risks and benefits of further imaging.

**Case:**
You’re working the morning shift in the emergency department (ED) when you encounter a 3-year-old boy and his family. His parents tell you that he was getting ready to go off to daycare and brushing his teeth while standing on a step stool by the sink. He slipped and the toothbrush poked him in the back of the mouth. Initially, his parents noticed that he was bleeding from his mouth and saw what looked like a wound towards the back of his throat. The boy cried immediately afterwards but has otherwise been acting like himself. On your exam, you notice a small penetrating intraoral injury just lateral to the soft palate without evidence of continued bleeding. His parents ask you, *“Is he going to be okay? We’re glad he’s not bleeding anymore. Do you think he needs any imaging to see if he hurt anything?”*

**Background:**
Oropharyngeal injury in children, often resulting from falls with objects in their mouths, can lead to blunt or penetrating trauma. Such injuries are clinically significant due to the potential for deep space neck infections and the risk of carotid artery damage, which can result in thrombosis, dissection, and cerebral infarctions. Although these severe complications are documented, they are relatively rare. Unlike pediatric head or abdominal trauma, there are no standardized clinical decision tools for imaging in cases of oropharyngeal injury, leading to variability in the imaging studies ordered.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the role of CT angiography (CTA) in the diagnosis and management of pediatric oropharyngeal trauma?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We don’t know the role of CT angiography (CTA) in pediatric oropharyngeal trauma because there is a lack of high-quality evidence to inform our care.

**Case Resolution:**
You are reassured by your physical exam of the patient. You consult your friendly otolaryngologist who based on your description of the child, exam, and mechanism recommends a trial of oral antibiotics for prophylaxis. You engage in shared decision-making with the family and discuss the potential harms and benefits of obtaining imaging. They state that given how good the boy is looking and acting now, they feel comfortable with going home and watching him carefully.

**Clinical Application:**
Perform a good examination of your patient! Imaging is not always needed, especially if the injury is midline or paramedian or only soft palate and does not go into the lateral pharyngeal wall. There are currently no standards of care from professional associations about how to manage these types of injuries and remains a highly debated topic.
Most times management is not changed by CT angiography. It can be useful in very specific clinical scenarios which include profuse bleeding, neuro status change, crepitus in the neck, or findings on CT with contrast (flow void of carotid, fluid extravasation, significant air in carotid space).

**What Do I Tell the Patient/Family?**
Your child has a small cut from where the toothbrush poked him in the mouth. There is no bleeding from the area. I am reassured that he looks well and is acting appropriately. Sometimes we get concerned about this kind of injury because there are some blood vessels behind it. Let’s discuss some of the options and decide together.
