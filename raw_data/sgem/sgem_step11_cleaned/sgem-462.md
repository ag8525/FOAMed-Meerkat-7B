**Summary:**
In the context of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), the choice between intravenous (IV) and intraosseous (IO) access for epinephrine administration remains a critical consideration, as highlighted by the Paramedic 3 Trial. This multi-center, randomized trial found no significant difference in 30-day survival rates between IO and IV access, with survival rates of 4.5% and 5.1% respectively. Despite the challenges of securing IV access in the field, the study suggests that both methods are comparable in terms of survival and neurological outcomes, although the trial faced limitations such as potential selection bias and early termination due to low enrollment. The findings emphasize the importance of focusing on high-quality CPR and early defibrillation over the choice of vascular access, as the overall impact of epinephrine on long-term outcomes remains uncertain.

**Case:**
You’re doing a ride along with your local emergency medical service (EMS) crews and responding to an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). After starting cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), you note pulseless electrical activity (PEA) on the monitor. The paramedic is trying to get intravenous (IV) access to give epinephrine per the protocol. She is struggling to find a good vein and she asks you what you think about going straight to intraosseous (IO) access.

**Background:**
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical emergency with a global survival rate to discharge often below 10%, necessitating rapid interventions to improve outcomes. Epinephrine is a key treatment in OHCA management, shown to enhance short-term outcomes like return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), though its effects on long-term survival and neurological outcomes remain uncertain, as highlighted by studies such as PARAMEDIC-2. The method of vascular access for epinephrine administration is crucial, with intravenous (IV) access being standard but sometimes difficult to secure in the field, prompting the use of intraosseous (IO) access as an alternative. Despite limited research comparing the efficacy of IO versus IV administration regarding survival and neurological outcomes, recent trials are investigating whether IO access offers advantages over IV, with the proximal tibial site commonly used by EMS providers. The optimal method of epinephrine delivery in OHCA remains under investigation.

**Clinical Point:**
Should we use an IV first or an IO first approach to deliver epinephrine to adult patients with an OHCA?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There does not seem to be a meaningful difference between IV or IO access in adults with OHCAs, and it might not be important because we lack high-quality evidence that epinephrine provides a patient-oriented outcome.

**Case Resolution:**
You tell the paramedic IV or IO seem to have similar outcomes, and it’s the dealer’s choice.

**Clinical Application:**
It is reasonable to choose either IV or IO access in adult OHCAs. However, we should not let issues that haven’t been shown to work (access, epinephrine, or airway) distract us from providing high-quality CPR and early defibrillation.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Hopefully, you achieve not only ROSC, but also good a neurologic outcome and we can tell the patient they received excellent prehospital care.
