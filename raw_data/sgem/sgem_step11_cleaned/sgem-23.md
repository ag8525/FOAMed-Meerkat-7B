**Summary:**
A 28-year-old woman with a history of ovarian cysts presents with right lower quadrant pain, stable vitals, and a negative pregnancy test, but without available imaging overnight. The case highlights the challenge of diagnosing appendicitis, a common cause of malpractice litigation, without immediate imaging. A study by Ashdown et al. suggests that pain over speed bumps may be a useful diagnostic tool for appendicitis, with a sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 30%. This finding, while not generalizable to pediatric populations, suggests that inquiring about pain during a bumpy car ride could aid in clinical assessment, especially when imaging is unavailable. The study emphasizes the potential utility of this simple diagnostic Point in emergency settings.

**Case Scenario:**
28-year-old woman presents to the emergency department at 2am with steadily increasing right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain. She has a past medical history of ovarian cysts. Her vital signs are stable, afebrile and tender over the RLQ. The blood work is unremarkable and specifically her pregnancy test is negative. Ultrasound and CT scan are not available overnight. What is your disposition and management of this patient?

**Background:**
Undifferentiated abdominal pain is a prevalent and high-risk condition, accounting for approximately 7% of emergency department visits, with acute appendicitis being a significant concern due to its association with malpractice litigation, particularly in children aged 6 to 17. Missed diagnoses of appendicitis contribute to 10% of all closed malpractice cases. The lifetime incidence of acute appendicitis is 8.6% in males and 6.7% in females, with appendectomy rates of 12% for males and 23.1% for females. The negative laparotomy rate ranges from 10-20%, and appendectomy complications occur in 4-13% of cases. Imaging every patient with lower abdominal pain to rule out appendicitis is impractical, highlighting the need for careful clinical evaluation.

**Point:**
Does a bumpy car ride predict appendicitis?

**BEEM Bottom Line:**
Perhaps we should ask our patients if it was a bumpy ride to the ED and did the bumps hurt?
