**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) in the management of gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, specifically evaluating its efficacy in reducing mortality. The HALT-IT trial, a large randomized controlled trial, found no significant reduction in mortality from GI bleeding with TXA compared to placebo, with a primary outcome of 3.7% mortality in the TXA group versus 3.8% in the placebo group. Secondary outcomes also showed no significant benefit, and there was a noted increase in venous thromboembolism risk with TXA. The trial's findings contrast with earlier meta-analyses suggesting a mortality benefit, highlighting the limitations of smaller, methodologically flawed studies. The authors conclude that TXA should not be used for GI bleeding outside of a trial setting, and the focus should remain on resuscitation and definitive hemorrhage control, such as endoscopy.

**Case:**
A 58-year-old man presents with hypotension, tachycardia, and pallor. He vomits a large amount of bloody emesis and has epigastric discomfort. He is not taking any anti-coagulants. He remains hemodynamically unstable despite initial resuscitation and has another episode of hematemesis in front of you. While waiting for your consultant to Point the phone, you consider treating him with tranexamic acid (TXA), but wonder if it will prevent death from gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding.

**Background:**
Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an anti-fibrinolytic agent that inhibits the breakdown of blood clots and has shown varied efficacy across different clinical scenarios. In the CRASH-2 trial, TXA provided a 1.5% absolute mortality benefit in adult trauma patients compared to placebo, and it appears to improve outcomes in epistaxis. However, TXA did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit in the WOMAN Trial for postpartum hemorrhage, nor in cases of hemorrhagic stroke or traumatic intracranial hemorrhage as per the CRASH-3 trial. A Cochrane review and meta-analysis of eight smaller trials suggested a 40% reduction in all-cause mortality for gastrointestinal bleeding, but this finding was sensitive to biases and the quality of the included trials, as the mortality benefit was not observed when accounting for missing data or limiting to trials with low attrition bias.

**Clinical Point:**
Does treatment with TXA reduce the mortality of patients with upper or lower GI bleeds?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The latest evidence does not support the use of TXA in GI bleeds.

**Case Resolution:**
You decide not to give TXA based on the results of HALT-IT. You continue to resuscitate the patient and organize for urgent endoscopy. He has two esophageal varices that are successfully ligated.

**Clinical Application:**
When it comes to GI bleeding, it is time to put aside TXA from your treatment toolbox and focus on basic volume resuscitation while liaising with your consultants for definitive hemorrhage control.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We have no specific medication that directly stops the bleeding, but we will stabilize you with blood transfusions to prepare you for definitive treatment with endoscopy.
