**Summary:**
In the case of a 43-year-old woman presenting with chest pain, the document discusses the use of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) assays in ruling out acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Despite the high negative predictive value (NPV) of hs-cTn tests, a single normal hs-cTn level at presentation should not be solely relied upon to exclude AMI, as 6%-23% of adjudicated AMI cases had normal initial hs-cTn levels. The document emphasizes the need for serial hs-cTn measurements, at least 3 hours apart, to detect the rise and/or fall indicative of AMI, as per the 3rd Universal Definition of AMI. The study highlights the variability in sensitivity and NPV among different hs-cTn assays and underscores the importance of considering clinical context and follow-up in decision-making. The case resolution involves shared decision-making and discharge with follow-up, given the low estimated risk of AMI.

**Case Scenario:**
43-year-old woman comes in complaining of chest pain for about an hour. The chest pain was at rest with no radiation of the pain. She takes no medications and medical history includes two c-sections. She is Well’s Criteria low and PERC Rule negative. Physical examination and ECG are non-diagnostic.

**Point:**
Can a single high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) rule out an acute myocardial infarction?

**Background:**
Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a critical condition characterized by myocardial necrosis due to ischemia, with significant morbidity and mortality implications. Historically, various cardiac biomarkers have been used for diagnosis, including total creatine kinase (CK), CK-MB, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), myoglobin, and troponin. Since the late 1990s, troponin has become the preferred marker due to its superior sensitivity and specificity. The European Society of Cardiology and the American College of Cardiology redefined myocardial necrosis in 2000, establishing cardiac troponin (cTn) assays as the primary diagnostic tool for AMI, with a cutoff at the 99th percentile of a healthy population. The 3rd Universal Definition of AMI, published in 2012, requires a rise and/or fall in cTn levels, with at least one value above the 99th percentile, alongside clinical evidence such as ischemic symptoms or ECG changes. Despite the utility of cTn, its assays can take several hours to detect changes, complicating the timely diagnosis of non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (Non-STEMI) and contributing to resource use and patient anxiety. The definition of high-sensitivity cTn (hs-cTn) assays remains under discussion, with implications for AMI prevalence and early detection.

**BEEM Bottom Line:**
One is a lonely number. A single negative hs-cTn should not be used to rule out acute myocardial infarction.

**Case Resolution:**
You order a hs-cTn on this 43-year-old woman when she arrives at the emergency department which is normal. A repeat hs-cTn and ECG are performed three hours later which are also normal. You discuss the results with her and estimate her chance of AMI in the next 30 days of 1/250 based on the NNT. Shared decision-making takes place and you discharge her home with a diagnosis of chest pain NYD and ask her to follow up with her primary care physician in the next week.
