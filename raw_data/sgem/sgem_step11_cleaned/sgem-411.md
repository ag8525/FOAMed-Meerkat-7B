**Summary:**
The document discusses the evolution of pre-hospital spinal motion restriction (SMR) practices from 2009 to 2020, highlighting a shift from routine spinal immobilization using cervical collars and backboards to more selective SMR approaches. This change was driven by the recognition of adverse effects and limited benefits of traditional immobilization methods. The study by McDonald et al. reveals a significant decrease in the use of SMR over time, with a notable protocol change in 2016 allowing for collar-only treatment in low-risk scenarios, which did not significantly alter SMR rates. The study also notes changes in patient demographics and practice characteristics, such as increased use of semi-fowlers positioning and variations in collar sizes. The findings emphasize the need for ongoing evaluation of SMR practices to optimize patient care and minimize harm, with local guidelines advising the use of cervical collars and semi-fowlers positioning for ambulatory patients with potential cervical spine injuries.

**Case:**
A 42-year-old is struck in the face by a slowly moving I-beam at work. He has a brief loss of consciousness (LOC) and then awakens and is ambulatory on scene. Emergency Medical Services (EMS) is called and on arrival the patient is walking but has obvious facial trauma and is complaining of some neck pain. He has midline neck tenderness but no limb numbness or paresthesia. As an EMS crew member, you are tasked with deciding what method of spinal motion restriction to use.

**Background:**
Head injuries, including concussions, are frequently addressed in emergency and pre-hospital care, with guidelines such as the Canadian CT Head Rules aiding in assessment. Spinal injuries are another critical focus, with traditional spinal immobilization (SI) methods, such as cervical spine collars and rigid backboards, evolving into spinal motion restriction (SMR) techniques. SMR involves more selective use of cervical collars and alternative positioning methods due to the recognition of adverse effects and limited benefits of traditional immobilization. The effectiveness of various devices and procedures in reducing harmful spinal motion remains unclear, with research indicating a shift from long backboards to collar-only treatments. However, concerns persist regarding under-treatment and inconsistent practices, as some patients meeting criteria for precautions receive inadequate care. Despite no observed increase in cervical spine injury diagnoses, optimizing patient protection while minimizing harm is essential for advancing acute spinal injury management standards.

**Clinical Point:**
How has the rate of pre-hospital spinal immobilization/spinal motion restriction changed from 2009 to 2020?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Patterns of spinal motion restriction are changing over time with reduced use of SMR and changing patient and practice characteristics.

**Case Resolution:**
You decide to place the patient in a cervical collar and transport lying at 30 degrees in semi-fowlers position, corresponding to local guidelines for an ambulatory patient with a potential c-spine injury.

**Clinical Application:**
The exact type and benefits of spinal immobilization/spinal motion restriction have not been fully elucidated at this point and you should follow local pre-hospital practices.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have had head trauma and there is a risk of having a bony neck injury. This risk is lower because you are ambulatory on scene, but to be cautious, we are going to place you in a cervical collar, limit your neck range of motion and transport you to hospital for further assessment as per our current protocol.
