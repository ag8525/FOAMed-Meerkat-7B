**Summary:**
In the context of septic shock management, the document discusses the comparison between early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) and usual care, highlighting findings from the ProMISe trial. The trial concluded that EGDT, which involves a strict protocol including continuous central venous oxygenation monitoring, did not improve outcomes compared to usual care, which includes early recognition, fluid resuscitation, lactate screening, and antibiotic administration. The study found no significant difference in 90-day mortality or secondary outcomes such as organ support and quality of life, while EGDT incurred higher costs. The results align with previous trials (ProCESS and ARISE), suggesting that invasive EGDT is unnecessary when usual care is effectively implemented. The clinical application emphasizes early intervention with fluids and antibiotics, reserving central line placement for volume-refractory shock.

**Case:**
You see a 62 year-old man sent from a nursing home with a three day history of a productive cough, intermittent fevers and today is a bit confused. The transfer notes include a history of congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, gout, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and mild dementia. His emergency department vitals are as follows: Temperature 39.1C, heart rate 103, blood pressure 115/100, respiratory rate 26, oxygen saturation is 92% on room air, and capillary blood sugar is normal.

**Point:**
Does an emergency department patient with septic shock need aggressive EGDT or is "usual" resuscitation just as good?

**Background:**
Sepsis is a clinical syndrome resulting from severe infection, characterized by systemic inflammation that can lead to multiple organ dysfunction. It is clinically significant due to its potential to progress to severe sepsis and septic shock, conditions associated with high morbidity and mortality. The diagnosis of sepsis requires the presence of at least two Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria—such as abnormal temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, or white blood cell count—alongside an infection. Severe sepsis involves sepsis with hypotension and end organ failure, including symptoms like hypoxia, renal and hepatic failure, coagulopathy, and elevated lactate levels. Septic shock is defined as severe sepsis with persistent hypotension despite fluid resuscitation or a lactate level exceeding 4 mmol/l.

**Case Resolution:**
Having recognized the sepsis potential of this patient and confirming a high lactate, you initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics for what is most likely a clinical pneumonia. You give aggressive fluid resuscitation with IV normal saline or ringers lactate. Then call your consultant to arrange admission to the intensive care unit.

**Clinical Application:**
If it is 02:00 and you are working in a single coverage emergency department you can start with IV fluids, antibiotics and lactate measurement. If you have volume refractory shock requiring vasopressors, then most guidelines support administering them through a central line. Given that about half of the usual care group received central lines within about 2 hours after being randomized indicates that emergency department providers are both decisive and capable when they feel central lines are needed.

**What Do I Tell Patients?**
You have a serious infection. We are going to give you lots of intravenous fluids, start antibiotics, measure something called a lactate level that can help us tell how sick you are and if you are responding to treatment and we are going to admit you to hospital.
