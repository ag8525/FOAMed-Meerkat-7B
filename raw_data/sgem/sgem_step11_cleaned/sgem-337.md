**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of recurrent, low-risk chest pain in the emergency department (ED), emphasizing the use of the GRADE system to develop guidelines for emergency medicine. It highlights that in patients with recurrent chest pain and recent normal cardiac investigations, a single high-sensitivity troponin test is often sufficient to exclude acute coronary syndrome (ACS) within 30 days, especially if a coronary CT angiogram (CCTA) within the past two years showed no coronary stenosis. The guidelines advise against routine repeat stress testing within 12 months of a normal result and suggest expedited outpatient follow-up rather than inpatient admission for patients with non-obstructive coronary artery disease. Additionally, the document recommends screening for anxiety and depression, as these may contribute to recurrent ED visits, and suggests mental health referrals to potentially reduce healthcare utilization. The guidelines underscore the need for further research due to the limited direct evidence available for this patient population.

**Case:**
You are seeing a patient who has returned to the emergency department (ED) with recurrent chest pain. It is their fourth visit in the last twelve months. He has had his chest pain for approximately three to four hours now and is not classic for ischemic symptomatology. His initial high-sensitivity (hs) troponin is negative, and his ECG is unremarkable. In reviewing his records, you note that he has undergone significant cardiac testing in the recent past, which was unremarkable. This included a normal exercise stress test and CCTA 18 months ago. You also note that he has an underlying anxiety disorder, which is being treated and followed by his family physician.

**Background:**
The SAEM GRACE initiative employs the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system to develop evidence-based guidelines for emergency medicine (EM) physicians, addressing clinical issues not extensively covered in EM research. The initiative involved selecting and systematically reviewing eight key clinical questions, with evidence rated and recommendations formulated using the GRADE Evidence-to-Decision framework. The process included training in GRADE methodology, which was disseminated among participants, many of whom were new to guideline development. After two years, the initiative produced a comprehensive document addressing diagnostic challenges in patients with recurrent chest pain and recent normal cardiac investigations, aiming to optimize testing and reduce emergency department length of stay without increasing the risk of major adverse coronary events within 30 days.

**Clinical Point:**
What are the recommendations for managing patients with recurrent, low-risk chest pain in the ED?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It all depends. The strongest evidence from GRACE-1 is recommendation #6. There is moderate level of evidence that ACS can be excluded in adult patients with recurrent, low-risk chest pain using a single hs-troponin below a validated threshold without further diagnostic testing in patients who have a CCTA within the past two years showing no coronary stenosis.

**Case Resolution:**
After discussing the likelihood of low-risk ACS in this patient’s recurrent chest pain, acknowledgement of some anxiety drivers for recurrent ED visits, and even lower risk of 30-day MACE given a normal exercise stress test and CCTA within the past two years, you both agree to discharge without further hs-troponin/other testing, and he will follow-up with his cardiologist within a few days.

**Clinical Application:**
The application of this GRACE-1 clinical practice guideline depends on many factors. This includes: where you work, personal risk tolerance, patient values/preferences/risk tolerances, regional practices/testing capabilities, which troponin test you have in your hospital, and other things.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You can be safely discharged home after one normal blood test. This blood test is called a high-sensitivity troponin. This is because you had a normal CCTA within the last two years. Your risk of having something bad happen in the next month because of your heart is very low. You should follow-up with your family physician in the next week. If you have more chest pain, get short of breath, or are worried please come back to the ED.
