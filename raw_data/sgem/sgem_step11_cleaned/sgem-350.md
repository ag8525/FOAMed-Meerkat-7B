**Summary:**
In the context of in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), a study evaluated the efficacy of a vasopressin and methylprednisolone protocol (VSE) compared to placebo in achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The trial demonstrated a statistically significant increase in ROSC with VSE, but this did not translate into improved long-term survival or favorable neurologic outcomes. Despite the potential for VSE to enhance immediate resuscitation success, the lack of impact on meaningful long-term outcomes raises questions about its routine use. The study highlights the ongoing challenge in cardiac arrest management of balancing immediate resuscitation success with long-term quality of life, emphasizing the need for further high-quality research to guide clinical practice.

**Case:**
A code blue is called for a 71-year-old male in-patient that is boarding in the emergency department (ED). He had been admitted the night before for a new diagnosis of rapid atrial fibrillation. He has a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type-2 diabetes. His medications include a beta-blocker, statin, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACE-I), metformin, ASA and direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC). You arrive and see that the Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) algorithm is being followed for adult cardiac arrest patients with pulseless electrical activity (PEA). Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is in progress. The monitor shows a non-shockable rhythm. Epinephrine is provided and you quickly place an advanced airway. A second dose of epinephrine is given, and you start to think about reversible causes and your next steps for in-hospital cardiac arrests (IHCA).

**Background:**
In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is a critical medical condition characterized by the cessation of cardiac activity within a hospital setting, necessitating immediate intervention to restore circulation and prevent mortality. A randomized trial conducted in Greek tertiary hospitals indicated that a protocol combining vasopressin, steroids, and epinephrine (VSE) improved return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and survival to hospital discharge with favorable neurological outcomes compared to epinephrine alone. However, these findings require further validation before altering existing IHCA protocols. Corticosteroids have been proposed as a therapeutic option in cardiac arrest, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggesting improved ROSC and discharge survival, though evidence remains limited by the lack of robust randomized controlled trials. The American Heart Association's Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) guidelines previously included vasopressin but now recommend epinephrine monotherapy. Current research trends emphasize the importance of not only achieving ROSC but also ensuring good neurological outcomes, prompting reevaluation of epinephrine's role and consideration of the VSE protocol in cardiac arrest management.

**Clinical Point:**
Does adding a combination of vasopressin and methylprednisolone increase the chance of achieving ROSC in cardiac arrest?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The routine use of vasopressin and steroids in addition to epinephrine cannot be recommended based on the available evidence in patients with in-hospital cardiac arrests.

**Case Resolution:**
Thinking about the causes of PEA arrest you obtain a stat glucometer reading and find out this man is hypoglycemic. An amp of D50 is given which aborts the arrest. Further blood work is obtained and reported showing he is also mildly acidotic and hyperkalemic. He is moved to a higher level of care in the hospital and the internist takes over his care to address his ongoing glucose, pH, and electrolyte management.

**Clinical Application:**
We still do not have high-quality, clinically relevant information to support the use of vasopressin, steroid, and epinephrine (VSE) protocol in patients with IHCA. That does not mean VSE should never be used and it will depend also on the clinicians’ judgment. We are now left with a difficult choice, deciding on which drugs can help us resuscitate someone while preserving their quality of life, and more and more it seems these goals are at odds with each other.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Hopefully, you’ll say "you probably don’t remember me, and you might not believe me when I tell you this…but you had a cardiac arrest. It’s very nice to be able to speak with you."
