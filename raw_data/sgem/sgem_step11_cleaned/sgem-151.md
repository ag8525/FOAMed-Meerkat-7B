**Summary:**
The document discusses the evaluation and management of low-risk chest pain in the emergency department (ED), focusing on the use of the HEART Pathway to identify patients safe for early discharge without objective cardiac testing. The HEART Pathway combines the HEART Score with 0- and 3-hour cardiac troponin testing to stratify risk and has shown potential to reduce unnecessary testing, increase early discharge rates, and decrease length of stay without increasing the risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) at 30 days. The study highlights that while the HEART Pathway can safely decrease resource use in low-risk patients, the authors caution that the study was not powered to definitively confirm a zero MACE rate. The clinical application suggests that despite the study's limitations, the HEART Score is effective in managing low-risk chest pain, with a 30-day MACE rate of less than 2%. The case resolution involves discussing with the patient the low risk of ACS based on negative ECG and troponin results, offering outpatient follow-up as a safe alternative to immediate provocative testing.

**Case:**
A 40 year-old male arrives to the emergency department via EMS with substernal chest pain that has lasted for three days without any relief. He denies shortness of breath, radiation, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis, or palpitations. He has no past medical history, does not smoke, but states his father died of a myocardial infarction at the age of 70, which is why this chest pain concerned him. He runs four times a week without any difficulty, but has had some increased stress at his workplace. Your evaluation in the emergency department shows an ECG with normal sinus rhythm without any other abnormalities and cardiac troponin testing was negative both at arrival and at three hours after arrival. You go to speak to your patient about objective/provocative testing. What will your conversation be?

**Background:**
Chest pain is a common complaint in emergency departments in the United States, with 8 to 10 million cases annually, leading to extensive testing to avoid missing acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Despite this, fewer than 10% of these patients are diagnosed with ACS, resulting in significant healthcare costs and resource use, particularly among low-risk patients who have an ACS rate of less than 2%. The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommends serial cardiac markers and provocative testing for suspected ACS, but this can lead to unnecessary downstream testing and potential harm due to false positives. Traditional risk scores like TIMI and GRACE are not designed for initial ACS diagnosis in emergency settings. The HEART Score, developed for emergency department use, stratifies patients based on the risk of a Major Adverse Cardiac Event (MACE) within six weeks, showing a high negative predictive value of over 98% for MACE, though a 2% risk remains concerning for some providers.

**Clinical Point:**
Does the HEART Pathway identify ED patients with acute chest pain who are safe for early ED discharge without objective cardiac testing?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The HEART Pathway appears to have the potential to safely decrease objective cardiac testing, increase early discharge rates and cut median length of stay in low risk chest pain patients presenting to the ED with suspicion of ACS.

**Case Resolution:**
You present the information to the patient letting him know that his ECG does not have any ischemic abnormalities, he is considered a low risk patient based on his HEART Score, and troponin testing at zero and three hours were both negative. At this point you offer him outpatient follow up with his primary care physician vs. objective provocative testing. The patient states the reason he came to the ED is he wanted to make sure he wasn’t having a heart attack and feels comfortable following up with his primary care physician as an outpatient and is discharged home.

**Clinical Application:**
Evaluation of low risk chest pain does not live in the bubble of this one study. Even though this study was not powered to detect the patient oriented outcome of MACE. There have been many studies looking at the use of the HEART Score in the evaluation of chest pain and the sum of these studies shows that we can do amazingly well in a low risk patient with a 30d MACE rate of <2%.
- **Jellema 2013:** The results of the entire population of HEART Score 0 – 3 were not included in this manuscript making it hard to know what the exact MACE rate was in this population. There was a subcohort of patients that were admitted to the hospital, for a second troponin test, that were included. Using this population creates a huge selection bias in that although these patients were HEART Scores of 0 – 3, they were admitted because they were thought to be higher risk.
- **Marcoon 2013:** The definitions of patient history and ECG used in Marcoon’s paper are different than the original HEART Score definitions.
  - **History:** Patients with 2 elements typical for ACS (oppressive, burning, left sided, substernal chest pain, radiation, diaphoresis, vomiting, short of breath) were scored as atypical instead of intermediate/suspicious and patients with 3-4 elements typical for ACS were scored as intermediate instead of highly suspicious.
  - **ECG:** Only ECGs showing ST elevations were scored as a 2, while t-wave inversions/ST depression were scored as a 1, and nonspecific changes were scored as a 0.  Again these definitions would result in underscoring of the ECGs.
