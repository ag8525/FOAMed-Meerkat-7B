**Summary:**
The document discusses sex-based disparities in the treatment of chlamydia and gonorrhea in US emergency departments, highlighting that women are significantly more likely to be under-treated compared to men, who are often over-treated. This systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 studies involving 32,592 patients reveals that females are 3.5 times more likely to receive inadequate empiric antibiotic treatment despite CDC guidelines recommending treatment based on clinical suspicion. The findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to address these disparities and improve treatment accuracy. The case resolution involves empiric treatment of a 24-year-old woman with suspected chlamydia, adhering to CDC guidelines, and underscores the importance of guideline-based protocols to ensure equitable care across all patient demographics.

**Case:**
A 24-year-old woman presents to the emergency department (ED) with a two-day history of dysuria, lower abdominal discomfort, and abnormal vaginal discharge. She is sexually active with multiple male partners and does not consistently use condoms. A urine nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) is sent, and the patient is clinically diagnosed with a possible sexually transmitted infection (STI). She is not in acute distress, has no fever, and requests discharge after symptom control.

**Background:**
Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as chlamydia and gonorrhea are significant public health issues in the United States, particularly affecting young adults. Emergency departments (EDs) play a crucial role in STI screening and treatment, yet disparities in treatment practices based on patient sex have been observed, raising concerns about equitable care. Women face greater risks from untreated STIs, including pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, and infertility, but may experience delays or inadequacies in treatment compared to men, who often receive expedited care. This disparity may be due to differences in clinical presentation, provider bias, and systemic barriers like follow-up challenges or diagnostic uncertainty. Both chlamydia and gonorrhea can be symptomatic or asymptomatic, complicating diagnosis and treatment. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends empiric treatment when there is high clinical suspicion, especially if follow-up is uncertain, but the consistent application of these guidelines across sexes is unclear.

**Clinical Point:**
Are there sex-based disparities in the treatment of chlamydia and gonorrhea among adults presenting to US emergency departments?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There is a high amount of UNDER-treatment (women>men) and OVER-treatment (men>women) in patients clinically diagnosed with chlamydia or gonorrhea in US EDs.

**Case Resolution:**
You diagnose the 24-year-old patient with suspected chlamydia based on symptoms and epidemiologic risk. Despite a pending confirmatory test, the ED team adheres to CDC guidelines and provides empiric treatment. The patient is counseled on safer sex practices and linked to local STI follow-up services.

**Clinical Application:**
EDs should consider guideline-based STI treatment protocols across all patient demographics. Clinical decision support and workflow optimization may help mitigate disparities and improve timely care for all patients.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
I would do some shared decision making and say something like…You may have a sexually transmitted infection. If you’re not sure you will be able to come back to the ED for treatment or to get your results, then it’s reasonable to treat you today with antibiotics to make sure we don’t miss an infection.
