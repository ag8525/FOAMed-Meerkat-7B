**Summary:**
In the management of new-onset atrial fibrillation, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients without myocardial ischemia, both immediate cardioversion and a delayed "wait-and-see" approach are viable strategies. The study by Pluymaeker et al. demonstrated that delayed cardioversion, involving initial rate control and potential next-day cardioversion, was non-inferior to immediate cardioversion in achieving sinus rhythm at four weeks, with 91% and 94% success rates, respectively. The delayed approach allows for spontaneous conversion in a significant number of cases, reducing the need for immediate intervention. Both strategies require consideration of anticoagulation based on stroke risk, and shared decision-making with patients is crucial to tailor the approach to individual preferences and logistical considerations. The study highlights the importance of flexibility in management strategies, with both approaches achieving high rates of sinus rhythm and low complication rates.

**Case:**
A 62-year-old woman with a history of hypertension presents with four hours of palpitations, described as a racing heart. Her vital signs: BP 148/90, HR 135, RR 16, T 37oC, O2 Sat 96%. Lungs are clear and cardiac exam shows a tachycardic and irregularly irregular rhythm without murmur. The ECG shows atrial fibrillation without ischemic changes. How do you proceed?

**Background:**
New-onset atrial fibrillation is a frequent condition encountered in emergency departments, characterized by an irregular and often rapid heart rate that can lead to poor blood flow. Clinically significant due to its prevalence and potential complications, management strategies vary, with practitioners divided between rate-control and rhythm-control approaches, the latter involving electrical or pharmacological cardioversion. In the United States, patients are typically admitted for rate control with cardiology determining subsequent cardioversion, whereas in Canada, patients are often cardioverted and discharged. A 2010 cohort study by Dr. Ian Stiell indicated that 92% of patients were electrically cardioverted, with 97% discharged home and 93% in sinus rhythm at discharge. Recent changes in the U.S. include a new atrial fibrillation algorithm that reduced hospital admissions from 80% to 67% and increased cardioversion from 17% to 21%, highlighting regional differences in management practices.

**Clinical Point:**
In adult patients who present with hemodynamically stable, symptomatic, recent-onset atrial fibrillation without signs of myocardial ischemia, is a wait-and-see approach, inferior to an immediate cardioversion strategy.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Both delayed and early cardioversion of acute onset atrial fibrillation achieve high rates of sinus rhythm in their patients at the time of a 4-week follow up.

**Clinical Application:**
New-onset atrial fibrillation is a common occurrence. Patients can be managed either with immediate cardioversion or with rate-control and a recheck the next day and cardioversion at that time if they are in the minority who remain in atrial fibrillation. Immediate cardioversion obviates the need for a visit the next day, which may be unavailable in many systems and inconvenient for patients. A wait-and-see approach, however, obviates the need for many cardioversions.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Because both approaches are reasonable you do some shared decision making. You tell your patient that returning them to normal heart rhythm is appropriate and could be achieved either with immediate cardioversion or during a next-day visit—if one can be arranged—and the latter approach would also give them the chance to spontaneously convert to a normal rhythm, which likely happens about two-thirds of the time. If they choose this approach, they will need to stay for a time to get their symptoms and heart rate controlled. Either way, for patients at high risk of stroke based on a CHA2DS2-VASc score >2, you begin anticoagulation. You tell your patient that regardless of the approach chosen, there’s no need to admit them to the hospital.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient chooses immediate cardioversion, which is successfully performed using electrical cardioversion. Given her CHA2DS2-VASc score of 2, you begin a DOAC and discharge her from the ED 1.5 hours after arrival.
