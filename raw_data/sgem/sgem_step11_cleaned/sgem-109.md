**Summary:**
In the case of a 28-year-old male with severe chest trauma and massive hemorrhage, the clinical management involved damage control resuscitation, including permissive hypotension, source control, and a massive transfusion protocol. The PROPPR trial compared transfusion strategies of plasma, platelets, and red blood cells in 1:1:1 versus 1:1:2 ratios, finding no significant difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality, though the 1:1:1 group achieved better hemostasis and had fewer deaths from exsanguination within 24 hours. Despite increased use of plasma and platelets in the 1:1:1 group, no additional safety concerns were identified. The study suggests that while a 1:1:1 strategy may not significantly reduce mortality compared to 1:1:2, it could improve early hemostasis outcomes, emphasizing the need for tailored local protocols in massive transfusion scenarios.

**Case:**
A 28-year-old male is involved in an altercation and shot multiple times in the chest, presenting to the emergency department. His initial vital signs include a BP of 72/46, HR 140, RR 30, O2 sat 89% on NRB, and a temp of 98.7F. You intubate the patient successfully, begin intravenous fluids, and place bilateral chest tubes with significant blood return from the right chest tube. Due to the patient's blood loss, you initiate damage control resuscitation including permissive hypotension, source control of bleeding, and a massive transfusion protocol.

**Background:**
Trauma is the leading cause of death for individuals aged 1 to 44 years in the United States and ranks as the third leading cause of death overall, with 20 to 40% of trauma-related fatalities occurring post-hospital admission due to massive hemorrhage. Despite the absence of large, multi-center, randomized clinical trials with survival as a primary endpoint to guide optimal trauma resuscitation practices, the Prospective Observational Multicenter Major Trauma Transfusion (PROMMT) Trial indicated that clinicians often use a transfusion ratio of 1:1:1 or 1:1:2 for plasma, platelets, and red blood cells. This approach, particularly the early transfusion of plasma and platelets, has been associated with improved survival within the first six hours after hospital admission.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the effectiveness and safety of transfusing adult patients with severe trauma and major bleeding using plasma, platelets, and red blood cells in a 1:1:1 ratio vs. with a 1:1:2 ratio?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
A 1:1:1 transfusion strategy is a reasonable approach to adult patients who require a massive transfusion and seems to achieve more hemostasis and less death from exsanguination at 24 hours without increased complications.

**Case Resolution:**
Unfortunately, our patient did not survive. He was started on a massive transfusion protocol of 1:1:1 and had an emergency department thoracotomy performed, but ultimately all the bleeding could not be stopped, and the patient coded.

**Clinical Application:**
For adult patients who require massive transfusions, a 1:1:1 is not superior to a 1:1:2 strategy. The PROPPR data suggests giving platelets earlier and in higher ratios. Local protocols will need to be developed using available resources and expertise to guide care of these critically ill patients.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your family member has been shot. They are critically injured. There is a lot of bleeding, and they require a massive transfusion. The US Department of Defense has come up with a treatment called damage control resuscitation. It involves early transfusion of different types of blood products (platelet cells that help stop bleeding, red blood cells that carry oxygen to the body, and plasma that fills up their tank). We are going to use this balanced approach to try and save their life.
