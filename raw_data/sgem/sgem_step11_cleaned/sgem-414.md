**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of mild to moderate diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in a 28-year-old male with type 1 diabetes, highlighting the potential use of the SQuID protocol, which involves fast-acting subcutaneous insulin instead of traditional insulin drips typically requiring ICU admission. The study referenced is a quasi-experimental design conducted at an urban academic hospital, showing that the SQuID protocol can reduce emergency department (ED) length of stay without compromising safety, as evidenced by similar rates of hypoglycemia requiring rescue dextrose compared to traditional methods. The study's limitations include its non-randomized nature, small sample size, and exclusion of patients with certain comorbidities, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. The authors conclude that subcutaneous insulin is a viable alternative for managing mild to moderate DKA outside the ICU, though further research is needed to confirm these findings across different healthcare settings.

**Case:**
A 28-year-old male with a history of type-1 diabetes mellitus presents to the emergency department (ED) with an increase in thirst and lightheadedness. He is otherwise healthy. Blood glucose in triage is 489 mg/dl (27.2 mmol/L). Venous blood gas (VBG) shows an acidosis with a pH of 7.21. Electrolytes show a gap of 21. The patient’s symptoms begin to improve after initial intravenous (IV) fluid administration of one litre of 0.9% saline. The patient states he has had multiple "diabetic emergencies" in the past and usually ends up in the intensive care unit (ICU) on a drip. He is wondering, "Hey doc, do I have to go back to the ICU strapped to an IV pole?" The flow nurse has similar questions for you and wants to know if she should clear out a bed in the critical care bay so that the patient can have appropriate nursing requirements for an insulin infusion. Your resident is eager to go ahead and sign off on the diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) insulin order set and the ICU attending’s "Spidey senses" are going off. They are on the phone asking you if you already have another admission for them on this busy day. However, the ICU is full and the patient will likely be boarding in your ED for a bit before coming upstairs. Just as all this is happening, you notice how the waiting room is filling up and you can hear the sirens of approaching ambulances becoming louder. You take a deep breath, and you think to yourself…let the squid games begin.

**Background:**
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a prevalent and potentially life-threatening condition primarily affecting individuals with type 1 diabetes, accounting for approximately 8.9 emergency department visits per 1000 adults with diabetes and resulting in over 500,000 hospital days annually, with costs exceeding $5 billion. Standard management typically involves an insulin drip and intensive care unit (ICU) level care, which can extend emergency department length of stay, particularly in busy hospitals or during pandemics. A notable study by Dr. Nathan Kuppermann indicated that the type or speed of intravenous fluid infusion does not significantly impact clinical outcomes in pediatric DKA. Emerging evidence over the past two decades, including a 2016 Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis, suggests that fast-acting subcutaneous insulin analogs may be a viable treatment for mild to moderate DKA, potentially reducing the need for insulin drips and ICU care, thereby decreasing emergency department length of stay and alleviating resource constraints.

**Clinical Point:**
Can a patient with mild to moderate severity DKA be safely managed with subcutaneous fast-acting insulin analogs on a non-ICU floor with blood glucose monitoring every two hours, and ultimately decrease ED length of stay?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Managing patients with mild to moderate DKA outside of an ICU with fast-acting subcutaneous insulin seems like a potential alternative while more data is needed from other sites to address efficacy and safety.
