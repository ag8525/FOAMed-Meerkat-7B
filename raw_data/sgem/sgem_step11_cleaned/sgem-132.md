**Summary:**
A four-year-old boy presents with suspected otitis media with effusion (OME), characterized by fluid behind the tympanic membrane without acute infection signs. OME is a prevalent chronic condition in children, often leading to conductive hearing loss and potential developmental issues if untreated. Diagnostic methods include otoscopy, pneumatic otoscopy, tympanometry, and pure tone audiometry. Treatment options such as antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, and steroids show limited efficacy, while grommets and nasal balloon devices offer some benefit but require further research. A recent study on nasal balloon autoinflation in children aged 4-11 years showed feasibility and some effectiveness in symptom improvement, but the evidence is insufficient to recommend routine use due to concerns about adverse events and study limitations. The current management approach involves reassurance and monitoring, with further evaluation if effusion persists beyond a few months.

**Case:**
A four-year-old boy presents to the emergency department after being picked up from school. The mother is worried he has another ear infection. This is because the teacher thought he was having trouble hearing in class. The child completed a course of antibiotics two months ago for acute otitis media. On examination, the child looks well and has no fever. Otoscopic examination demonstrates some fluid behind the tympanic membrane.

**Background:**
Otitis media with effusion, also known as glue ear, is a condition characterized by the presence of fluid in the middle ear for more than six weeks, and it is the most common chronic condition in children, often leading to surgery. In 2004, over two million cases were diagnosed in the USA, incurring costs of approximately four billion dollars. The primary clinical concern is conductive hearing loss, which can lead to developmental difficulties if untreated. Diagnostic methods include otoscopy, pneumatic otoscopy, tympanometry, and pure tone audiometry, with tympanometry providing objective measures of tympanic membrane compliance. Treatment options have varied efficacy; antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, and steroids show limited benefit, while grommets offer temporary hearing improvement. Nasal balloon devices are low-cost and have no adverse effects, but further research is needed to confirm their efficacy.

**Clinical Point:**
Can a nasal balloon autoinflation device be a safe and effective treatment for children with otitis media with effusion?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Autoinflation devices to treat otitis media with effusion do not have enough good evidence to recommend their use at this time.

**Case Resolution:**
Provide reassurance to the mother that her son’s effusion should resolve on its own without treatment. If it persists past a few months the child should be seen by her pediatrician/primary care physician.

**Clinical Application:**
Will not change current management.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your child has fluid in the middle ear. Unfortunately, antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, and nasal steroids do not help clear this up quicker. Most children will get better without treatment and it can take a few months. If they get a fever, ear pain, or you are worried bring them back in and I will take a look. If the problem persists past a few months we will arrange pure tone audiometry testing to check on your son’s hearing.
