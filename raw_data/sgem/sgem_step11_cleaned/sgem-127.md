**Summary:**
In the context of pediatric trauma, particularly blunt torso trauma, the document discusses the challenge of identifying intra-abdominal injuries in children and the potential overuse of CT scans due to concerns about radiation exposure. A study by Mahajan et al. compared clinician suspicion against a clinical prediction rule for identifying such injuries, finding that the prediction rule had higher sensitivity but lower specificity. Despite clinicians' reasonable accuracy in predicting injuries requiring intervention, the study highlights the need for a validated clinical decision instrument to guide CT scan use and reduce unnecessary radiation exposure. The case resolution involved routine imaging and observation, leading to the safe discharge of the patient without the need for a CT scan, reflecting current practice pending further validation of predictive tools.

**Case:**
A ten-year-old girl is a restrained rear-seat passenger in a motor vehicle collision. The driver of the other car was texting and ran a red light at low city speeds, "T-boning" her vehicle on her side. There was minimal intrusion into the vehicle space. The girl presents to the emergency department via EMS with a c-spine collar and on a spine board. She is alert, appropriate and in no distress. Her exam is normal, including no evidence of abdominal wall trauma, tenderness or nausea/vomiting. The remainder of her exam is normal. Given the mechanism of the injury you are concerned about blunt abdominal trauma. You wonder if you should do a CT abdomen, but are also concerned about the risk of radiation. You heard of a study in 2013 that developed a clinical decision instrument for this very Point, but don’t know if you should use that, or trust your own 'gut feeling'.

**Background:**
Trauma is the leading cause of death in children, with blunt torso trauma contributing significantly to mortality rates. Computed tomography (CT) scanning is routinely used to diagnose intra-abdominal injuries in pediatric patients. However, concerns have been raised about the radiation exposure from CT scans and the associated risk of radiation-induced malignancies. Clinical suspicion alone has proven unreliable in accurately identifying intra-abdominal injuries in children, leading to variability and potential overutilization of abdominal CT scans, particularly in cases with a low pre-test probability of significant injuries.

**Clinical Point:**
How well do clinicians identify children suffering from blunt torso trauma who have intra-abdominal injuries compared to an established clinical prediction rule?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Clinicians appear to do reasonably well at predicting which children with blunt torso trauma require investigations for intra-abdominal injuries requiring intervention. Nothing can be said at this time about the derived clinical decision instrument.

**Case Resolution:**
Trauma labs and routine X-rays were performed including chest and c-spine – all of which were normal. The c-spine collar was removed and the 10-year-old was observed for a couple of hours, during which time she tolerated oral intake and was able to ambulate. She continued to be pain-free and is discharged home.

**Clinical Application:**
No change for now.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I’m a bit worried that your child has a significant abdominal injury. We will need to do some more tests to find out if there are internal injuries. One of the tests that can help us identify important abdominal injuries is a CT scan.
