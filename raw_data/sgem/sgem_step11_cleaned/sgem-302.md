**Summary:**
A 34-year-old male with recurrent epigastric pain was treated in the emergency department with antacid monotherapy, which improved his symptoms. The clinical focus is on the management of dyspepsia, comparing antacid monotherapy to combinations with lidocaine. A randomized double-blind trial found no significant difference in pain relief between antacid alone and combinations with lidocaine, though antacid monotherapy was more palatable and acceptable to patients. The study suggests considering a shift in practice towards using antacid monotherapy for dyspepsia in the ED, as it provides effective analgesia with fewer adverse effects and better patient acceptability.

**Case:**
A 34-year-old male presents to the emergency department with burning epigastric pain after eating two hours ago. He says he gets this from time to time but this is the worst it has ever been. He denies chest pain, shortness of breath, fever, and vomiting. His vital signs are within normal limits and his abdominal exam reveals mild epigastric and left upper quadrant tenderness with no peritonitis.

**Background:**
Epigastric pain in emergency departments is commonly managed with antacids, either alone or in combination with medications such as viscous lidocaine, antihistamines, proton pump inhibitors, or anticholinergics. In Canada, a combination of antacid and viscous lidocaine is known as a "Pink Lady," while in the US, similar treatments are referred to as a "GI Cocktail." Studies on the management of acute dyspepsia in the ED show mixed results. A single-blind study indicated that adding viscous lidocaine to antacid significantly improved pain relief compared to antacid alone. However, another single-blind RCT found no significant difference between antacid combined with benzocaine or viscous lidocaine, lacking a monotherapy comparison. A larger double-blind RCT involving 113 patients compared antacid monotherapy, antacid with an anticholinergic, and antacid with both an anticholinergic and viscous lidocaine, finding all treatments effective with no significant difference in pain relief. Berman et al concluded that antacid monotherapy is recommended.

**Clinical Point:**
Is antacid monotherapy more effective in relieving epigastric pain than in combination with lidocaine?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Consider using antacid monotherapy in place of lidocaine/antacid combination therapy for patients with dyspepsia.

**Case Resolution:**
You give your patient 20 mL of antacid and his epigastric pain improves. You suggest he try antacids in the future if he has recurrent postprandial pain and follow-up with his family physician.

**Clinical Application:**
Give antacid monotherapy for dyspepsia in the emergency department.

**What Do I Tell the Patient:**
We have given you some medication to treat your stomach pain. This is usually related to eating and sometimes to reflux or in rare cases an ulcer. If you are having recurrent pain you can use over-the-counter antacid medications. If it is persistent and frequent, you may need to see your family doctor to start a daily medication and perhaps have more investigations.
