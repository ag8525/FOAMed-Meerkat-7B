**Summary:**
In the context of a 62-year-old male patient with suspected sepsis, the document discusses the management of septic shock, specifically comparing early goal-directed therapy (EGDT) with usual care. The ARISE trial, referenced in the document, found no significant difference in 90-day all-cause mortality between EGDT and usual care, suggesting that aggressive EGDT protocols are not necessary for improving survival in septic shock patients. Key management steps include early recognition of sepsis, liberal IV fluid resuscitation, and empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly targeting lung and genitourinary infections. The study emphasizes that invasive resuscitation beyond these measures is not required, aligning with findings from the ProCESS trial, and supports the use of these interventions in emergency department settings for sepsis care.

**Case:**
62-year-old man sent from a nursing home with a three-day history of a productive cough, intermittent fevers, and today is a bit confused. The transfer notes include a history of congestive heart failure, COPD, gout, hypertension, type-2 diabetes, and mild dementia. His vital signs are as follows: Temp 39.1C, heart rate 103, blood pressure 115/100, respiratory rate 26, Oxygen saturation Sat 92% and a normal blood sugar.

**Point:**
Does an ED patient with septic shock need aggressive EGDT or “usual” resuscitation?

**Background:**
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition resulting from the body's extreme response to infection, leading to tissue damage, organ failure, and potentially death. Early recognition is crucial for improving outcomes, as delayed treatment can significantly increase morbidity and mortality. Standard care involves the prompt administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics to combat the underlying infection and fluid resuscitation to maintain hemodynamic stability. Monitoring lactate levels is essential for assessing the severity of sepsis and guiding treatment decisions. Patients with sepsis should be transferred to appropriate care settings, such as intensive care units, to ensure comprehensive management and monitoring.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Invasive EGDT-based sepsis resuscitation is not required compared to early recognition and liberal IV fluid resuscitation and empiric antibiotics in the septic patients.

**Case Resolution:**
Having recognized the sepsis potential of this patient and confirming a high lactate, you initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics for what is most likely a clinical pneumonia. You give aggressive fluid resuscitation with IV normal saline or ringers lactate. Then call your consultant to arrange admission to the intensive care unit.

**Clinical Application:**
Early recognition, liberal IV crystalloid resuscitation, empiric antibiotics (at least cover lung and GU tract) are key interventions for emergency department sepsis care. This is ready for immediate use in the emergency department.

**What Do I Tell My Patients?**
We are going to give you lots of IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and admit you to the hospital.
