**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of esophageal foreign body impaction (EFBI), focusing on the use of glucagon as a treatment option. EFBI is commonly caused by meat boluses and often occurs in patients with underlying esophageal pathology. The study reviewed indicates that glucagon, which works by relaxing smooth muscle and lowering esophageal sphincter pressure, has a low success rate (14.2%) in resolving EFBI compared to observation alone (10.3%), with no significant difference in outcomes. Glucagon administration is associated with adverse effects such as vomiting, and the study suggests that it does not offer significant advantages over expectant management. The document concludes that endoscopy remains the definitive management approach for EFBI, especially in cases with persistent symptoms or underlying esophageal abnormalities.

**Case:**
It’s the day after St. Patrick’s Day, and you have a 28-year-old man that presents to the emergency department with difficulty swallowing. He was enjoying a feast of corned beef and cabbage several hours prior to arrival when he said it felt like *“something got stuck”* in his throat. He does have a history of esophageal strictures, and this has happened to him before but he thought if he washed the food down with enough Guinness he wouldn’t have a problem.

**Background:**
Esophageal foreign body impaction (EFBI) is the most prevalent type of foreign body impaction encountered in U.S. emergency departments, constituting approximately 75% of cases, with meat boluses being the most common. The clinical presentation is often complicated by underlying esophageal pathology, present in 30% to 90% of cases. Management guidelines recommend endoscopic interventions, such as food extraction or advancing the bolus into the stomach, pharmacologic treatments like glucagon, and observation. Glucagon, which relaxes smooth muscle and reduces esophageal sphincter pressure, is used as a temporary measure while coordinating endoscopic therapy, though it should not delay definitive treatment. Despite its safety, glucagon has variable success rates (9% to 38%) and potential adverse effects, including hypersensitivity, hypotension, nausea, vomiting, and dizziness.

**Clinical Point:**
Is glucagon safe and effective for the management of esophageal foreign body impaction?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Glucagon has a low success rate for EFBI, does not seem to offer much benefit over observation alone and is associated with adverse events like vomiting.

**Case Resolution:**
You discuss with the patient that glucagon is sometimes used to treat esophageal foreign body impactions, but it doesn’t work much better than just observation alone and could result in nausea or vomiting. You call the gastroenterologist consultant to arrange for an emergent endoscopy for your patient if the impaction doesn’t resolve spontaneously.

**Clinical Application:**
Glucagon doesn’t appear to offer additional benefit over expectant management for EFBI, may potentially result in adverse effects, and also has costs associated with it. The data makes sense in that it generally fits with what else is out there, but it doesn’t seem like it is strong enough to change practice on its own. Endoscopy for either management or diagnosis of underlying abnormal esophageal anatomy should be performed.

**What do I tell my patient?**
You probably have food stuck in your throat. Glucagon is a medicine that has been used to get the food unstuck. However, there is not good evidence that it works any better than just watching you for a bit. Glucagon also has side effects like vomiting. Our plan is to watch you and if the food is still stuck we will call a specialist. They will come and talk about putting a tube down your throat with a camera on the end of the tube to see what is wrong and fix the problem.
