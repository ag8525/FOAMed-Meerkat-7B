**Summary:**
A 38-year-old female presented with acute left flank pain suggestive of renal colic, with a CT scan confirming a 4mm stone at the ureterovesical junction. A randomized clinical trial compared ketorolac alone to ketorolac plus dexamethasone for pain management in suspected renal colic, finding that the addition of dexamethasone improved pain control at 30 minutes but not at 60 minutes. However, the study's limitations, including diagnostic accuracy and lack of adverse event data, suggest caution in interpreting these results. The SGEM concludes that dexamethasone should not be routinely added to ketorolac for renal colic, emphasizing NSAIDs as the first-line treatment. The patient was managed with ketorolac, discharged with pain management instructions, and referred for urological follow-up.

**Case:**
A 38-year-old female presents to the emergency department (ED) with a five-hour history of acute onset left flank pain. The pain comes in waves, radiates into her left groin and is associated with nausea and vomiting. She noticed darkening of her urine, but does not have dysuria, fever, or vaginal discharge.

**Background:**
Renal colic, a severe pain caused by kidney stones, has been treated with various therapies, including fluid bolus, diuretics, tamsulosin, acupuncture, and lidocaine. Current evidence suggests that neither fluid bolus nor diuretics are necessary for stone passage, and medical expulsive therapy with tamsulosin is not required for stones smaller than 5 mm, though it may benefit distal ureteral stones larger than 5 mm. Acupuncture is not more effective than morphine for pain relief, and lidocaine lacks supporting evidence for use in renal colic. Glucocorticoids, such as dexamethasone, are being considered for their potential to alleviate pain and vomiting and reduce opioid use due to their anti-inflammatory and vasoconstrictive properties.

**Clinical Point:**
Should we be adding a dexamethasone to NSAIDs for the management of suspected acute renal colic?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We cannot recommend the addition of dexamethasone to ketorolac in the treatment of adult patients with suspected renal colic.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is provided with 15mg IV ketorolac and her pain improves. The CT scan shows a 4mm stone at the ureterovesical junction without any hydronephrosis. She is discharged home with a prescription for ketorolac, strainer for urine, instructions on when to return and referred to urology for an outpatient consult.

**Clinical Application:**
I will not be suggesting dexamethasone for patients presenting with suspected acute renal colic. I fully support ketorolac or NSAIDs in general as first line and this is certainly supported in the literature. I do applaud the consistency of this approach in this group.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have a small kidney stone. Most people will be able to pass this size of stone. We are going to give you a prescription for some pain medicine. If your pain gets worse, you can’t keep anything down, develop a fever or are worried please return to the ED. A referral has been sent to the urologist on-call and their office will contact you about an appointment.
