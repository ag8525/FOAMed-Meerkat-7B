**Summary:**
A 19-year-old female with a peripheral pulmonary embolism (PE) was evaluated for potential outpatient management. Despite being Wells' low risk but PERC positive due to birth control use, her elevated D-dimer and CT findings confirmed PE. The document discusses the trend of over-diagnosis of clinically insignificant PEs due to advanced CT imaging, leading to unnecessary hospital admissions and associated harms. A systematic review supports the safety of outpatient management for low-risk PE patients, with zero thromboembolic or hemorrhage-related deaths reported in selected studies. The Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) and Hestia score are recommended for risk stratification. The case resolution involved shared decision-making, resulting in the patient opting for outpatient treatment, aligning with evidence supporting the safety and feasibility of this approach in low-risk cases.

**Case:**
Healthy 19-year-old undergraduate student presents with chest pain and shortness of breath. BP 120/80, HR 60, RR 16, O2 Sat 100%, and T 37.1C. She is Wells’ low but PERC positive for being on birth control. The D-dimer comes back elevated, and the CT scan shows a peripheral PE.

**Point:**
Can she be safely discharged home from the emergency department for out-patient management of her pulmonary embolism?

**Background:**
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a prevalent condition diagnosed in emergency departments, with an incidence of approximately 0.1% to 0.2% among patients. The advent of advanced CT imaging has led to an increase in PE diagnoses, raising concerns about over-diagnosis of clinically insignificant cases that do not contribute to patient symptoms or mortality. Despite the rise in diagnosed cases since CT technology became widespread in 1998, PE-related mortality rates have remained stable over the past 40 years, suggesting over-diagnosis rather than an actual increase in clinically significant PEs. Over-testing for PE has been associated with significant patient harm, including deaths and major medical complications such as renal failure and hemorrhage, outweighing the benefits of preventing PE-related deaths and events. Additionally, the economic burden of PE diagnosis and treatment is substantial, with inpatient costs ranging from $25,000 to $44,000 and ongoing expenses for anticoagulation therapy.

**Clinical Application:**
In select and agreeable non-geriatric adult patients with newly diagnosed PE, transportation access to outpatient anticoagulation care, and a reliable caregiver at home, outpatient management of PE is safe with PE or hemorrhage-related deaths <1%.

**Case Resolution:**
You engage the patient in shared decision-making. You inform her multiple studies have demonstrated that treating a low-risk PE at home with shots and pills is as safe and effective as treating you with the same medications in the hospital if she has the ability to follow up within 7-10 days as scheduled, and have somebody at home to help you monitor your care. She decides on out-patient treatment.
