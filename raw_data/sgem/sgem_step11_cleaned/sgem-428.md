**Summary:**
The document discusses the administration of nitrates in patients with right ventricular myocardial infarction (RVMI), challenging the traditional caution against their use due to potential hypotension. Historically, this caution was based on a small 1989 study, but recent evidence, including a systematic review and meta-analysis, suggests no significant increase in adverse events when nitrates are used in RVMI compared to other myocardial infarctions. The review highlights that hypotension, if it occurs, is typically transient and manageable. The authors advocate for revising guidelines to allow nitrate use in RVMI with appropriate caution, emphasizing the need for IV access to manage potential hypotension. The document underscores the importance of balancing potential benefits, such as symptom relief, against the risks.

**Case:**
You are an advanced care paramedic dispatched to the scene of a 53-year-old female with chest pain. She developed retrosternal chest discomfort shortly after she came in from using her snowblower. You provide her with two tablets of ASA to chew while your partner acquires an ECG, which ultimately shows clear ST elevation in the inferior leads and some ST changes in V1-V2 that make you wonder if your patient is having an acute myocardial infarction (MI) with right-sided ventricular involvement (RVI). Your patient rates their pain as an 8/10 and looks visibly uncomfortable. Do you give nitroglycerin?

**Background:**
The recommendation to avoid nitrates in acute myocardial infarctions (MIs) with right ventricular involvement is primarily based on a single 1989 observational study of 40 patients, which suggested an increased risk of hypotension due to nitrate-induced vasodilation reducing right ventricular preload. This study has been cited by major guidelines from the American Heart Association and the European Society of Cardiology. However, the study's design, including its retrospective nature and lack of standardization in nitrate administration, raises concerns about its validity. The incidence of hypotension was exaggerated by the study's equal distribution of hypotensive and non-hypotensive patients, whereas the actual incidence in MIs is likely much lower. In contrast, a larger 2016 Canadian study involving 1,004 patients found no significant difference in adverse events between patients with and without right ventricular MI when standardized nitroglycerin was administered, challenging the earlier findings. This discrepancy highlights the variability in guideline recommendations, as evidenced by a review of ACC/AHA guidelines, where only a small percentage of recommendations are based on high-level evidence.

**Clinical Point:**
Is nitrate administration to patients with right ventricular myocardial infarction (RVMI) associated with increased adverse events compared with nitrate administration to patients with myocardial infarctions only in other regions?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Don’t be afraid to give nitrates to patients with right ventricular MI. However, be prepared to manage transient hypotension in any patient to whom nitrates are administered.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient is clearly in discomfort, and nitroglycerin may help to ease their symptoms. Though you recognize the historical call for caution when administering nitrates to a patient with suspected RVMI, the best evidence says that doing so is no more dangerous than giving it to any other MI patient. Hypotension is a possibility regardless of the cardiac territory involved, though usually transient and easily managed, so you make sure to start an IV line before giving that first dose.

**Clinical Application:**
The overall level of evidence as assessed by GRADE criteria is very low. The authors recommend that the AHA and European guidelines be revised from contraindicating nitrates in RVMI to permitting them with a more appropriate degree of caution. We echo that position as a reasonable step in the *right* direction.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
This nitroglycerin spray might help to ease some of your chest discomfort, but there’s always the risk that it could drop your blood pressure too. We’ll watch your vital signs on the monitor, and we have this IV set up in case your blood pressure drops too low.

I would also tell my EMS partner if their BP pressure drops do what Bobby McFerrin says, *“it will soon pass, whatever it is. Don’t worry…be happy”*
