**Summary:**
The document discusses the prevalence of low-quality evidence in emergency medicine, highlighting that many interventions lack strong, unbiased support for improved outcomes. It critiques the reliance on underpowered and methodologically flawed studies, emphasizing the need for more robust, well-powered trials. Key findings include the limited number of interventions with high-level evidence, such as mechanical CPR and transfer for angioplasty, which show some mortality benefits. The document underscores the importance of scientific skepticism and critical appraisal in clinical practice, advocating for a balance between evidence-based medicine and clinical judgment. It concludes that while literature informs care, it should not dictate it, and emphasizes the need for ongoing research to strengthen the evidence base in emergency medicine.

**Case:**
A resident has been following the literature over their four years of training. They have already seen several things come into fashion and go out of fashion during this short time. This includes therapeutic hypothermia for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), tranexamic acid (TXA) for epistaxis, and electrolyte solutions for mild pediatric gastroenteritis. They wonder how strong the evidence is for much of what we do in emergency medicine.

**Background:**
Medical practice often relies on low-quality evidence, as demonstrated by studies examining the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines, where only a small percentage of recommendations are based on high-quality evidence. For instance, from 1984 to 2008, only 11% of recommendations were Level A, indicating strong evidence, while this decreased to 9% from 2008 to 2018. This issue extends beyond cardiology, as seen in orthopedic procedures, where a review found that only carpal tunnel decompression and total knee replacement were superior to non-operative care. Additionally, several medical practices, such as the use of topical anesthetics for corneal abrasions, epinephrine for out-of-hospital cardiac arrests, and tranexamic acid for various hemorrhages, have been shown not to improve primary outcomes, challenging long-held beliefs in their efficacy.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the effect of faults such as underpowered studies, flawed studies (i.e., methodologic and statistical errors, poorly designed studies) and biases in the field of therapeutic interventions in the emergency medicine literature?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Many interventions in emergency medicine are not supported by high-quality, unbiased evidence.

**Case Resolution:**
You discuss scientific skepticism with the resident. Remind them that each claim needs to be supported by evidence and logical arguments. Without high-quality evidence, we should usually accept the null hypothesis. That does not mean an intervention could not work. Rather, we do not have good evidence that it does work. This is an important distinction.

**Clinical Application:**
The literature guides and informs our care but should not dictate our care. There are few interventions in EM with high-quality, unbiased evidence. We still need to apply our clinical judgment and ask the patients about their values and preferences. *“It is instructive to note that most people make patient-centered decisions every day without high-quality (e.g., RCT) evidence, and these decisions are not always wrong. Furthermore, foundational papers in EBM make it explicitly clear that EBM was never meant to exclude information derived from experience and intuition.”* (Braithwaite RS. JAMA 2013).

**What Do I Tell the Resident:**
Much of what we do in emergency medicine is based upon low-quality biased evidence. We are often standing on pillars of salt and sand. Stay skeptical, develop your critical appraisal skills, and try to avoid nihilism.
