**Summary:**
The document discusses the challenges in diagnosing sepsis in emergency department (ED) patients, highlighting that many patients meeting sepsis criteria are not diagnosed with sepsis at discharge, potentially leading to unnecessary and harmful treatments. The study, a retrospective observational cohort design, found that 75% of patients meeting Sepsis-3 criteria and 52% of those meeting Sepsis-3 plus shock criteria were not diagnosed with sepsis at discharge. It also noted that a significant proportion of non-septic patients were at risk of harm from aggressive fluid resuscitation, particularly those with conditions like CHF and renal failure. The study underscores the importance of clinical judgment in sepsis management, cautioning against the automatic application of sepsis treatment protocols, such as the administration of 30cc/kg IV fluids, without considering individual patient risk factors and the potential for harm. The findings suggest a need for more precise diagnostic criteria and treatment strategies to avoid unnecessary interventions in non-septic patients.

**Case:**
A 60-year-old man presents to the emergency department with a non-productive cough and increasing shortness of breath. He has a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension (HTN), congestive heart failure (CHF), and benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH). He’s afebrile. He has a heart rate of 93 beats per minute, a blood pressure of 145/90 mm Hg, respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 92% on room air.

Initial labs come back with a slightly decreased platelet count (149) and a minimally elevated creatinine (1.21 mg/dl or 107 umol/L). He triggers a sepsis alert, and you get a pop-up suggesting IV antibiotics and 30cc/kg of IV fluids. So, you ask yourself, is this guy really septic and should we bypass those fluids?

**Background:**
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by a dysregulated immune response to infection, leading to organ dysfunction. It is clinically significant as it contributes to approximately 5.3 million deaths annually among hospitalized patients worldwide and is the leading cause of death in intensive care units in the United States, also being the most costly diagnosis. Despite the lack of statistical difference in outcomes between early goal-directed therapy and usual care, early administration of antibiotics has been associated with improved mortality rates. The 2018 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, which recommend fluid, antibiotics, and pressor administration within the first hour of triage, have been controversial due to insufficient high-quality evidence supporting these sepsis bundles. Many hospitals have integrated sepsis alerts into their electronic medical records to identify and treat septic patients promptly, although the effectiveness of interventions like the infusion of 30cc/kg of IV fluids remains debated due to potential harm. Diagnosing sepsis is challenging, as the need for high sensitivity often results in the inclusion of patients without infections, such as those with cirrhosis or on dialysis, who may be at risk from certain interventions. Since 2015, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services have linked hospital care quality for sepsis to the SEP-1 core measure, emphasizing the importance of early antibiotic treatment.

**Clinical Point:**
What proportions of patients meeting sepsis criteria were actually diagnosed with sepsis, and how many non-septic patients had risk factors for harm from aggressive fluid resuscitation?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Many people meeting sepsis criteria will not have sepsis and that exposes them to potential harm from protocolized treatments.

**Case Resolution:**
You do a bedside lung ultrasound, and you see B-lines. So, you sign the order for antibiotics but hold off on the IV fluids based on the patient’s sufficient blood pressure and what you just saw on the ultrasound. It turns out, his cough and shortness of breath is due to a CHF exacerbation and not sepsis. He’s admitted to cardiology, they give him diuretics, and he is discharged home a few days later.

**Clinical Application:**
This article is a reminder that there is a balance between the potential benefits and potential harms of any treatment. If you cast the net too wide you will increase your sensitivity (the true positives) but this can be at the cost of specificity (the true negatives). Treating people without disease can lead to an increase in harms. When the evidence is not great for benefit, such as the 30 cc/kg bolus for every sepsis alert, we would be wise to take a moment and use our clinical judgment before relying solely on an algorithm.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I think your cough and shortness of breath is due to fluid backing up on your lungs. Our hospital has a system to try and identify patients that might have a severe infection called sepsis. The protocol is to give you a large amount of fluid through your IV. My clinical judgment is this is not a lung infection. Giving you extra fluid could actually make things worse. The cardiologists agree. We will admit you to the hospital and work on getting the fluid off your lungs to make you feel better.
