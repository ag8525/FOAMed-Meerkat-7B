**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of therapeutic hypothermia in the pre-hospital setting for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, specifically examining its impact on survival and neurological outcomes. Despite previous studies suggesting neuroprotective benefits of hypothermia, recent large-scale trials, including a JAMA study with 1359 patients, found no improvement in survival or neurological status with pre-hospital cooling, and noted potential harms such as increased re-arrest rates. The study highlighted that while cooling reduced core temperature upon hospital arrival, it did not confer benefits over standard care, challenging earlier findings and suggesting that pre-hospital cooling may not be necessary. The conclusion emphasizes a "scoop and run" approach without field cooling, reflecting a shift in clinical practice based on new evidence.

**Case Scenario:**
A 72yo man has witnessed arrest while watching his grandson’s hockey game. Bystander CPR is started and he is shocked out of ventricular fibrillation using the automatic external defibrillator (AED). EMS arrives and finds a patient with vital signs stable but unconscious. The paramedic calls the base hospital and asks if they should start cooling en route.

**Background:**
Therapeutic hypothermia is a medical intervention used post-cardiac arrest to provide neuroprotection, as evidenced by two pivotal randomized control trials published in 2002, which demonstrated improved neurological outcomes with hypothermia. The first trial, involving 273 participants, showed a 55% good outcome at six months compared to 39% in the control group, while a smaller Australian study with 77 participants reported a 49% good neurologic outcome at hospital discharge versus 26% in the control group. Dr. David Newman calculated a number needed to treat (NNT) of 6 for mild therapeutic hypothermia following cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The Cochrane Collaboration's 2012 review supported the use of conventional cooling methods for improved survival and neurological outcomes, aligning with International Resuscitation Guidelines. However, skepticism remains, as highlighted by the SGEM and a 2010 study by Bernard SA et al., which found no benefit from pre-hospital therapeutic hypothermia using ice-cold lactated Ringer’s in 234 patients. A recent large study published in NEJM with 950 participants concluded that targeted temperature management at 33°C did not offer additional benefits over 36°C in unconscious survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest of presumed cardiac cause.

**Point:**
Does pre-hospital cooling improve survival to hospital discharge and neurological outcome in patients presenting with ventricular fibrillation (VF) or without VF?

**Bottom Line:**
Scoop and run after cardiac arrest with no cooling required in the field.

**Case Scenario Conclusion:**
Patient was not cooled in the field but he was on arrival as part of a pre-existing protocol. He survived to hospital admission but unfortunately, not to hospital discharge.
