**Summary:**
The document discusses disparities in the emergency department management of pediatric migraine, focusing on the influence of race, ethnicity, and language (REaL) on treatment decisions. A retrospective analysis of 833 pediatric patients revealed that non-Hispanic White and English-speaking patients were more likely to receive intravenous (IV) medications compared to oral or intranasal treatments, with adjusted odds ratios indicating significant disparities. The study highlights the persistence of oligoanalgesia and suggests that REaL factors may influence the likelihood of receiving certain types of pain management. The findings underscore the need for awareness and potential intervention to address these disparities in pain management practices.

**Case:**
A 15-year-old patient presents to the Emergency Department with a unilateral pounding headache. The headache is similar to prior migraine headaches. They have photophobia but no vision changes, weakness, numbness, tingling, or neurologic deficits. They took 400 mg ibuprofen at home without relief. The patient and their mother ask what the next steps will be and what type of medication will be administered.

**Background:**
Migraines are a common condition leading patients to seek emergency department (ED) care for pain relief, yet effective pain management remains a challenge despite the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations (JCAHO) designating pain as the "fifth vital sign" in 2001 to address oligoanalgesia. This issue is particularly pronounced among vulnerable groups, including the elderly, women, the mentally ill, certain ethnic groups, and individuals with specific insurance statuses. Children are notably less likely to receive adequate analgesia. The role of race, ethnicity, and language (REaL) in influencing pain management outcomes is hypothesized but not well-documented, especially in conditions like migraines where opioids are not typically indicated.

**Clinical Point:**
Is there an association between patient demographics (race, ethnicity, and language) and pain management among pediatric ED patients presenting with migraine headaches?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Non-Hispanic White English-speaking pediatric patients presenting to the ED are more likely to receive IV medication to treat their migraine headache compared to other races/ethnicities and non-English speaking patients.
