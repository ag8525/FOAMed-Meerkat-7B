**Summary:**
Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is proposed as a first-line imaging modality for suspected small bowel obstruction (SBO) to reduce costs, length of stay, and radiation exposure compared to the traditional use of CT scans. Clinical examination alone is insufficient for diagnosing SBO, necessitating imaging, with CT being the standard. However, a 2018 meta-analysis supports ultrasound's high sensitivity and specificity for SBO diagnosis. A Monte Carlo model study by Brower et al. suggests that adopting a POCUS-first approach could prevent approximately 143,000 CT scans annually in the US, saving $30.1 million and reducing radiation exposure, potentially preventing 98 excess cancer deaths. While the study supports significant national cost savings and reduced radiation exposure, its applicability outside the US requires further investigation. In practice, POCUS can effectively identify SBO, allowing for conservative management and potentially avoiding CT scans unless surgical intervention is anticipated.

**Case:**
A 63-year-old woman presents to your emergency department (ED) with a two-day history of nausea, vomiting, and constipation. She tells you that she had appendicitis complicated by perforation and peritonitis ten years ago, and you suspect she has adhesional small bowel obstruction. You call your surgical colleague who, predictably, asks you to order a CT. The patient asks if there is an alternative as she had several CTs on her last admission and is worried about her radiation exposure and her co-pay.

**Background:**
Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is a condition affecting 2-4% of patients with abdominal pain in US emergency departments, with significant clinical implications, including 60,000 hospitalizations and 565,000 inpatient care days annually for those requiring surgical intervention. Clinical examination alone is inadequate for diagnosing SBO due to its low sensitivity and specificity, necessitating imaging for accurate diagnosis. Computed tomography (CT) is the preferred imaging modality, particularly with intravenous contrast, as recommended by a 2020 UK report, due to its effectiveness in timely diagnosis. While plain X-rays have limited predictive value, ultrasound has demonstrated high sensitivity (92.4%) and specificity (96.6%) according to a 2018 meta-analysis.

**Clinical Point:**
Does using point of care ultrasound first line in suspected small bowel obstruction reduce cost, length of stay, and radiation exposure?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
POCUS as first-line imaging in suspected SBO could avoid significant numbers of CT scans in the US.

**Case Resolution:**
You meet your surgical colleague at the bedside and perform POCUS, which shows SBO. After discussion with the patient, she is admitted for conservative management and a CT is avoided.

**Clinical Application:**
We may be able to avoid significant numbers of CTs for suspected SBO by using POCUS as first-line imaging.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
We can perform bedside ultrasound which can demonstrate SBO – it is likely though that if operative intervention is needed the surgeon will still want you to have a CT scan performed.
