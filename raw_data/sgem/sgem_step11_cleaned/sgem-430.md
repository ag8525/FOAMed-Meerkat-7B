**Summary:**
The DAShED study investigates the diagnostic challenges of acute aortic syndrome (AAS) in the emergency department, highlighting the condition's high mortality and frequent misdiagnosis. Despite the low incidence of AAS (0.3%) among patients presenting with potential symptoms, 7% underwent CT angiography (CTA). The study evaluates clinical decision tools (CDTs) like the Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS) and their integration with clinical gestalt and D-dimer testing, finding that CDTs did not significantly outperform clinical judgment alone. The study underscores the need for improved diagnostic strategies to prevent delays and misdiagnoses, as current CDTs are not yet sufficiently reliable. The case resolution emphasizes the importance of clinical gestalt in suspecting AAS, leading to a CTA that confirmed a thoracic aortic dissection, necessitating urgent surgical intervention.

**Case:**
A 66-year-old female with a history of smoking, hypertension, and type-2 diabetes presents to the emergency department (ED) with syncope while walking her dog. She complains of retrosternal chest pain radiating to her jaw. She is bradycardic, hypotensive, and tachypneic.

She is received in the resuscitation room. Monitors have been attached, and intravenous (IV) access has been achieved and IV analgesia has been given. The ECG shows sinus tachycardia with non-specific ST-T changes. The chest x-ray is unremarkable. However, she is still in severe pain. A post-graduate year 2 (PGY-2) resident asks you if it could be a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection, or angina.

**Background:**
Acute aortic syndrome (AAS) is a critical condition encompassing aortic dissection (AD), intramural hematoma (IMH), and penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU), collectively known as the "lethal triad." It is rare but highly fatal, affecting approximately 4,000 individuals annually in the UK and 43,000 to 47,000 in the USA, with an AD incidence of 2.9 to 7.2 per 100,000. Misdiagnosis rates range from 16% to 38%, with diagnostic delays of up to 24 hours in 25% of cases, leading to a mortality increase of 0.5% per hour within the first 48 hours. A study in Ontario reported an AAS incidence of 0.61 per 100,000 and highlighted a significant mortality rate, with a one-year mortality decreasing from 47.4% to 29.1% and ED mortality at 14.9%. The gold standard for diagnosing AAS is a CT aorta angiogram (CTA), but its widespread use is limited by low diagnostic yield, radiation exposure, and cost. Clinical decision tools (CDTs) like the Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS) have been developed, with studies suggesting high sensitivity for ruling out AAS when combined with d-dimer levels, though their clinical utility and impact on reducing unnecessary CTAs remain uncertain.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It remains challenging to diagnose AAS based on clinical gestalt alone, but CDTs are not yet ready for prime time.

**Case Resolution:**
The nurse administers some fentanyl for pain control while you perform a point-of-care ultrasound. This exam is equivocal while your clinical gestalt suggests an AAS. You tell the resident to order a CTA and the scan reveals an aortic dissection of the thoracic aorta. Cardiothoracic and Vascular surgery are consulted, and the patient is rushed to the operation room (OR) for surgical repair.

**Clinical Application:**
While incorporating clinical gestalt and a D-dimer into a CDT shows promise, further research is needed to evaluate whether it can reduce the risk of delayed or missed diagnoses of AAS compared to clinical gestalt alone.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
In my clinical judgment, your symptoms may be due to a condition called acute aortic syndrome (AAS). AAS is a rare but life-threatening condition. We are sending you for a CT scan to find out. If you do have this condition, we will be calling the surgeons who can fix this problem.
