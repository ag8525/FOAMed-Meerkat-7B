**Summary:**
A randomized controlled trial involving 1,652 patients from Australia and New Zealand evaluated the efficacy of paracetamol compared to placebo in treating acute low-back pain without red flags. The study found no significant difference in the median time to recovery between regular paracetamol, as-needed paracetamol, and placebo groups, with hazard ratios indicating no advantage of paracetamol over placebo. Secondary outcomes, including pain intensity, disability, and quality of life, also showed no differences between groups. The authors concluded that paracetamol does not expedite recovery in low-back pain, challenging its universal endorsement as a first-line treatment. The study highlights the self-limiting nature of acute low-back pain and the potential influence of placebo effects, suggesting that treatment should focus on patient education and maintaining activity rather than relying solely on pharmacological interventions.

**Case Scenario:**
35-year-old man presents to the emergency department with mechanical back pain after doing some heavy lifting on the weekend. He has no "red flags".

**Point:**
Does paracetamol improve time to recovery from pain compared to placebo in patients with low-back pain?

**Background:**
Low-back pain is the leading cause of disability globally, with paracetamol (acetaminophen) recommended as the first-line treatment, although no randomized control trials have compared it to placebo. Clinical red flags associated with low-back pain include indicators of serious underlying conditions such as cancer, infection, cauda equina syndrome, significant herniated nucleus pulposus, vertebral fractures, and abdominal aortic aneurysm. Cancer-related red flags include a history of cancer, significant unexplained weight loss, age extremes, therapy failure, persistent pain, and nocturnal pain. Infection-related red flags involve persistent fever, intravenous drug use, severe pain, recent lumbar surgery, bacterial infections, and immunocompromised states. Cauda equina syndrome is suggested by urinary issues, saddle anesthesia, decreased anal sphincter tone, bilateral lower extremity symptoms, and progressive neurological deficits. Significant herniated nucleus pulposus is indicated by major muscle weakness and foot drop. Vertebral fracture red flags include prolonged corticosteroid use, advanced age, osteoporosis, and trauma. Abdominal aortic aneurysm red flags include an abdominal pulsating mass, vascular disease, rest or nocturnal pain, and age over 60.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It appears that paracetamol does not improve time to recovery compared to placebo for out-patients with low-back pain.

**Clinical Application:**
This is just one randomized clinical trial looking into the issue and is not strong enough evidence for me to abandon recommending this treatment modality. I would like to see the study replicated in my practice environment. Specifically, a study looking at consecutive patients presenting to the emergency department with low-back pain.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You appear to have a mechanical injury to your low-back. There are no "red flags" to suggest anything more serious is going on right now. The natural history of this condition is for it to resolve with or without treatment. Has anything worked well for you in the past? Different treatments have different potential benefits and harms. Taking paracetamol has not been shown to speed up how fast you get better. Most people get better within a few weeks. You should try to stay active and see your primary care doctor in a couple of weeks if the pain is not resolving. Please return to the emergency department if your pain is getting worse, you develop any of those red flags we discussed, or are otherwise concerned.
