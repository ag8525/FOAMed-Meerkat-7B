**Summary:**
A 74-year-old man with a history of COPD, type 2 diabetes, and other comorbidities presents with altered consciousness and is diagnosed with sepsis secondary to pneumonia, evidenced by right lower lobe infiltrate on CXR and meeting SIRS criteria. Initial management includes Early Goal Directed Therapy with IV fluids and antibiotics, considering vasopressors to maintain MAP. The Asfar et al. study, referenced in the case, evaluated the impact of targeting a higher MAP (80-85mmHg) versus a lower MAP (65-70mmHg) in septic shock patients, finding no significant difference in 28-day mortality or serious adverse events between the groups. The study suggests that while higher MAP may reduce renal injury in hypertensive patients, the ideal MAP target remains unclear and should be individualized based on patient factors. The patient in this case received aggressive fluid resuscitation, non-invasive ventilation, and vasopressor support, and was admitted to the ICU for further management.

**Case:**
74-year-old man is brought by EMS for altered level of consciousness. He lives alone, family hadn’t heard from him in several days so went to check, found him to be coughing and mildly confused. EMS vitals: HR 115, RR 24, BP 103/70, O2 89% on room air, 99% on non-re-breather mask, T 38.4C and blood glucose is normal. The patient is not intubation, wishes no CPR, but full medical management.

Past medical history includes: COPD, type 2 diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension and peripheral vascular disease. He is triaged to acute care bed, IV access obtained and bolus of 1 liter normal saline provided. ECG shows sinus tachycardia and portable CXR demonstrates right lower lobe infiltrate. Laboratory tests are drawn and sent.

You start Early Goal Directed Therapy (EGDT) by rapidly recognizing this man is septic, provided IV fluids and appropriate IV antibiotics for pneumonia. Now you are starting to think about more fluids +/- vasopressors to increase his Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP).

**Point:**
Does a MAP of 80-85mmHg decrease 28 day mortality as compared to a MAP of 65-70mmHg?

**Background:**
Sepsis is a clinical syndrome resulting from severe infection, characterized by systemic inflammation that can lead to multiple organ dysfunction. It is a significant cause of morbidity in emergency departments, with a short-term mortality rate of approximately 20% and around 750,000 cases annually in the United States. The condition progresses through a continuum from Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) to septic shock, with SIRS defined by specific criteria such as abnormal temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and white blood cell count. Sepsis is identified by the presence of infection alongside two or more SIRS criteria, while severe sepsis includes hypotension and organ failure, and septic shock is marked by hypotension unresponsive to fluid resuscitation. Management of sepsis emphasizes early recognition and intervention, including the rapid administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, fluid resuscitation to central venous pressure targets, vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure, and transfusions to optimize hematocrit and central venous oxygen saturation. Recent evidence suggests that invasive blood pressure monitoring may not be necessary for effective management.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
The ideal target MAP in septic shock is still unclear – patient factors play a role and care should likely be individualized.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient receives 4 liters of normal saline in the emergency department. His resuscitation also includes non-invasive positive pressure ventilation, a central line and levophed to support blood pressure. He is then admitted to the ICU for ongoing critical care.

**Clinical Application:**
Reasonable to consider MAP above 65mmHg depending on the case.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It looks like you have a serious infection. We are going to give you intravenous fluids, increase your blood pressure with medication if needed, provide broad-spectrum antibiotics, monitor your response to treatment and admit you to the hospital for further care.
