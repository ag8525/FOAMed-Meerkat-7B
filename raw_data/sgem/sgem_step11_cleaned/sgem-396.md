**Summary:**
In the context of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), this document evaluates the effectiveness of two supraglottic airway devices, the iGel and the King Laryngeal Tube (King-LT), as alternatives to endotracheal intubation. The retrospective study analyzed data from 93,866 patients, revealing that the iGel was associated with better outcomes, particularly when used as a rescue device after failed intubation, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.16 for survival to hospital discharge home. The iGel also demonstrated higher first-pass success rates, increased prehospital return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), and better intra-arrest ETCO2 values compared to the King-LT. However, no significant difference was found between the devices when used as the initial airway management strategy. The study highlights the limitations of retrospective data, including potential selection bias and the lack of comprehensive outcome data, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation of the results.

**Case:**
A paramedic crew responds to a 54-year-old male in cardiac arrest at a private residence. A fire company is on scene providing high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and has defibrillated twice with an automated external defibrillator (AED). The fire-based crew has basic life support (BLS) airway supplies including the King Laryngeal Tube, the paramedic crew carries iGel supraglottic airways (SGAs) in addition to their intubation equipment. They plan to use a supraglottic airway as their initial airway strategy during the arrest, but they wonder if either of these two devices is superior.

**Background:**
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical condition requiring immediate intervention, with airway management being a crucial component of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). The clinical significance of OHCA lies in its high mortality rate and the challenges associated with effective resuscitation. Traditional endotracheal intubation (ETI) during cardiac arrest is technically demanding, prompting the exploration of alternative airway management strategies. Recent large trials, including PART by Wang et al and AIRWAYS-2 by Benger et al, have evaluated the use of supraglottic airway devices such as the King-LT and iGel as viable alternatives to ETI in the prehospital setting. These devices are gaining popularity due to their ease of use and potential to improve outcomes in OHCA scenarios.

**Clinical Point:**
Which supraglottic airway is associated with better patient outcomes, the iGel or the King-LT in patients with an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

**Case Resolution:**
The paramedic responsible for airway management elects to insert an iGel because this is the device they are most familiar with. The crew continues high quality CPR on scene and defibrillates the patient twice before he attains sustained ROSC. Serial 12-lead ECGs post ROSC do not demonstrate STEMI. The patient is transported to the nearest emergency department where he admitted to the Cardiac ICU. He later undergoes delayed cardiac catheterization which demonstrates no culprit lesions, he subsequently has an IACD implanted and is discharged home on hospital day four.

**Clinical Application:**
The iGel and King-LT are both appropriate options for airway management in OHCA. The iGel may be the superior rescue device after failed ETI and it appears to be more likely to be successfully inserted on the first attempt.

**What Do I Tell the Patient’s Family?**
We’re doing what we can to save your loved one. We are going to put something in his mouth to help him breathe. The most important things we can do now are to continue high quality CPR. Then we will try to shock him out of the cardiac arrest. If that is successful, we will transfer him the closest hospital for more care.
