**Summary:**
The document discusses the effectiveness of common elective orthopedic procedures, highlighting a case of a 55-year-old man with severe tri-compartment knee arthritis. It emphasizes the limited high-quality evidence supporting the superiority of most elective orthopedic surgeries over non-operative care, with only carpal tunnel decompression and total knee replacement showing clear benefits. The document critiques the reliance on surgical interventions without robust evidence, referencing studies like the KANON trial, which found no superiority of early ACL reconstruction over rehabilitation. It underscores the importance of considering potential surgical risks and exhausting conservative treatments before opting for surgery, aligning with the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons guidelines. The case resolution involves referring the patient to an orthopedic surgeon to explore treatment options, emphasizing conservative management before considering joint replacement.

**Case:**
A 55-year-old man comes into the emergency department (ED) for increasing knee pain and decrease in function. He’s had an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) repair and used to run marathons. However, he is finding it more difficult to even put his socks on. Physical exam shows varus deformity at the knee, decreased range of motion, crepitus, no locking and neurovascularly intact distal. X-rays show severe, tri-compartment arthritis.

**Background:**
Musculoskeletal complaints frequently lead to emergency department visits, where physicians often address orthopedic surgical procedures. However, the evidence supporting the efficacy of common elective orthopedic procedures is limited, with only 2.8% of interventions in systematic reviews and meta-analyses relevant to emergency medicine demonstrating unbiased and strong evidence for improved outcomes. This issue reflects a broader challenge in medical evidence quality. A study by Tricoci et al. in 2009 reviewed ACC/AHA guidelines from 1984 to 2008, revealing that only 11% of 7,196 recommendations were based on Level A evidence, with the majority being Level B or C. A subsequent update by Fanaroff et al. in 2019 showed little improvement, with Level A recommendations decreasing to 9% among 2,930 recommendations from 2008 to 2018. This underscores the need for critical evaluation of the evidence supporting elective orthopedic procedures.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the effectiveness of common elective orthopedic procedures compared with no treatment, placebo, or non-operative care?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There is a lack of high-quality evidence to support all but two out of the ten most common elective orthopedic procedures.

**Case Resolution:**
The patient with his progressively worse knee is referred to an orthopedic surgeon to discuss his options.

**Clinical Application:**
This information can help patients and physicians in their decision-making process. In their supplemental material, they compare their results to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) guidelines in Appendix 11. AAOS clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) use similar methodology with work groups analyzing the best available evidence and grading it. They look at not only surgical options but also non-surgical options (orthobiologics, steroids, physiotherapy for knee osteoarthritis, etc). As is highlighted in this review, there is frequently a lack of high-level studies to support any intervention (operative or nonoperative) and that is reflected in the grading of CPG.
