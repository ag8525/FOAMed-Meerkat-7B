**Summary:**
In the case of Ms. Jones, a 45-year-old woman presenting with a sudden severe headache, the clinical focus is on ruling out subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Despite a normal neurologic exam and a negative non-contrast head CT read by a community radiologist, the Point arises whether a lumbar puncture (LP) is necessary to exclude SAH. Recent studies, including a retrospective analysis by Blok et al., suggest that in non-academic settings, a negative CT scan performed within six hours of headache onset has a high negative predictive value (99.9%) for ruling out SAH, thus potentially obviating the need for an LP in low-prevalence populations. The Ottawa SAH Rule, while sensitive, has limited specificity and applicability, further supporting the sufficiency of a negative CT in these scenarios. The study highlights the comparable diagnostic accuracy of community radiologists to their academic counterparts in identifying SAH on CT scans, emphasizing the importance of shared decision-making with patients regarding further invasive testing like LP, considering its potential risks and low yield in such contexts.

**Case:**
Ms. Jones is a 45-year-old woman who presents to her local community hospital with the *“worst headache”* of her life that started suddenly four hours ago. She has a normal neurologic exam. She gets a non-contrast head CT that is read as normal by your local radiologist.

**Background:**
Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a critical medical condition characterized by bleeding into the subarachnoid space, often due to a ruptured aneurysm. Its clinical significance lies in its potential for high morbidity and mortality if not promptly diagnosed and treated. Standard diagnostic approaches include computed tomography (CT) scans, with studies indicating that a normal CT scan performed within six hours of headache onset at a tertiary care hospital has a 100% negative predictive value for ruling out SAH. The necessity of lumbar puncture (LP) following a normal CT scan is debated, as some studies suggest it may not be required. The Ottawa SAH Rule, developed to aid in the diagnosis of SAH, demonstrated 100% sensitivity but only 7.6% specificity in a validation study, limiting its practical application due to low specificity and its relevance to a limited patient population in emergency settings. Further validation, particularly in non-academic centers, is needed to assess its broader utility.

**Clinical Point:**
Can a SAH be ruled out in a patient presenting to a non-academic ED within 6hrs of headache onset by a head CT read by a community staff radiologist?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
These community radiologists were just as good at reading CT heads as academic radiologists when looking for blood using a third generation scanner

**Case Resolution:**
You feel Ms. Jones has a low pre-test probability of having a SAH. Her head CT is negative and you discuss the risk and benefits of a lumbar puncture. A shared decision is made. She decides not to proceed with any further testing and will return if she develops focal neurologic symptoms, pain increases, loss of consciousness, seizure or is otherwise worried.

**Clinical Application:**
A negative CT head scan read by a community radiologist using a third-generation scanner within 6 hours of headache onset is sufficient to exclude the diagnosis of SAH in a low prevalence population.

**What do I tell my patient?**
Subarachnoid hemorrhages can present with sudden headaches. These types of bleeds in the brain can be devastating and even deadly. Our local radiologist looked at the CT scan of your brain and did not see any bleeding. They are just as good as radiologists at the big academic hospitals for reading these tests. CT scans are very good to rule out a bleed when done within six hours of headache onset but no test is 100%. A lumbar puncture could be done if we are really concerned. That involves sticking a needle in your back to get fluid from around your spinal column. This test can have complications. A common side effect of the test is a headache. In addition, often the test is falsely positive. That means doing even more testing that could potentially cause harm. There are studies suggesting hundreds or maybe even thousands of LPs would need to be done to find one of these serious/life-threatening bleeds not seen on CT scan. What do you want to do?
