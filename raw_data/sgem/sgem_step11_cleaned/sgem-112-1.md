**Summary:**
The document discusses the use of intravenous hypertonic saline (HTS) as a potential treatment for pediatric concussive pain, specifically in children aged 4-17 with moderate to severe concussion symptoms. A randomized controlled trial compared HTS to normal saline (NS), showing that HTS significantly reduced pain both immediately and at 2-3 days post-treatment. However, the study's conclusions are questioned due to significant limitations, including a small sample size, potential bias from higher initial pain scores in the HTS group, and issues with generalizability due to strict inclusion criteria and follow-up inconsistencies. The SGEM conclusion advises against the routine use of HTS for concussion pain in children, suggesting further research is needed before it can be recommended for clinical application.

**Case:**
A 11-year-old snowboarder has a mild headache after her concussion. The mother wants to know if there is anything else besides acetaminophen or ibuprofen to treat her daughter’s headache?

**Clinical Point:**
What about using intravenous hypertonic saline as a therapy for paediatric concussive pain?

**Background:**
Hypertonic saline is a therapeutic agent used to manage increased intracranial pressure, a condition that can arise from various causes such as traumatic brain injury, stroke, or brain tumors. This treatment was first documented nearly a century ago by Weed and McKibben, highlighting its longstanding clinical significance. Hypertonic saline works by creating an osmotic gradient that draws fluid out of the brain tissue, thereby reducing intracranial pressure and potentially preventing further neurological damage. It remains a critical intervention in the management of patients with elevated intracranial pressure, contributing to improved outcomes and reduced morbidity and mortality associated with this condition.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Although an interesting therapeutic modality to be further studied, IV hypertonic saline is not ready for routine use in children with moderate-severe concussions.

None at this time.
