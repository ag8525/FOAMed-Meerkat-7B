**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of acute uncomplicated (Grade 1) appendicitis, focusing on the comparison between operative (appendectomy) and non-operative (antibiotic) treatments. Historically, appendectomy has been the standard treatment, but recent studies, including a large database analysis by Sceats et al., have explored non-operative management. The study found that while non-operative management had a lower failure rate than previously reported, it was associated with higher rates of abscess, readmissions, and overall costs, suggesting it may not be the preferred first-line therapy for all patients. The analysis highlighted a selection bias, as most patients underwent appendectomy, and noted that non-operative management might be more suitable for selected patients. The document emphasizes the importance of shared decision-making, considering patient preferences and clinical judgment, and concludes that while non-operative management is viable, it carries a small increase in complications.

**Case:**
An 18-year-old woman presents with a Grade 1 appendicitis (Tominaga et al J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2016).

**Background:**
Acute appendicitis is a condition characterized by inflammation of the appendix, traditionally treated with appendectomy, a surgical procedure first described by Charles McBurney in 1889. The safety of laparoscopic appendectomy has been demonstrated, with a study by Omar et al. (2008) reporting no deaths among over 230,000 UK patients under 49 years old. Despite the success of surgical intervention, there is ongoing debate about the potential for treating uncomplicated acute appendicitis with antibiotics, similar to the management of diverticulitis. However, two meta-analyses examining this approach have yielded conflicting results, highlighting the complexities inherent in evidence-based medicine.

**Clinical Point:**
Operative treatment or non-operative treatment of acute Grade 1 (uncomplicated) appendicitis?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Nonoperative management of acute uncomplicated appendicitis may be better than we thought in selected patients but comes with a cost of a small absolute increase in some complications.

**Case Resolution:**
She chooses to have her appendix removed, does well, and is discharged home that night.

**Clinical Application:**
Empower appropriately selected patients with the evidence and your clinical judgment. Engage them in shared decision-making and ask about their preference.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You are lucky because there are two pretty good options to treat appendicitis. Option one is to take your appendix out and you will be home later tonight and you should never have trouble again. The rate of a successful operation is really, really high. The price you will pay is three small holes in your belly and some pain over the next week or so. The other option, if you prefer, is to take oral antibiotics for one week. There is about a 95% success rate with a small risk of bouncing back to the hospital with appendicitis or an abscess. I think either option would work well for you.
