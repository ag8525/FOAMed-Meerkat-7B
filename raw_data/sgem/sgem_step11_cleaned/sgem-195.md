**Summary:**
In the emergency department, two patients with severe sepsis and cellulitis presented with differing body temperatures, highlighting the clinical debate on fever's role in infection. The study by Sundén-Cullberg et al. suggests that higher body temperatures in septic patients correlate with lower mortality and shorter hospital stays, challenging the perception that fever is solely detrimental. The observational study, involving 2,225 patients, found a 5% decrease in mortality for each degree increase in temperature, though it acknowledges potential confounders and measurement errors. The clinical implication is to prioritize afebrile septic patients, as they may have a higher risk of poor outcomes, necessitating rapid intervention and resource allocation. This reframes fever not as a marker of acuity but as a potential indicator of a more robust immune response.

**Case:**
You are working night shift in the emergency department. Two patients present to the front desk in close succession, brought in by concerned family members. Both are similarly hypotensive and tachypnoeic, drowsy but orientated, with hot, reddened and increasingly painful cellulitis. Following the triage sepsis pathway, recently amended to use qSOFA. The nurse triages both to beds in the acute/resuscitation area of the department. Each patient is seen quickly and has initial vital signs and blood samples obtained. One patient has a temperature of 38.3C and the other 35.5C. Being a night shift, staff resources are tight. How do you allocate resources wisely?

**Background:**
Fever is a common physiological response to infection, characterized by an elevated body temperature. Its clinical significance is debated, with one perspective suggesting that fever may increase metabolic stress and potentially harm patients with existing illnesses, while another view supports fever as a beneficial immune response aiding in infection control. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that fever alone does not pose a danger to generally healthy children and may be beneficial, emphasizing that the primary aim of antipyretic treatment should be to enhance the child's comfort and well-being rather than merely reducing body temperature.

**Clinical Point:**
Does patient body temperature in the emergency department predict survival of adult patients with severe sepsis and septic shock admitted to the intensive care unit?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We should pay greater attention to patients presenting with features of severe sepsis and septic shock that do not have fever in the emergency department as they have an associated high mortality rate.

**Case Resolution:**
The senior physician and two nurses attended to the initial treatment of the patient without a fever. He required rapid fluid administration and treatment with empirical antibiotics and initiation of vasopressor via peripheral cannula until a central line could be placed. The intensive care unit was involved early and facilitated transfer.
The gentleman with a fever received further assessment via the remaining nurse and a junior resident, responded to 30ml/kg of intravenous crystalloid, empirical antibiotics and was safely admitted under internal medicine to the ward in the early hours of the morning.

**Clinical Application:**
It is difficult to say what to do with this observational data. The clinical application may be to augment the triage of patients presenting with characteristics of severe sepsis or septic shock, flagging those without fever as having a greater risk of poor outcome and allocating resources/prioritizing accordingly. This could reframe the common perception that fever is a marker of acuity in sepsis.

**What do I tell My Patients?**
- **Patient Without Fever:** You are very sick with a serious skin infection. However, you do not seem to have the natural response of a fever and this makes us more concerned. We would like to treat you very rapidly and there will be a lot of activity in getting antibiotics, blood tests, giving you fluids and quite possibly medications to raise your blood pressure. We will be monitoring you very closely and will very likely get the Intensive Care team to come and see you.
- **Patient with a Fever:** You are very sick with a serious skin infection. Your body seems to be fighting it with a fever. Lowering your body temperature has not shown to save lives but it could make you feel better. The most important things right now are to get you intravenous antibiotics, intravenous fluids and admit you to the hospital.
