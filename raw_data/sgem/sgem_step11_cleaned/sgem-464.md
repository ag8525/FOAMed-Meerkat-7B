**Summary:**
The document discusses the prevalence of fear of enacted stigma among patients with Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) presenting to emergency departments (EDs) and the impact of ED staff compassion on this fear. A survey-based observational study involving 116 adult patients with OUD found that nearly all participants experienced some degree of enacted stigma, with a median stigma score indicating moderate-to-high levels of perceived stigma. The study concluded that increased compassion from ED staff is significantly associated with a reduction in the fear of enacted stigma, suggesting that fostering a compassionate environment could improve patient experiences and outcomes. The findings emphasize the importance of addressing stigma in healthcare settings to enhance care for patients with OUD.

**Case:**
You and your resident physician have been working hard all day taking care of patients, saving lives, and making a difference. You hear your resident groan. You ask her what’s the matter, and she says: *“I just picked up a patient with the chief complaint ‘abdominal pain,’ thinking it would be a simple, bread-and-butter workup, but the patient is on methadone. I don’t feel like dealing with this at the end of my shift.”* Your resident looks up at you intently, looking for answers and hoping for wisdom. You wonder what you can say to help motivate your resident the right way and bring your patient back to the center of the encounter.

**Background:**
Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) is a chronic, relapsing condition marked by a compulsive urge to use opioids despite adverse consequences, affecting millions globally and over six million in the United States as of 2022. It poses significant public health challenges, with emergency departments (EDs) often serving as initial contact points for opioid-related emergencies, such as overdoses and withdrawal. These settings are crucial for initiating treatment, providing education, and linking patients to long-term care. However, stigma in healthcare, particularly in EDs, remains a barrier, manifesting as discriminatory attitudes and reluctance to provide evidence-based treatments like medication-assisted therapy (MAT). This stigma, rooted in misconceptions of addiction as a moral failing, can deter individuals from seeking care. Education and empathy-focused interventions are promising strategies to reduce stigma, improve patient-provider relationships, and enhance care quality for individuals with OUD.

**Clinical Point:**
What is the prevalence of the fear of enacted stigma among patients who present to the ED with OUD, and is experiencing greater compassion from the ED staff associated with lowering this fear?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
There is fear of enacted stigma amongst patients with OUD. Increasing compassion seems to decrease fear of enacted stigma amongst OUD patients, particularly the ones who are presenting to the ED primarily for an OUD-related complaint.

**Case Resolution:**
You look at the resident, smile, and validate their feelings. They are human and can have feelings across the entire spectrum of emotions, but it’s important to take note of these feelings and how they may impact their patient care. You encourage them to read the new study that was just published on how patients with a history of opioid use disorder commonly have a fear of enacted stigma, especially the patients who are presenting for OUD-related complaints. This study also suggests increased compassion amongst the ED care team can decrease this fear. You encourage the residents to tap into the empathetic side of their humanity when caring for patients to help navigate their feelings. The resident takes a deep breath, says thank you for the information and perspective, and proceeds to take excellent care of the patient before signing them out to the overnight team!

**Clinical Application:**
The next time you and your team are caring for an OUD patient in the ED, reflect on how they may fear enacted stigma and how being more compassionate can help decrease this fear.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Not much. However, you discuss with the resident, other colleagues and staff how patients with OUD are fearful of being subjected to enacted stigma by the ED staff. You remind them that the ED is a light in the house of medicine, with an ever-shining ray of hope, always open to help all, particularly the more vulnerable and hopefully without judgement. By being more authentically compassionate, we can decrease the fear of enacted stigma amongst our patients who have battled the opioid crisis.
