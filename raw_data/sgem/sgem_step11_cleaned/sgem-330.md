**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of acute ischemic stroke, focusing on the use of mobile stroke units (MSUs) equipped with CT scanners and immediate thrombolysis capabilities compared to conventional care. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies, primarily from Germany, evaluated the outcomes of MSUs versus standard care. The findings indicated that while MSUs provided a statistically significant 13-minute reduction in time-to-treatment, they did not demonstrate a significant improvement in mortality or long-term neurologic outcomes. The study highlighted the lack of high-quality evidence supporting the efficacy of tPA and questioned the cost-effectiveness of MSUs given the uncertain benefits. The conclusion suggests that current evidence does not justify the widespread adoption of MSUs, and further high-quality research is needed to assess their utility.

**Case:**
A 70-year-old man develops sudden right-sided weakness beginning shortly after breakfast and his partner appropriately calls emergency medical services (EMS). Their local EMS service arrives quickly with a conventional ambulance. He has heard about these special ambulances with CT Scanners and wonders if that will make an important difference for his partner.

**Background:**
Stroke, specifically acute ischemic stroke, is a critical medical condition characterized by the sudden loss of blood flow to a part of the brain, leading to potential brain damage. The clinical significance of stroke is underscored by its prevalence and the urgency of treatment, encapsulated in the phrase "time is brain," which suggests that earlier intervention may prevent further brain injury. However, the evidence supporting this notion is not robust, as highlighted by the lack of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing early versus late administration of thrombolytics like tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). The largest RCT, IST-3, which evaluated tPA for acute ischemic stroke, faced several methodological issues, including a largely unblinded design, early termination, and reliance on self-reported outcomes. The trial did not demonstrate superiority for its primary outcome and showed a 4% absolute increase in early mortality. Subgroup analyses from IST-3 did not support the "time is brain" hypothesis, as no statistical difference was found between different time windows post-stroke onset, and the use of a 99% confidence interval further complicates the interpretation of results. Consequently, the recommendation for tPA in acute ischemic stroke has been downgraded to "yellow," indicating unclear benefits and harms due to data uncertainty.

**Clinical Point:**
Does a mobile stroke unit (MSU) with earlier imaging and delivery of tPA improve outcomes, or is the downstream effect of improved resources at a comprehensive stroke center that improves outcomes for patients with strokes?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
We cannot recommend the use of MSU based on the available evidence.

**Case Resolution:**
The man is taken to the closest stroke center and is worked up for acute ischemic stroke.

**Clinical Application:**
The evidence does not support local EMS agencies to purchase specialized ambulances with CT scanners to set up mobile stroke units.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your partner appears to be having a stroke. There are some places that have these special ambulances with CT scanners. The evidence shows they get people treated a few minutes faster. However, we don’t have good evidence those minutes make a clinical difference.
