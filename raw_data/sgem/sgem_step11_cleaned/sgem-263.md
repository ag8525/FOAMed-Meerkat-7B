**Summary:**
The document discusses a study evaluating the effectiveness of an enhanced intervention using audit and feedback, peer comparisons, and nudges to reduce inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory infections (ARIs) in emergency departments (EDs) and urgent care centers (UCCs). The study involved 292 clinicians across nine sites and found that both adaptive and enhanced interventions significantly reduced inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, with a decrease from 6.2% to 2.4%. However, the enhanced intervention did not show a significant advantage over the adapted intervention in high-performance settings. The study highlights the challenges of implementing antibiotic stewardship in busy clinical environments and the potential for contamination and Hawthorne effects. It emphasizes the importance of using absolute effect sizes for reporting scientific findings and suggests that strategies to reduce unnecessary antibiotic use are feasible and effective in ED and UCC settings.

**Case:**
A 25-year-old female presents to the urgent care with two days of cough, purulent sputum, fever, and myalgias. Vital signs are within normal limits and her exam is unremarkable. She asks for a prescription for antibiotics to help treat her infection.

**Background:**
Inappropriate antibiotic use, particularly for acute respiratory infections (ARIs), is a significant concern in emergency departments (EDs) and urgent care centers (UCCs) in the United States, where approximately half of the 10 million annual antibiotic prescriptions are deemed inappropriate. This misuse contributes to the rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, opportunistic infections, and adverse drug events. Despite the recognized need for antibiotic stewardship, EDs face challenges such as frequent interruptions, overcrowding, and high patient volumes, which hinder practice changes. Evidence supports the use of feedback, nudges, and peer comparisons to improve prescribing practices, with studies indicating that these interventions can sustainably reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions in primary care settings.

**Clinical Point:**
Is an enhanced intervention using audit and feedback, peer comparisons, and nudges more effective than a standard intervention in reducing inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory infections by clinicians in an ED/UCC setting?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Consider implementing strategies to reduce inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in your ED or UCC.

**Case Resolution:**
After completing your history and physical examination, you conclude that this patient has a viral illness and do not prescribe an antibiotic.

**Clinical Application:**
This study provides strategies that could be tried to reduce unnecessary antibiotics for acute respiratory infections in the ED and UCC.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your history, reassuring vital signs, and examination do not show any evidence of an infection requiring antibiotics. If anything, they may lead to harm, such as diarrhea, stomach upset, rashes, and even nasty intestinal infections are possible. At this point, you are likely to improve with fluids, rest, and ibuprofen/acetaminophen for your fever and muscle aches. If you are developing significantly worsening shortness of breath or your fever is persistent after another few days, you should be re-assessed.
