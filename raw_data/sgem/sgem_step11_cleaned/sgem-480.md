**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of pediatric agitation in emergency settings, focusing on a randomized controlled trial comparing oral olanzapine and diazepam for sedation in children with acute severe behavioral disturbances. The study found no significant difference in efficacy between the two medications, with approximately 40% of participants in each group not achieving successful sedation. Both medications were well-tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported. The trial highlights the importance of ruling out organic causes of agitation, employing non-pharmacologic de-escalation techniques, and considering patient preferences in medication administration. The study also emphasizes the need for awareness of potential biases in treatment decisions and the importance of involving caregivers in the management process.

**Case:**
A 14-year-old girl with no known medical or psychiatric history presents to the emergency department (ED) with her family for aggression. Her parents tell you that they have been getting into arguments a lot recently. Today, she became so angry that she started punching and kicking the walls at home. You interview the girl and perform your physical examination, and determine that there are likely no medical diagnoses contributing to her aggression, nor that she has sustained any injuries requiring immediate management. After you leave the room and her parents enter, you hear them get into another argument, and she gets more agitated. The staff try a combination of de-escalation techniques, but she continues to be aggressive and starts threatening the staff. A nurse working with you asks, “I don’t think our de-escalation techniques are working. Do you want to give her something to help calm her down? We have olanzapine or diazepam here. Which one do you want to give?”

**Background:**
Pediatric agitation is a clinical condition marked by increased motor activity, emotional arousal, and often aggressive or disruptive behavior beyond typical developmental expectations. It can arise from psychiatric disorders, medical conditions such as delirium or metabolic disturbances, substance issues, or situational stressors like hospitalization. In emergency settings, pediatric agitation poses significant challenges, potentially compromising safety and delaying care. Initial assessment should rule out medical causes like hypoglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis, as overlooking these can be hazardous. Once organic causes are excluded, early recognition and de-escalation are crucial, employing non-pharmacologic strategies such as environmental adjustments and supportive communication. Physical restraints or medications are considered only when necessary, with oral medications preferred to minimize trauma. If oral administration is not feasible, intramuscular options may be used, guided by local protocols and the specific cause of agitation. Medications like antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, antihistamines, or ketamine are selected based on the clinical context, aiming to alleviate distress while ensuring safety and respect for the child and family.

**Clinical Point:**
Is oral olanzapine or oral diazepam more effective in achieving successful sedation in pediatric patients with acute severe behavioral disturbances?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Both oral olanzapine and oral diazepam are similarly effective (or ineffective) for achieving sedation in agitated pediatric patients.

**Case Resolution:**
You speak with the patient and ask if she’d be open to taking a medication to help her feel calmer. She agrees. When you ask if she’s able to take pills, she shakes her head no. You offer her an orally dissolving dose of olanzapine while continuing to use verbal de-escalation techniques. Over time, she becomes calmer and more open, eventually willing to talk about what’s been going on.

Taking medication for agitation is a lot like taking something for pain. It’s about treating something that’s bothering you, not about “drugging” or tricking anyone. Framing it this way gives the patient a sense of choice and control in a moment when so much feels out of their hands.

**Clinical Application:**
Many medications can be considered for managing agitation in pediatric patients. It’s important to first identify and address any underlying factors contributing to the agitation, and to start with non-pharmacologic strategies and de-escalation techniques. When medication is necessary, involve the child’s parent or guardian in the decision-making process.

Interventions that are carefully chosen, compassionately delivered, and well-documented might still fall short for some families. Remain thoughtful about the risks and benefits of each medication we use, and open to learning from the experiences of our patients, even when the outcomes challenge our expectations.

**What Do I Tell the Nurse?**
There’s no evidence that olanzapine or diazepam works better than the other. Let’s first see whether the patient is willing to take an oral medication. If she agrees, we can see whether it’s preferable to give a medication that is dissolvable or must be swallowed.
