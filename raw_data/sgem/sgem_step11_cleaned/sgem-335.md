**Summary:**
In the emergency department setting, self-obtained vaginal swabs (SOVS) have been shown to be noninferior to provider-performed endocervical sampling (PPES) for diagnosing *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and *Chlamydia trachomatis* using rapid nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT). This study, involving 515 patients, demonstrated a 95% sensitivity for SOVS, with excellent agreement (kappa = 0.93) between SOVS and PPES results. The study also highlighted high patient acceptability for SOVS, with 93% of participants finding it acceptable, although 28% expressed concerns about performing it correctly. The findings support offering SOVS as a viable alternative to pelvic exams for STI testing in the ED, potentially reducing patient discomfort and expediting care.

**Case:**
A 31-year-old female presents to the emergency department with pelvic pain and vaginal discharge for the past 24 hours. She is afebrile, vital signs are normal and she is having a significant amount of pain. She says the pain is so severe that she cannot even imagine having a pelvic exam done right now for STI testing.

**Background:**
Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis are the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections in the United States, with an increasing number of cases diagnosed in emergency departments. The standard diagnostic method is nucleic acid amplification testing using provider-performed endocervical sampling, which is uncomfortable for patients and requires specific resources, potentially causing delays in busy emergency settings. The necessity of universal pelvic examinations for STI testing in emergency departments is being questioned. Self-obtained vaginal swabs, initially developed for non-emergency settings, have shown comparable sensitivity to provider-performed sampling and high patient acceptability. This study evaluates the effectiveness and patient preference of self-obtained vaginal swabs versus provider-performed endocervical sampling in an emergency department context.

**Clinical Point:**
Do self-obtained swabs have noninferior sensitivity for the diagnosis of NG/CT compared to provider performed swabs in an ED setting using a rapid NAAT.

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Consider the use of self-obtained vaginal swabs for STI testing and the necessity of performing a pelvic exam for patients presenting with clinical suspicion for NG/CT.

**Case Resolution:**
You provide your patient with analgesic for her pain and ask if she would prefer a self-obtained vaginal swab or for you to perform a pelvic exam to obtain a swab. She chooses to perform the swab herself, and you treat her empirically for an NG/CT.

**Clinical Application:**
This was an excellent study that supports the use of self-obtained vaginal swabs for STI testing in the ED and can reduce uncomfortable pelvic exams for patients. It is reasonable to offer this option to patients presenting to the ED who clinically require a swab for STI testing but there is no other need for a pelvic examination.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I am concerned that you may have an STI. A vaginal swab should be done to test for this infection. Doing a vaginal swab by yourself is just as accurate as having me perform a pelvic exam. It will also be faster and probably less painful for you. I understand if you are uncomfortable doing it yourself. I am happy to do the exam and get the swab sample. It is your choice and both options are fine.
