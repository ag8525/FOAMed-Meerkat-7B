**Summary:**
The document discusses a case of a 74-year-old man with acute ischemic stroke, focusing on the use of MRI-guided thrombolysis with low-dose alteplase (0.6 mg/kg) for strokes of unknown onset, as explored in the THAWS trial. The trial's subgroup analysis suggested potential benefits in patients with DWI-ASPECTS scores of 5 to 8, although the study was stopped early and lacked a placebo control, raising concerns about bias and the reliability of the findings. The broader context includes skepticism about the efficacy of thrombolysis in acute ischemic stroke, with mixed results from foundational trials and concerns about early study termination and conflicts of interest. The document emphasizes the need for further research to confirm these findings and advises caution in interpreting subgroup analyses as definitive evidence.

**Case:**
A 74-year-old man presents to the emergency department after waking up with left sided weakness. He was last seen well when going to bed at 10pm the night before. He has a history of hypertension and dyslipidemia. His medications include an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and a statin. The NIHSS score is 7. The MRI shows an occlusion of the right MCA-M2, the DWI-ASPECT is 9, and lesion volume is 3.5ml.

**Background:**
Thrombolysis for acute ischemic stroke has been extensively studied, with 13 foundational trials conducted; however, only two, ECASS-3 and the NINDS trial, demonstrated benefit for their primary outcomes, though both also highlighted potential harm. Recent trials have explored extending the treatment window beyond 4.5 hours using advanced imaging, but these were stopped early, potentially biasing results. Endovascular therapy (EVT) for clot retrieval, which many patients in these extended window trials would qualify for, has stronger evidence for efficacy and safety compared to systemic thrombolysis. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Mistry et al. included both RCTs and observational studies, showing a number needed to treat (NNT) of 17 for a good neurologic outcome, but no significant difference when only high-quality RCT data were considered. Recent RCTs, including those by Yang P et al., Suzuki et al., and Zi et al., have investigated the efficacy of EVT alone versus EVT plus tPA, with mixed results regarding non-inferiority. The THAWS trial is a sub-analysis focusing on low-dose tPA in patients with stroke symptoms upon waking or with an unknown onset time.

**Clinical Point:**
Is MRI guided thrombolysis (0.6 mg/kg) beneficial for patients with an unknown stroke onset time?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
This subgroup analysis of the THAWS data is an interesting finding but should be considered hypothesis generating and would need to be confirmed in a proper trial.

**Case Resolution:**
The stroke service is called, and the patient is admitted to the stroke unit under their care.

**Clinical Application:**
The kindest interpretation of this subgroup analysis of a small, unblinded trial that was “*negative*”, that was stopped early, is that it is hypothesis generating. The literature on replication of hypothesis generating subgroup analyses indicates that pursuit of this is unlikely, and if achieved, rarely reproduced.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You’ve had a stroke. It was caused by a blockage to one of the blood vessels in the brain. All strokes are bad but this one is at the milder end of the spectrum. We’re going to call the stroke team who will probably admit you to the stroke unit for therapy to help get you better.
