**Summary:**
The document discusses the management of primary spontaneous pneumothorax, particularly focusing on whether conservative management is non-inferior to interventional treatment with chest tube insertion. The study by Brown et al. in NEJM 2020 evaluated patients aged 14 to 50 with a unilateral primary spontaneous pneumothorax of 32% or more, excluding those with secondary pneumothorax or other complicating factors. The trial found that conservative management was non-inferior to interventional management, with a primary outcome of complete radiographic resolution within eight weeks achieved in 94.4% of the conservative group compared to 98.5% in the intervention group. The study suggests that conservative management can be a viable option, reducing the risk of serious adverse events associated with chest tube insertion, provided there is close follow-up. The authors concluded that conservative management is reasonable for large first-time spontaneous pneumothoraxes, aligning with practices that avoid hospital admission and use of Heimlich valves for outpatient management.

**Case:**
A 49-year-old healthy male electrician presents to the emergency room with right chest pain and dyspnea. The work-up reveals a diagnosis of a right pneumothorax confirmed by chest x-ray (CXR). What do you do?

**Background:**
A pneumothorax is a medical condition characterized by the presence of air in the pleural space, which can be classified as either primary, occurring in healthy individuals, or secondary, associated with underlying lung disease. It is a common emergency department presentation. The standard treatment for primary spontaneous pneumothorax often involves interventional drainage, which may escalate to surgical intervention. However, chest tube insertion, a common procedure, carries risks such as pain, organ injury, bleeding, and infection. An alternative management strategy is conservative treatment, reserving intervention for cases where the pneumothorax becomes physiologically significant.

**Clinical Point:**
Does everyone with a large first-time spontaneous pneumothorax need a chest tube?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
It is reasonable to provide conservative management in a patient with large first-time spontaneous pneumothoraxes as long as you can ensure close follow-up.

**Case Resolution:**
You enter a shared decision-making process with the patient on whether or not to place a chest tube. Part of that is to support whatever decision he chooses. He decides to go with conservative management and will follow-up in the clinic.

**Clinical Application:**
This new evidence provides more confidence in our practice of treating some of these patients with patients with a first large spontaneous pneumothoraxes conservatively.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have a collapse of your lung. This can happen randomly in some people. It seems to be stable and not getting worse. We are going to keep an eye on you and not put a tube in your chest. These tubes have been used before to let the lung re-inflate. While a chest tube is a very safe procedure, there are always some risks. New research shows more than 90% of patients will be fine without a tube in their chest. If the repeat CXR in about four hours is ok, we will send you home with follow-up with your friendly Thoracik surgeon. You can come back to the emergency department at any time if you are feeling worse or are concerned.
