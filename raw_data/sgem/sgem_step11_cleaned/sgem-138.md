**Summary:**
In the case of a 75-year-old woman with a femoral neck fracture, regional nerve blocks, such as the femoral nerve block (FNB), 3-in-1 FNB, and fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB), are highlighted as effective alternatives to standard opiate-based pain management. These blocks have been shown to reduce pain scores and decrease the need for parenteral opioids, with no life-threatening complications reported, although minor complications may increase. Despite the moderate to high risk of bias in the studies reviewed, the systematic review suggests that regional nerve blocks can be recommended for managing pain in hip fractures, with a call for further high-quality randomized controlled trials to better assess safety. In clinical practice, offering regional nerve blocks can be a viable option for patients, potentially reducing opioid-related side effects.

**Case:**
A 75-year-old woman has a ground level fall in her apartment. She is brought to the emergency department with an isolated hip injury. She has a past medical history of high blood pressure and gastroesophageal reflex disease. Her only complaint is hip pain. On exam her vital signs are normal. The only abnormality found is a shortened and externally rotated left leg. An x-ray demonstrates a femoral neck fracture. The nurse wants to know what you want to give her for pain.

**Background:**
Oligoanalgesia, defined as inadequate pain control, is a significant issue in emergency departments, particularly affecting the elderly who are at risk due to conditions like hip fractures. These fractures are prevalent in the elderly and are associated with high morbidity and mortality rates. Pain management in this demographic is complicated by the potential for increased complications from opioid use. To address pain in hip fractures, various regional nerve blocks are employed. The Traditional Femoral Nerve Block (FNB) involves injecting local anesthetic around the femoral nerve in the groin. The 3-in-1 Femoral Nerve Block enhances this by applying pressure distal to the needle, allowing the anesthetic to also affect the obturator and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves. The Fascia Iliaca Compartment Block (FICB) indirectly anesthetizes the same three nerves by injecting a large volume of dilute anesthetic into the fascia iliaca compartment.

**Clinical Point:**
Do regional nerve blocks effectively reduce pain, decrease opiate use and are they safe compared to standard pain management in patients with hip or femoral neck fractures?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
While the evidence comes from small studies with a moderate to high risk of bias, femoral nerve blocks appear to be an effective alternative to standard treatment of pain associated with femoral neck or hip fracture in the emergency department. More high-quality studies are needed to comment strongly about safety.

**Case Resolution:**
You tell the nurse to start an intravenous line and you are going to talk to the patient about her pain control options. These will include intravenous opiates and/or a regional nerve block.

**Clinical Application:**
I am going to offer regional nerve blocks to patients who present with hip or femoral neck fractures.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have broken your hip. This is a very painful injury. We can give you pain medicine like morphine. These drugs work very well for pain but can make you sick to your stomach, hallucinate, and drop your blood pressure. Another option is to inject some “freezing” in the hip that blocks the nerve. This usually works very well with few complications. It often means you do not need as much pain medication. Would you like me to do this type of nerve block?
