**Summary:**
The document discusses the SPEED protocol, a sonographic approach for diagnosing acute aortic dissection (AoD) in emergency settings, particularly when CT imaging is unavailable. AoD, part of the acute aortic syndrome, is classified into Stanford Type A and B, with Type A being more dangerous due to potential complications like cardiac tamponade. The study evaluates the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS (point-of-care ultrasound) performed by emergency medicine residents, focusing on three sonographic findings: pericardial effusion, intimal flap, and aortic outflow track diameter. The SPEED protocol demonstrated a sensitivity of 93.2% for detecting AoD, though the confidence interval was wide due to the low incidence of cases. The study suggests that while POCUS can be a valuable tool in diagnosing AoD, further validation in diverse clinical settings is necessary.

**Case:**
A 59-year-old man walks into your community emergency department (ED) complaining of chest pain. It is described as a ripping sensation that radiates to his back. His vital signs are all normal and the ECG done at triage does not show an occlusive myocardial infarction. The chest x-ray is unremarkable, and his troponin is not elevated. You suspect an acute aortic dissection (AoD). However, your CT scanner is offline for two hours of scheduled maintenance. He will need to be transferred to the tertiary care center which is 35 minutes away by ground EMS if it is a dissection. Your Spidey senses are tingling, and you don’t want to wait for the CT scanner to be back online to make the diagnosis. Arrangements are made for him to be transferred stat to the tertiary hospital while he is still stable. You wonder if a quick POCUS examination looking for three sonographic findings while waiting for the paramedics could help determine the likelihood of this being an AoD.

**Background:**
Acute aortic syndrome (AAS) encompasses aortic dissection (AD), intramural hematoma (IMH), and penetrating aortic ulcer, and is a rare but life-threatening condition often presenting atypically, leading to diagnostic delays and increased mortality. Acute aortic dissection (AoD), a component of AAS, is classified by the Stanford system into Type A and Type B based on the tear's location, guiding treatment strategies. Type A dissections involve the ascending aorta and are more common and dangerous, potentially causing complications like cardiac tamponade, aortic valve insufficiency, or myocardial infarction, necessitating immediate surgical intervention. Type B dissections occur in the descending aorta and, while less common and immediately life-threatening, still pose significant risks if untreated. Rapid diagnosis is crucial due to the high mortality associated with delays, yet clinical decision tools are not recommended for routine use in suspected AoD cases, aligning with the American College of Emergency Physicians' guidelines.

**Clinical Application:**
