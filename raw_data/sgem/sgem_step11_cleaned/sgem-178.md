**Summary:**
A randomized controlled trial by Ireland et al. investigated the impact of a ten-week mindfulness training intervention on stress and burnout among intern doctors during their emergency department rotation. The study found significant reductions in both burnout and stress levels, as measured by the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory and the Perceived Stress Scale, respectively. Despite limitations such as lack of blinding and potential baseline differences, the results suggest that mindfulness interventions can equip medical practitioners with skills to manage stress and burnout effectively. The authors recommend incorporating mindfulness training into medical education, although further research is needed to assess its clinical impact on outcomes like medical errors and mental health issues. The case resolution highlights the positive personal and professional changes experienced by a resident who adopted a daily mindfulness practice.

**Case:**
A resident comes to you looking for advice. He is having trouble feeling tired, short-tempered and it is affecting his work interactions and personal sense of satisfaction with his job. You suspect he is suffering from early burnout.

**Background:**
Burnout, a term introduced by Herbert Freudenberger in 1974, is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, as defined by the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI). It is particularly prevalent among physicians, with over 50% of US physicians experiencing at least one symptom, and emergency physicians reporting the highest prevalence at approximately 70%. Burnout can lead to severe consequences such as depression, suicidal ideation, illness, increased alcohol use, and negatively impact patient care through increased medical errors and reduced quality of care. Mindfulness-based interventions, such as the Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program developed by Dr. Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979, have been shown to effectively reduce burnout. Mindfulness, which involves present-moment awareness and non-judgmental observation of internal and external experiences, has historical roots in Buddhist meditation practices and has gained significant attention in Western medicine as a therapeutic approach.

**Clinical Point:**
Can a mindfulness program reduce stress and burnout among interns on an emergency medicine rotation?

**SGEM Bottom Line:**
Mindfulness meditation is something to consider when dealing with the stress of an emergency medicine rotation.

**Case Resolution:**
The resident who was suffering early burnout had a clear and remarkable benefit from starting a daily, app-based mindfulness and meditation practice. Everyone who works with him noticed the change (without knowing why he was different), and he tells me the practice is now a vital and required part of his daily routine.

**Clinical Application:**
We are not sure if it has a clinical impact at this point. More studies are needed, but a calmer and happier doctor should logically translate into a better clinical impact.

**What do I tell the Resident?**
Thank you for letting me know you are having difficulties coping. Residency is stressful and you are not the only one who has had trouble with stress, but there are ways to mitigate the stress and stay healthy. In addition to eating well, getting enough sleep, exercising regularly, and staying socially connected, mindfulness meditation may help.
