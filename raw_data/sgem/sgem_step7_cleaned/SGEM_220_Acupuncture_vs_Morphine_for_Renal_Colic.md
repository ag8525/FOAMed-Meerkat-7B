**Case:** A 51-year-old man presents to the emergency department complaining of right flank pain radiating to his groin like his previous episodes of renal colic. He states the pain comes in “waves,” is associated with nausea but no vomiting. On exam, he is afebrile and appears very uncomfortable while grabbing his right flank. He has used ketorolac, acetaminophen, and tamsulosin in the past. He really wants to avoid taking any opioids and is wondering if acupuncture could work?

**Background:**
- Expulsive therapy is unnecessary for ureteric stones < 5mm.
- There is some weak evidence that tamsulosin may help passage of larger stones (5 to 10 mm).
- You don’t need to push fluids (oral/IV) or use diuretics to pass kidney stones.
- Bedside emergency department ultrasound is safe and has several advantages over CT for the diagnosis of kidney stones.
- Lidocaine cannot be recommended for the treatment of renal colic at this time.
- There is no high-quality evidence that acupuncture works for patients presenting to the emergency department with back pain, ankle sprains, or migraines.
- The study on acupuncture to prevent the re-occurrence of migraine headaches in patients without aura does not provide any evidence of the efficacy.
- The authors of the trial we are going to look at today say that acupuncture has been proven to have efficacy in treating renal colic pain.

**Clinical Question:** In adult patients presenting to the emergency department with renal colic, is acupuncture superior to morphine for pain control?

**Reference:** Beltaief K et al. Acupuncture versus titrated morphine in acute renal colic: a randomized controlled trial. J Pain Res. 2018

- **Population:** Patients greater than 18 years old presenting to a Tunisian emergency department with clinical suspicion of uncomplicated renal colic and a pain score greater than 70 (out of 100) on visual analog scale (VAS).
  - **Exclusion:** Complicated renal colic (bilateral pain, fever, and/or decreased urine output), traumatic pain, on anticoagulants, skin afflictions, unable to assess VAS, analgesics in 6 hours prior, refusal of consent and pregnant women.
- **Intervention:** 30 minutes of acupuncture seating needles until deqi (numbness and tingling) was achieved using the urinary bladder meridian points.
- **Comparison:** Titrated morphine chloral hydrate using a bolus of 0.1 mg/kg every five minutes until pain score reached 50% of baseline.
- **Outcome:** 
  - **Primary Outcome(s):** Success was a composite outcome of durability and rapidity
    - **Durability:** Drop in the VAS of at least 50% from baseline at 30 minutes and lasting to 60 minutes.
    - **Rapidity:** Time to a drop in the VAS of at least 50% from baseline.
  - **Adverse Events:**
    - **Intervention:** Local rash/bleeding, itching, needle blockage, and fainting.
    - **Control:** Drowsiness, dizziness, nausea and vomiting, respiratory distress, and hypotension.

**Authors’ Conclusions:** "In ED patients with renal colic, acupuncture was associated with a much faster and deeper analgesic effect and a better tolerance profile in comparison with titrated IV morphine.”

**Key Results:** They consecutively recruited 119 patients to be included in this trial. The mean age was in the low 40’s with about a 50/50 split between men and women.

No difference in success rate but a statistical difference in resolution time favoring acupuncture.

**Commentary:**

**1. There were some problems with the result section of the paper:**
- **Success:** No difference between groups (87% acupuncture vs. 83% morphine p=0.6)
  - **Durability:** Drop in the VAS of at least 50% from baseline at 30 minutes and lasting to 60 minutes – No statistical difference
  - **Rapidity:** Time to a drop in the VAS of at least 50% from baseline (14.5 +/- 7.8 vs 28.2 +/- 12.4 minutes, *P*<0.001)
- **Adverse Events:**
  - Less adverse events in the acupuncture group (3) vs. the morphine group (42)
  - No serious adverse events reported

**2. Randomization and Concealment:** It's critical to have **concealed randomization** in a clinical trial. In this study, there's a potential that randomization wasn't properly concealed. Although the researchers used sealed envelopes, they didn't specify if the envelopes were opaque. This oversight opens up the possibility that the envelopes could have been opened and resealed or held up to a light to breach the randomization process. An electronic randomization system is a much better method for assigning patients to study arms.

Furthermore, even if the randomization process was concealed, there was **no blinding** of the participants or the providers. This absolutely introduces **bias** into the study, such as the **placebo effect** and expectation bias.

**3. Baseline Characteristics and External Validity:** The study reported that common baseline patient characteristics were similar between the groups. However, a key characteristic they didn't report was whether the patients had used acupuncture before or their personal beliefs about this treatment modality. Given the strong placebo effect often seen in acupuncture studies, this should have been investigated and controlled for in the trial. Notably, 15 out of 153 consecutive patients refused to participate. Could this be because they didn't believe in acupuncture and didn't want to risk being randomized to that group?

This trial was conducted in Tunisia. The cultural acceptance of acupuncture in Tunisia may be different than that in North America, which could limit the study's **external validity**. Cultural factors may influence the applicability of these findings to North American patients, who may be conditioned differently or hold inherent biases about acupuncture's effects. A belief in the treatment is a key component of the placebo effect. Would the same trial produce the same results if conducted in North America or in a society that is generally more skeptical of acupuncture?

**4. Outcomes:** The study's outcome measures were somewhat confusing. It wasn't immediately clear what the primary outcome was. The authors state the main outcome was "success," which was defined by two components, making it a **composite outcome**. Composite outcomes can make it easier to show a statistically significant result because you're measuring more than one thing. However, in this case, the results didn't show a statistical difference between the two groups (87% success in the acupuncture group vs. 83% in the morphine group).

When the authors analyzed the two components of the composite outcome separately, they did show significant differences in the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score at 60 minutes and in the mean time to pain resolution. We will discuss that a bit more in the fourth and fifth nerdy points.

Regarding **adverse events**, there were many more in the morphine group, with the vast majority being dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. None of these events were considered serious, and no patients appeared to drop out of the trial because of them. The researchers could have also asked patients in the morphine group if the **side effects** of the treatment were bothersome.

**5. Statistical vs. Clinical Significance:** It is generally accepted in pain research that a **13 mm change** on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is the minimum amount required to be considered **clinically significant** (Gallagher et al., *AEM* 2001). Given that the study was unblinded and may have had unbalanced groups, the observed difference could represent bias in addition to the random noise present in any dataset.

There are ways to further explore the issue of **statistical versus clinical significance**. For instance, researchers could have asked the patients questions to determine if the outcomes were patient-oriented:
* Were you satisfied with your pain reduction?
* Which treatment group would you have preferred?
* If you had another attack of renal colic, would you want the same treatment?

These questions could have helped clarify whether the observed differences were truly meaningful to the patients.

**6. Comparison Group:** Was the control group a **straw man comparison**? It's unclear if the "morphine chloral hydrate" dosing is equivalent to morphine sulfate, but a total dose of 16.7 mg seems larger than what is typically used in the emergency department for renal colic.

Furthermore, the study design may have favored acupuncture. The acupuncture treatment started at time zero and continued for a 30-minute session, allowing the **placebo effect** to begin immediately and be sustained. In contrast, the control group received intermittent contact as morphine was titrated every five minutes. Could this delayed interaction have shifted the control group's results to the right on the time axis?

A visual inspection of the graph's x-axis also raises questions. The interval between 0 and 10 minutes (a 10-minute span) appears to be the same length as the interval between 45 and 60 minutes (a 15-minute span). The slopes of the two treatment lines look the same, and it appears that if you shifted the morphine results to the left by 10 minutes, its line would overlap the acupuncture line.

The **Achilles' heel** of this trial was the lack of a **sham acupuncture group**. It has been demonstrated that there is often no difference between "*true*" acupuncture and sham acupuncture. One trial for back pain showed that simply pressing toothpicks against a patient's back achieved the same efficacy as real acupuncture (Cherkin et al., *Arch Intern Med* 2009). Without a sham acupuncture group, it is impossible to minimize and account for the placebo effect.

**Comment on Authors’ Conclusion Compared to SGEM Conclusion:** We disagree with the authors’ conclusions. It was a flawed study, it does not represent a place in the emergency department analgesic armamentarium and does not deserve to be further investigated due to a lack of biological plausibility.

**SGEM Bottom Line:** This trial does not support the claim that acupuncture works or is superior to morphine for adult patients presenting to the emergency department.

**Case Resolution:** You give 10mg IV ketorolac for the pain and 8mg IV of ondansetron for the nausea. He is re-assessed in 30 minutes without much relief. You discuss using sub-dissociated doses of ketamine to minimize the amount of opioid. He agrees and ends up having his pain resolved.

**Clinical Application:** Acupuncture is based on meridians that have never been proven to exist. This seriously calls into question the biological plausibility of manipulating meridians to improve qi (chi). The Bayesian pre-test probability of acupuncture working is very low and would require a large effect size to demonstrate efficacy. This is another low-quality study that does not demonstrate acupuncture is useful for acute pain. It is wrong to use the serious opioid problem to justify a therapy which has not been proven to work.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?** It would be great if acupuncture had been shown to work in renal colic. Unfortunately, there is no good evidence to support it being effective. We will try to get your pain down using medications proven to work and even have some new options available that can minimize opioid use.
