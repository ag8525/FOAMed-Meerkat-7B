## Summary

In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted in central Denmark, the administration of intravenous or intraosseous calcium was evaluated against saline in adults experiencing out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. The study aimed to determine if calcium could improve the sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). Despite calcium's known inotropic and vasopressor effects, the trial found no significant improvement in ROSC with calcium administration compared to saline, with a primary outcome risk ratio of 0.72 (p = 0.09). Secondary outcomes, including survival at 30 and 90 days and neurological outcomes, also showed no significant benefit and suggested potential harm. The trial's early termination and regional specificity were noted as limitations. Overall, the findings do not support the use of calcium in this context, highlighting the need for cautious consideration of its use in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest scenarios.

## Clinical Point

In patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, does the administration of calcium compared with placebo improve sustained return of spontaneous circulation?

## Background

Calcium plays a crucial role in myocardial contractility, exhibiting both inotropic and vasopressor effects. Previous small RCTs in 1985 suggested potential benefits of calcium in cardiac arrest, though results were not statistically significant.

## Bottom Line

In patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, calcium administration compared with saline did not significantly improve sustained return of spontaneous circulation. Point estimates suggest potential harm from calcium use in this population.
