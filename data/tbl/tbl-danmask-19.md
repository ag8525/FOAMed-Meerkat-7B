## Summary

The DANMASK-19 trial investigated whether recommending surgical mask use to adults in Denmark, who spent significant time outside during the early COVID-19 pandemic, reduced SARS-CoV-2 infection rates. Conducted as a non-blinded, randomized controlled trial, participants were divided into a mask group, provided with surgical masks, and a control group with no mask recommendation. The primary outcome showed no significant difference in infection rates between the groups, with an odds ratio of 0.82. Despite being adequately powered and having a high completion rate, the study's limitations include potential selection bias, short follow-up, and lack of ethical oversight. The authors concluded that mask recommendations did not significantly protect wearers, but the study was not designed to assess masks' effectiveness in source control, and its methodological flaws limit its utility in guiding public health policy.

## Clinical Point

In adults in Denmark who spent three hours or more outside the home during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, did a recommendation to wear a surgical face mask, compared with no recommendation, reduce SARS-CoV-2 infection at one month?

## Background

- SARS-CoV-2 is primarily transmitted through droplets and aerosols.
- Observational studies suggest universal mask-wearing reduces transmission.
- The study aimed to assess if wearing a surgical mask protects the wearer from infection.

## Bottom Line

This trial did not demonstrate a protective effect of masks against SARS-CoV-2 infection in a society without a mask mandate. Methodological flaws mean it should not guide public health policy. The study does not address masks' impact on source control.
