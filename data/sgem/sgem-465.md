## Summary
The document discusses the limitations of single-center randomized controlled trials (sRCTs) in emergency medicine, particularly their failure to replicate mortality benefits in multi-center randomized controlled trials (mRCTs). A systematic review by Kotani et al. found that only 6% of sRCTs' mortality reduction findings were confirmed by mRCTs, highlighting the need for caution in using sRCT results to inform clinical guidelines. The review emphasizes the importance of external validity and the potential for publication bias, which can skew evidence and decision-making. The document advises skepticism towards sRCT conclusions unless conditions can be precisely replicated, and it underscores the necessity of integrating clinical judgment and patient preferences with evidence-based medicine.

## Background
Single-center randomized controlled trials (sRCTs) play a crucial role in emergency medicine by providing a controlled environment to test medical interventions, though their findings may have limited applicability due to their localized context. To ensure broader clinical relevance, multi-center randomized controlled trials (mRCTs) are often conducted to validate these findings across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings, addressing the external validity essential for evidence-based practice. The transition from sRCTs to mRCTs is necessary because different institutions have varied patient demographics, resources, and protocols that can affect outcomes. While sRCTs offer valuable insights, their ability to mirror real-world complexities is limited, making mRCTs important for guiding clinical decisions in emergency medicine. Despite the hierarchical preference for mRCTs, there is ongoing debate about their consistency in confirming sRCT results, which is critical for generalizing findings and integrating them into clinical guidelines. Emergency physicians must evaluate the relationship between sRCTs and mRCTs to assess evidence reliability and inform intervention implementation.

## Clinical Point
How often are single-centred RCTs of critically ill patients reporting a mortality benefit confirmed in a multi-centred RCT?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Be skeptical of accepting the conclusions of sRCT unless you can precisely duplicate the conditions that led to positive sRCT results.

## Clinical Application
sRCT reporting a mortality benefit in critically ill patients often does not replicate in mRCTs and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. They probably should only be used to change clinical practice if you know exactly what went into the intervention, especially behind the scenes, and can precisely replicate those methods.
