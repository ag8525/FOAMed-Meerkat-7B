## Summary
In evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of clinical decision rules versus physician judgment for identifying clinically important traumatic brain injuries (TBI) in children with minor head injuries, the study compared PECARN, CATCH, CHALICE, and physician practices. Conducted at a level 2 pediatric trauma center, the study found that only PECARN and physician practice achieved 100% sensitivity in detecting clinically important TBIs, with PECARN being slightly more specific. CHALICE was the most specific but less sensitive, while CATCH had the poorest specificity. The study highlighted the importance of integrating clinical decision tools with physician judgment and patient preferences, acknowledging the limitations and potential biases in the study, such as selection and verification biases. The findings suggest that while decision tools are valuable, they should complement rather than replace clinical judgment in managing pediatric head injuries.

## Background
Children are at an increased risk of traumatic brain injury (TBI) due to their proportionally larger head size compared to adults. Computerized tomography (CT) is a standard diagnostic tool used in emergency departments to assess TBI, but it involves radiation exposure, which can elevate the risk of developing leukemia or brain cancer in the future. Despite this, clinically significant intracranial injuries are uncommon in pediatric cases. The decision to perform a CT scan in children with minor head injuries is guided by clinical decision rules such as PECARN, CATCH, and CHALICE, which are designed to optimize the balance between diagnostic accuracy and minimizing unnecessary radiation exposure. These rules are evaluated in comparison to physician judgment to determine their effectiveness in clinical practice.

## Clinical Point
What is the diagnostic accuracy of clinical decision rules and physician judgment in identifying clinically important traumatic brain injuries (TBI) in children with minor head injury?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Clinical decision tools should be used in conjunction with clinical judgment and parents/caregivers preferences to determine the need for CT scans in children with mild TBI.

## Case Resolution
You tell the parents that it is likely the patient sustained a mild traumatic brain injury and discuss the option to undergo head CT versus close observation in the emergency department. The family decides that it would give them more peace of mind if a CT was performed. A head CT is performed and does not demonstrate any evidence of skull fracture or intracranial bleed. You counsel the family on signs and symptoms of concussion prior to discharging them from the emergency department.

## Clinical Application
Joe works in a system that utilizes CHALICE. You may work in a healthcare system that uses a different prediction tool. It is important to realize that none of these tools are perfect and the studies all have their limitations. These tools should inform clinical decision-making, rather than dictate management.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
Your child likely sustained a mild head injury, but I understand why you are concerned. I have not found any concerning findings on my physical exam. I think we can potentially do two things. We can observe her in the emergency department for a few hours. If she remains well, we can let you go home. If she becomes symptomatic, we can decide to obtain a head CT. Alternatively, we could perform a head CT now. Recognize that although a head CT can tell us whether there is a skull fracture, it does expose her to some radiation. We can discuss which option you would prefer.
