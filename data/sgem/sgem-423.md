## Summary
The document discusses a study on patient perceptions of microaggressions and discrimination in emergency department (ED) settings, highlighting the impact of clinician behavior, environmental pressures, and patient demographics on perceived discrimination. The study utilized a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data from the Discrimination in Medical Settings (DMS) scale and qualitative interviews, revealing that nearly half of the participants reported experiencing discrimination during their ED visit. Key themes identified include clinician behavior, emotional responses, perceived reasons for discrimination, environmental pressures, and hesitancy to complain. The findings emphasize the importance of addressing implicit biases and improving communication to enhance patient care and reduce discrimination in the ED. The study underscores the need for healthcare systems to invest in building patient rapport and satisfaction to mitigate negative expectations and improve healthcare outcomes.

## Case
A 57-year-old Chinese woman presents to the emergency department (ED) with chest pain. She speaks some English, but it is her second language. It is a very busy day, and you proceed to ask her questions in rapid succession. You roll your eyes when you must repeat yourself and ask in a louder and louder voice in order to get a response.

## Background
Discrimination and microaggressions in healthcare, particularly in emergency departments (EDs), are linked to negative health outcomes, including delays in seeking treatment, nonadherence to medical advice, and mistrust of healthcare providers. These subtle or unintentional discriminatory behaviors can disempower patients, especially those from marginalized groups, leading to differential care and poorer outcomes. While these issues have been studied in various healthcare settings, there is limited research specifically focusing on EDs, which are characterized by a chaotic environment, time constraints, and lack of prior patient-staff interactions. Understanding and addressing these factors is crucial for improving patient experiences and outcomes in the ED.

## Clinical Point
How can patient perceptions of microaggressions that occur during an ED visit inform potential interventions and prevent future occurrences?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Patients perceive discrimination in the ED for many reasons. We should address our ED culture and individual biases to help reduce patient perceptions of discrimination and improve ED care.

## Case Resolution
Rather than asking the patient questions quickly and in a loud voice, you slow down, speak in a normal tone of voice, and enunciate clearly. This helps collect a clearer history, but you are still having a bit of difficulty, so you find a Chinese speaking translator service to collect the history more accurately and explain things to the patient.

## Clinical Application
Being aware of environmental pressures and your own biases will allow you to provide better care for your patients with fewer microaggressions and discrimination.
