## Summary
A multicenter, open-label, randomized trial investigated the impact of prehospital antibiotic administration on sepsis outcomes, revealing no mortality benefit from administering ceftriaxone in the ambulance compared to usual care, which includes fluid resuscitation and oxygen. The study enrolled 2,698 patients, primarily with non-severe or severe sepsis, and found no significant differences in 28-day mortality, hospital length of stay, ICU admissions, or quality of life between the intervention and control groups. Despite a 96-minute earlier administration of antibiotics in the intervention group, this did not translate into improved survival, highlighting the distinction between statistical and clinical significance. The study underscores the importance of early sepsis recognition and suggests that prehospital antibiotics may not be necessary in well-optimized EMS systems, although further research could explore different settings or EMS configurations.

## Case
EMS is dispatched to a retirement home. They have a 73-year-old man who complains of weakness and a cough for the last 48 hours. You arrive and find the man lying in bed looking ill. He has a history of hypertension, benign prostatic hypertrophy, and osteoarthritis. His medications include ramipril, hydrochlorothiazide, and tamsulosin. On examination, he has a temperature of 38.7C, heart rate of 105 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 26, oxygen saturation of 88%, and a blood pressure of 88/50 mmHg. You load him on the stretcher, start an intravenous of normal saline, and provide some supplemental oxygen via face mask. Clearly, something infectious is going on, and you wonder if starting antibiotics en route to the hospital would help?

## Background
Sepsis is a critical medical condition characterized by a systemic inflammatory response to infection, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates. It is comparable to other urgent medical conditions like myocardial infarction and trauma, which have seen improved outcomes through emergency medical services (EMS). Over half of sepsis patients reach emergency departments via ambulance, yet the impact of administering antibiotics in the pre-hospital setting on survival rates remains uncertain. While some studies suggest that early recognition and prehospital antibiotic administration can enhance survival, with delays in antibiotics linked to a 7.6% decrease in survival per hour, these findings are primarily from retrospective studies and should be interpreted with caution. Prospective, observational studies have not consistently demonstrated a mortality reduction with early antibiotics. However, recent evidence from the first prospective, randomized study offers new insights into the potential benefits of early antibiotic administration for suspected sepsis patients.

## Clinical Point
Can patients with varying degrees of sepsis benefit from early recognition and prehospital administration of IV antibiotics in the ambulance?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Pre-hospital antibiotics in the ambulance do not appear to have a mortality benefit in patients with varying degrees of sepsis in an optimized EMS system.

## Case Resolution
After loading him up on the stretcher, you make your way to the hospital. He receives about 500cc of normal saline en route to the hospital in addition to supplemental oxygen. He arrives no longer hypotensive or hypoxic. You sign over to the triage nurse and express your concerns about this patient having sepsis.

## Clinical Application
This reaffirms that we should focus on things that provide benefit in the pre-hospital setting and not on those that do not. Until there is good evidence demonstrating a patient-oriented outcome, antibiotics will not be part of our EMS protocol. However, as the superintendent of education, I will use this as an opportunity to remind staff about early recognition of sepsis and the use of personal protective equipment.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You are probably weak because of an infection. We suspect a chest infection because you were coughing and your oxygen was low. We are going to start an intravenous line, give you some fluids on the way to the hospital, and provide extra oxygen. They will do some tests in the emergency department and determine your diagnosis and give you the proper treatment.
