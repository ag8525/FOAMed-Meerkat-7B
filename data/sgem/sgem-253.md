## Summary
A study examining the "weekend effect" in trauma centers found no significant difference in 30-day mortality between weekend and weekday admissions, with an odds ratio of 1.01. However, higher mortality rates were observed on Fridays and Saturdays, attributed to more severe head injuries and lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores, rather than staffing differences. The study, conducted at a single UK trauma center, included 1,424 patients with an Injury Severity Score greater than eight. The findings suggest that the perceived "weekend effect" may be influenced by the definition of the weekend and the severity of injuries rather than systemic healthcare factors. The study's applicability to other healthcare systems remains uncertain, and further research is needed to explore this phenomenon in different settings.

## Case
A 52-year-old man presents to the emergency department via EMS after a motor vehicle collision while driving home from the city. It is 2am Saturday morning and the night shift has been busy. You suspect he has been drinking. He has a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13 and an Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 19. There is a small frontal head laceration. He is complaining of some right-sided chest wall pain and shortness of breath. There is an obvious knee injury. While he is waiting to get imaging and laboratory tests done, he asks if he will be more likely to die because it’s a weekend.

## Background
The text addresses several medical myths, including the belief that epinephrine improves neurologic outcomes in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), that all buckle and greenstick fractures require casting, and that a vitamin C cocktail can cure sepsis based on observational studies. It also challenges the notion that higher doses of ketorolac (30mg IV) are superior for pain control compared to lower doses (10mg or 15mg IV), and the necessity of endotracheal airways for OHCA patients. Additionally, it mentions the "weekend effect," a debated concept suggesting increased mortality rates for patients admitted on weekends compared to weekdays. These myths highlight the importance of evidence-based practice in medicine.

## Clinical Point
Does the *“weekend effect”* exist (increased mortality) in a UK trauma centre?

## SGEM Bottom Line
It is still unknown if the *“weekend effect”* exists in trauma centres and it also depends on how you define weekend.

## Case Resolution
The trauma team at LHSC is activated, they assess and identify all his injuries and he is admitted to hospital. He is found to have a tibial plateau fracture, a couple of non-displaced rib fractures, a hemopneumothorax, and a head laceration. After a four-day stay, he is transferred from the trauma centre to his home community hospital outside the city for on-going rehabilitation of his knee.

## Clinical Application
It is unclear how we can apply this information clinically. It would be a great research project to find out if there is a *“weekend effect”* in Ontario’s trauma centers.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We have a great trauma team and we will take great care of you. There is no evidence that more trauma patients are more likely to die in a health care system like ours on the weekend compared to the weekdays.
