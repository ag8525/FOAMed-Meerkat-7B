## Summary
The document discusses the management of pulmonary embolism (PE) in a 48-year-old female patient with a distal sub-segmental PE and normal vital signs, highlighting the potential for outpatient management. Despite guidelines supporting outpatient treatment for low-risk PE patients, the majority in the US are admitted, contrasting with practices in Canada and supported by international guidelines. The study by Westafer et al. found that only 4% of PE patients were discharged from the ED, with a high return visit rate but low subsequent hospitalization. Risk stratification tools like PESI, sPESI, and Hestia Criteria are recommended to identify candidates for outpatient management. The case resolution emphasizes shared decision-making and the use of oral anticoagulants for outpatient treatment, aligning with the SGEM conclusion that US healthcare systems should consider reducing hospital admissions for low-risk PE patients.

## Case
You are evaluating a 48-year-old female for pleuritic chest pain. She is low risk by Wells Criteria but PERC Rule positive because of an appendectomy last month. Her d-dimer comes back elevated, so you order a CT-PA to evaluate for pulmonary embolism (PE). The radiologist notes a distal sub-segmental PE on the right. The patient has normal vital signs and no comorbidities.

## Background
Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a condition where a blood clot blocks a pulmonary artery, posing significant health risks. Historically, most PE patients in the USA have been hospitalized, whereas Canadian studies in the early 2000s demonstrated the safety of outpatient management for low-risk cases. A 2010 study in Ontario showed that half of PE patients were treated as outpatients. A 2015 study by Dr. Jeff Kline reported successful outpatient treatment of 27% of PE patients using the direct oral anticoagulant rivaroxaban. Despite this, 90% of PE patients in the USA are hospitalized, with less than 10% discharged for outpatient therapy. International guidelines, including those from the European Cardiology Society and the British Thoracic Society, support outpatient treatment for low-risk PE patients. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) provides a Level C recommendation for outpatient management of low-risk PE patients, identified using the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI), Simplified PESI (sPESI), or Hestia Criteria. These tools stratify risk based on specific criteria, with online calculators available for ease of use. Patients deemed low-risk by these criteria may be safely discharged with anticoagulation and close follow-up, while those not meeting low-risk criteria should be admitted for inpatient care.

## SGEM Bottom Line
Patients with PE, given the right criteria, can be discharged home from the ED. US healthcare systems should consider decreasing the number of patients who are admitted, with the understanding that the risk of return visits is high.

## Case Resolution
You discuss anticoagulation outpatient management with your patient. She prefers to be discharged and has close follow-up with her primary care clinician in two days. Using shared decision-making, you and the patient agree to discharge her home on oral anticoagulants.

## Clinical Application
Consider using risk score systems and having shared decision-making discussions with your patients to determine who can be safely managed as an outpatient.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have a small blood clot in your right lung. Your vital signs are normal, and all other risk factors are minimal. There is some weak evidence that you do not need to be treated with blood thinners. However, most people still decide to be treated for their blood clot. This treatment can be either in the hospital or as an outpatient. There is a 1 in 4 chance that if you decide to be treated at home you will return to the hospital and 1 in 10 patients need to be admitted when they return. What would you like to do?
