## Summary
Bell's palsy, characterized by idiopathic unilateral facial nerve paralysis, is commonly managed with corticosteroids, which have been shown to significantly improve recovery outcomes. A Cochrane review demonstrated that steroids reduce the risk of incomplete recovery of facial motor function at six months, with a risk ratio of 0.71, indicating a clear benefit. Conversely, antiviral treatments have not shown significant efficacy in improving recovery from Bell's palsy, as evidenced by another Cochrane review. Therefore, the recommended management for Bell's palsy includes corticosteroids, while antivirals are not advised unless the condition is suspected to be Ramsay Hunt Syndrome. In the presented case, the patient was appropriately treated with a 10-day course of oral steroids, with expectations of complete recovery.

## Case Scenario
35yo healthy awakes with unilateral facial droop. Triage nurse brings promptly this case to your attention for query CVA.

## Point
Steroids Y/N, Antivirals Y/N or both?

## Background
Bell's palsy is a form of facial paralysis caused by dysfunction of the cranial nerve VII, leading to an inability to control facial muscles on the affected side. It is the most common acute mononeuropathy and the leading cause of acute facial nerve paralysis. Characterized by a rapid onset of partial or complete paralysis, often occurring overnight, Bell's palsy is typically idiopathic and unilateral, though it can rarely (<1%) affect both sides. The condition is believed to result from an inflammatory process causing swelling of the facial nerve, which travels through a narrow bone canal beneath the ear, leading to nerve compression and potential damage. No specific cause has been identified for Bell's palsy, and it is usually self-limiting.

## BEEM Bottom Line
Steroids for Bell’s Palsy – **YES**.

## BEEM Bottom Line
Antivirals for the treatment of Bell’s Palsy – **NO** – But if the diagnosis is Ramsay Hunt Syndrome the treatment does include antivirals –

## Case Resolution
35yo woman was given a 10day course of oral steroid. She was advised on how to protect her eye from corneal damage. Appropriate follow-up was arranged with her primary care physician or neurologist. Like most individuals, she was expected to have complete recovery.
