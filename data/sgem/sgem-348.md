## Summary
A 46-year-old male with diabetes presents with cellulitis of the right lower leg, confirmed to be free of DVT and without fever, suggesting a stable condition suitable for outpatient management. The document discusses a clinical pathway involving a single-dose intravenous dalbavancin for skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs), which significantly reduced hospitalization rates from 38.5% to 17.6% compared to usual care. This pathway, implemented in emergency departments, showed a decrease in hospitalizations and resource utilization, although it raised concerns about cost-effectiveness and potential antibiotic resistance. The patient was offered this outpatient treatment, avoiding hospital admission, with follow-up care arranged.

## Case
A 46-year-old male with a history of diabetes controlled on metformin presents with erythema and warmth to his right lower leg measuring 27 cm by 10 cm for the past four days. The patient is neurovascularly intact and there is no evidence of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) on ultrasound. He has no fever, and his white blood cell count is 12,500.

## Background
Skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs), including cellulitis and abscesses, are increasingly common reasons for emergency department visits. While most cases are managed on an outpatient basis, inpatient care averages a one-week stay and incurs significant healthcare costs, approximately $5 billion annually in the USA. The mortality rate for hospitalized SSTI patients is less than 0.05%. Notably, 40% of hospital admissions for SSTIs are primarily for the administration of parenteral antibiotics, prompting interest in long-acting parenteral antibiotics as a potential alternative to hospitalization.

## Clinical Point
Does the use of a clinical pathway, including a dose of intravenous dalbavancin, in emergency department patients with skin and soft tissue infections reduce hospitalizations?

## SGEM Bottom Line
In hospital systems with access to IV dalbavancin and the ability to establish expedited telephone and in-person follow-up, this clinical pathway is associated with a decrease in hospitalizations for patients with moderately severe cellulitis.

## Case Resolution
You offer the man the new long-acting single-dose IV antibiotic and outpatient management. He is happy to not need to be admitted to the hospital and is discharged home with follow-up instructions.

## Clinical Application
It all depends. This medication costs ~$5,000 for 1,500mg. It is unclear if this would be a cost-effective strategy. There could also be a concern with indication creep leading to increased antibiotic resistance.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have a skin infection. Traditionally, people are often admitted to the hospital for about one week to get IV antibiotics. We have a new medication that only requires one dose here in the ED. It is a long-acting antibiotic. You can go home today after the treatment. We will give you a call to make sure you are doing ok. You will also get an in-person follow-up in the next couple of days. Would you like to be admitted to the hospital or be treated and sent home today?
