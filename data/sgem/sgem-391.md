## Summary
The document discusses the management of comatose patients after in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), specifically evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic hypothermia. Historically, hypothermia was introduced based on early studies suggesting improved neurological outcomes and reduced mortality, but recent evidence, including the Wolfrum et al. trial, challenges its benefit. This multicenter, randomized controlled trial found no significant difference in all-cause mortality or functional outcomes at 180 days between hypothermia and normothermia groups. The study faced limitations such as lack of blinding, early termination, and potential selection bias, which may affect the generalizability of its findings. Current evidence suggests prioritizing normothermia over hypothermia in post-arrest care, emphasizing the importance of rapid CPR and defibrillation in improving patient outcomes.

## Case
You are working an overnight shift at a small rural hospital. You are tidying your things in anticipation of the arrival of the dayshift when a code blue is called. A 50-year-old man who was admitted to the hospital with a non-ST elevated myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) overnight was found unconscious and without a pulse. The nurses started CPR immediately and place pads before you even arrived. The patient is in ventricular fibrillation, and you achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) on the second shock. The patient is still unconscious. A post-arrest ECG doesn’t show any signs of STEMI. At this point, the dayshift doc walks into the room and asks, *“I can’t keep up with all the evidence. Are we supposed to be starting hypothermia?”*

## Background
Therapeutic hypothermia, introduced into clinical practice following the 2002 publication of two pivotal randomized control trials, is a treatment method used in comatose adult patients who have achieved return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after cardiac arrest. The Hypothermia after Cardiac Arrest (HACA) study and the Bernard study demonstrated that cooling patients to a target temperature of 32-34 degrees Celsius for 24 hours improved neurological outcomes and reduced mortality rates compared to normothermia. The HACA study reported a 55% rate of good neurological outcomes in the hypothermia group versus 39% in the normothermia group, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 6 for neurological outcomes and 7 for mortality. The Bernard study showed a 49% rate of favorable neurological outcomes in the hypothermia group compared to 26% in the normothermia group, with an NNT of 4 for neurological outcomes. However, both studies had limitations, including potential selection bias, lack of blinding, and small sample sizes. Despite these initial findings, subsequent larger trials have questioned the efficacy of therapeutic hypothermia, highlighting the need for further research to validate its clinical benefits.

## Clinical Point
Does hypothermia improve all-cause mortality in adult patients who remain comatose after inpatient cardiac arrest?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There are many priorities when managing a post-arrest patient. For now, the best available evidence seems to suggest that hypothermia is not one of them, and that we should aim to keep our patients in a normothermic range.

## Case Resolution
You do not start a hypothermic protocol on the patient and are thrilled when you return a day later to find the patient sitting up in bed and talking to his family. You make a note of thanking his nurse for rapidly initiating CPR and defibrillation, which probably saved both his life and brain.

## Clinical Application
Although there is remaining uncertainty, this trial adds to the body of research that suggests there is nothing therapeutic about therapeutic hypothermia. We don’t know if fever avoidance is important, but for now the best advice is to target normothermia in post-arrest patients.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Unfortunately, if this Point arises, the patient is critically ill and comatose. When talking to their family, I might say something like, *“You might have heard in the past that cooling patients down, or inducing hypothermia, could save lives. The best available research seems to suggest that it doesn’t work, so we are instead focusing our efforts on the many other aspects of critical care that are essential to your loved one’s survival.”*
