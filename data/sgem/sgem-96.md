## Summary
In this case study, a 55-year-old man with a history of COPD, asthma, and congestive heart failure presents with severe respiratory distress, managed effectively with non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) en route to the hospital. The document discusses the utility of NIPPV in pre-hospital settings for patients with undifferentiated respiratory distress, highlighting its benefits in reducing in-hospital mortality and the need for invasive ventilation, particularly in conditions like acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema and COPD exacerbations. A systematic review and meta-analysis of seven randomized control trials (n=632) demonstrated a significant reduction in in-hospital mortality (RR 0.58) and need for invasive ventilation (RR 0.37) with NIPPV compared to standard care. The study underscores the importance of equipping EMS providers with NIPPV to improve patient outcomes, while noting the need for further research to optimize its use across diverse EMS systems and patient populations.

## Case
55-year-old man with a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and congestive heart failure calls 911 complaining of shortness of breath. Emergency Medical Services arrive to find a patient very short of breath, diaphoretic, and speaking only one-two word sentences. His respiratory rate is 40, heart rate is 110, O2 saturation 88%, and afebrile. He is clearly working hard to breathe. The paramedics decide to use non-invasive positive pressure ventilation on-route to support this man’s respiratory distress.

## Background
Acute dyspnea, a prevalent emergency department complaint, accounted for approximately 3.5% of over 115 million visits in 2003, with some cases presenting in respiratory distress, which is linked to higher morbidity and mortality. Rapid identification of the underlying cause is often challenging, necessitating immediate treatment initiation. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) is a common therapeutic approach for conditions such as acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations, and asthma exacerbations. A 2013 Cochrane systematic review by Vital et al. demonstrated that NIPPV significantly reduced hospital mortality in cardiogenic pulmonary edema compared to standard care (RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.48-0.89). Similarly, a 2004 Cochrane review by Ram et al. found that NIPPV decreased mortality in COPD exacerbations (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.35-0.76). For asthma exacerbations, a Cochrane review by Lim et al. indicated that while NIPPV did not significantly affect intubation rates due to limited data, it reduced hospitalizations and improved discharge rates and respiratory function.

## Clinical Point
Does out-of-hospital non-invasive positive pressure ventilation improve in-hospital mortality compared to standard treatment in patients with grossly undifferentiated respiratory distress?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation is a reasonable out-of-hospital treatment option for adult patients with grossly undifferentiated severe respiratory distress.

## Case Resolution
The 55-year-old man with acute respiratory distress arrives with non-invasive positive pressure ventilation in place and doing better. His respiratory rate is normal, HR<100, and O2 saturation is 97%. You start working him up to differentiate what the cause of his decompensation and are optimistic you will not need to intubate him.

## Clinical Application
As Emergency Medicine doctors, we like to think of ourselves as the front lines of medicine, but the pre-hospital providers are usually the first point of patient contact. They work in more strained and sometimes hostile conditions, they have fewer diagnostic tools at their disposal, and often times are the difference between our patients’ good or bad outcome. We should think of prehospital transport as an extension of our ED and our Emergency Medical Service providers as extensions of our staff. In this way, we should be arming Emergency Medical Service personnel with the best tools in order to deliver the most effective care and to do so safely. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation can and should have a huge role in pre-hospital care. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation would likely lead to fewer suboptimal in-field intubations, in addition to fewer patients arriving to the ED in extremis. We should be working with Emergency Medical Service coordinators to enable Emergency Medical Service providers to use non-invasive ventilation, implement clear protocols for appropriate use, and enhancing physician-Emergency Medical Service communication to help make these decisions and manage potential complications.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
It would be paramedics telling the patients that they appear in acute respiratory distress. We have a device that can help you breathe, been shown to save lives, and may prevent you from having a large tube put down your throat.
