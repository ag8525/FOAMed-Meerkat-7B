## Summary
The document discusses the effectiveness of the Valsalva maneuver (VM) in converting supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) to sinus rhythm. It highlights that while VM is a non-invasive method that increases vagal tone to potentially terminate SVT, evidence supporting its efficacy is insufficient. A Cochrane review of three randomized controlled trials involving 316 patients showed varied success rates, with significant heterogeneity and methodological limitations, particularly as most studies involved induced SVT in controlled settings rather than spontaneous cases in emergency departments. The authors conclude that VM should not be considered essential before pharmacological intervention due to the lack of standardized, evidence-based techniques, although it may be effective in about 20% of cases.

## Case Scenario
Thirty-year-old woman presents for the first time with supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). You call the cardiologist after three unsuccessfully attempts to chemically convert her into sinus. The cardiologist asked you why you did not try the Valsalva maneuver (VM).

## Point
Is the Valsalva maneuver effective in converting supraventricular tachycardia?

## Background
Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is a condition characterized by an abnormally fast heart rhythm originating above the heart's ventricles. Clinically significant due to its frequent presentation in emergency settings, SVT requires prompt intervention to restore normal sinus rhythm. Standard treatment approaches include the Valsalva maneuver (VM), which is a non-invasive technique that increases vagal tone by stimulating baroreceptors, thereby prolonging the myocardial refractory period. Pharmacological interventions involve the administration of adenosine, calcium channel blockers, or beta-blockers. Synchronized cardioversion is an electrical method used when other treatments are ineffective or inappropriate. Additionally, the mammalian dive reflex, which involves immersing the face in ice-cold water, is occasionally employed, particularly in pediatric cases, as a non-pharmacological and non-electrical alternative.

## Bottom Line
There is no standardized methods to perform a VM to terminate uncomplicated SVT that are evidence-based.

## Clinical Application
VM is a viable technique that is poorly researched for the conversion of SVT and should not be considered essential to attempt prior to chemical cardioversion. It may work in up to 20% of presentations. What do I tell my patient: We can try a Valsalva maneuver (pushing air out with your throat, mouth, and nose closed) with reasonable safety while preparing medications for a rapid heartbeat like you have to attempt to correct your palpitations. However, there is no evidence that pushing the air out will be effective and may only work approximately 1 out of every 5 attempts.
