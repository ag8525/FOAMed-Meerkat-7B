## Summary
A 48-year-old woman presented with wrist pain following a FOOSH injury, with clinical suspicion of a scaphoid fracture despite normal initial radiographs. Scaphoid fractures, common after such injuries, often pose diagnostic challenges when X-rays are inconclusive. Traditionally, these cases are managed with casting and follow-up imaging due to risks like non-union and avascular necrosis. However, recent research, including a multicenter RCT, suggests that short-term bandaging with early reassessment may be a viable alternative, showing non-inferior functional outcomes compared to casting. This approach could reduce unnecessary immobilization and associated costs, provided reliable follow-up is ensured. The patient was managed with a removable splint and scheduled for follow-up, aligning with this evolving management strategy.

## Case
A 48-year-old woman presents to the emergency department (ED) with left wrist pain after slipping on a wet pavement while walking into work. It was a FOOSH injury (fall on out-stretched hand). She did not lose consciousness and was able to drive herself to the ED, but she reports increasing wrist pain with movement. She rates the pain as 6/10 and notes it’s worse with lifting or rotating the wrist. She denies numbness, weakness, or swelling of the fingers. No previous wrist fractures or injuries. On inspection, she has no obvious deformity and minimal swelling at the wrist. The examination reveals tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox and over the scaphoid tubercle. Her range of motion is decreased due to pain, especially with radial deviation and wrist extension. Sensation and cap refill intact; radial pulse present. Plain radiographs (PA, lateral, scaphoid view) show no fracture.

## Background
Scaphoid fractures, the most frequently fractured carpal bone, commonly occur after a fall onto an outstretched hand, particularly in young, active individuals. These fractures present a diagnostic challenge in the emergency department due to the potential for normal initial radiographs despite clinical suspicion, often indicated by tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. The standard approach has been to immobilize the wrist and arrange follow-up imaging or orthopedic review within 10 to 14 days to prevent complications such as non-union and avascular necrosis. However, this conservative strategy has resulted in over-treatment, as only 10–20% of suspected cases with normal initial X-rays are confirmed as fractures upon follow-up. Recent research questions the necessity of immediate casting, suggesting that selective immobilization and early reassessment might be equally effective, prompting a reevaluation of treatment protocols to balance the risks of under-treatment against the drawbacks of unnecessary immobilization and associated costs.

## Clinical Point
Can patients with suspected scaphoid fractures and normal initial radiographs be managed without casting, using a brief period of bandaging and reassessment?

## Case Resolution
The 48-year-old patient is placed in a removable wrist splint rather than a rigid cast. She is provided with detailed discharge instructions and scheduled for follow-up reassessment in 10–14 days. At follow-up, she had no signs of fracture progression or complications and returned to work without difficulty.

## Clinical Application
This study supports a more selective, less immobilization-heavy approach for managing patients with suspected scaphoid fractures and negative X-rays, especially in settings with reliable follow-up. It reinforces the shift toward minimizing overtreatment while still prioritizing safety and patient-centred care. EDs may consider updating protocols to offer wrist splinting and early re-evaluation rather than default casting. However, you also must consider the ability or willingness to follow up. Although we may overtreat occult scaphoid fractures, scaphoid non-union poses a real problem. Suppose you place someone in a bandage and they don’t come back in 2 weeks (as per the authors is the standard of care in the Netherlands). Do the 10% with a scaphoid fracture not come back until it’s an established non-union at 6 months, and thus have a much more invasive treatment rather than the potential for casting? In a universal healthcare system where you can get regular follow-up, and I don’t know the landscape in the Netherlands, but perhaps it is less litigious? The results need to be interpreted through the lens of external validity bias.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have signs of a possible wrist fracture, but your X-rays today don’t show one. In the past, we would put almost everyone in a cast for a couple of weeks just in case. But newer evidence suggests that for many people, we can try a supportive bandage or splint instead, check again in 10–14 days, and only treat more aggressively if needed. This approach can be more comfortable, avoids unnecessary casting, and doesn’t increase the risk of long-term problems if you can come back for follow-up. But it is also critical that if you aren’t improving, you should return for a recheck.
