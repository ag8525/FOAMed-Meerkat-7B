## Summary
A 25-year-old female presented with a stab wound resulting in decreased air entry on the right side, necessitating the insertion of a chest tube, which initially returned 100 ml of blood. Despite the tube's suboptimal position on X-ray, it was functioning adequately, prompting further investigation for persistent white-out, leading to the discovery of a bronchial injury confirmed by bronchoscopy and subsequently repaired surgically. The historical evolution of chest tube use highlights its critical role in managing pneumothorax and hemothorax, with current practice emphasizing placement within the triangle of safety. A study by Kong et al. suggests that routine post-insertion chest X-rays have a low yield in altering management unless clinical concerns persist, while Benns et al. indicate that the precise location of the chest tube within the pleural space is less critical than ensuring its safe insertion. The case underscores the importance of clinical judgment in chest tube management and the potential need for surgical intervention in complex trauma cases.

## Case
A 25-year-old female was allegedly stabbed while standing on the corner minding her own business. She was found unconscious (Glasgow Coma Scale 7) and was intubated by the EMS. On arrival in your emergency department, the vitals are stable, but there is decreased air entry on the right side. You skillfully insert a right chest tube and get back 100 ml of blood. You review the post insertion chest tube x-ray and are somewhat disappointed by the position of your tube. It is hitting the mediastinum and curling back on itself and there is persistent white-out on the right.

## Background
Chest tubes, a critical component in the management of pneumothorax and hemothorax, became standard care in the 1950s, though the concept of chest drainage dates back to Hippocrates. The first recorded use of a chest tube for trauma was in the 1200s, with a linden tree bark tube used to treat a tension hemopneumothorax. Over centuries, the approach to chest wounds evolved, with significant debate over open versus closed treatment. By the 17th and 18th centuries, wound sucking became a practice to remove retained blood. During World War I, chest wound mortality was 56%, with repeated thoracenteses as the preferred treatment for hemothorax. The introduction of plastic chest tubes in 1961 and the adoption of closed thoracostomy with underwater seal drainage during the Vietnam War reduced mortality to 2.9%. Modern chest tubes, made from clear vinyl or silastic, feature multiple side holes, centimeter markings, and a radiopaque strip for x-ray visualization, continuing the ancient principle of evacuating pus, air, blood, and fluid from the pleural cavity.

**Point #1: In a trauma patient, how clinically useful is a chest X-ray (CXR) after putting in the chest tube?**


## SGEM Bottom Line
Put the tube on the correct side, within the triangle of safety, and within the pleural space. Continue to obtain a CXR post chest tube knowing it will probably not change management. Be more concerned if the patient is doing poorly or the tube is not draining.

**Point #2: Does chest tube location matter?**


## SGEM Bottom Line
Safely put the chest tube in the pleural space.

## Case Resolution
The tube you placed appears to be fluctuating with breathing, has a small air leak, and continues to drain a small amount of blood. It appears to be working, so you leave it alone. Because of the persistent “white-out” you wonder if there may be something else going on such as a bronchial injury. You consult your friendly thoracic surgeon who confirms your suspicions with a bronchoscopy. The patient is taken to the operating room for repair and the patient recovers well. He informs you that your tube was in good position.

## Clinical Application
Focus on getting the chest tube placed in the triangle of safety and try to place it superiorly and posteriorly, but don’t worry too much if it is not “perfect” on the post insert CXR. What matters most is if the patient is doing well post procedure.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
In this case the patient was intubated so I would be speaking to her family. I would let them know she was stabbed in the chest that resulted in some internal bleeding and possibly a collapsed lung. The emergency department physician expertly put in a chest tube that drained out the blood. However, the CXR showed something else was going on. We therefore needed to take her to the operating room to repair an injury to the lung. This was done successfully and she is doing well.
