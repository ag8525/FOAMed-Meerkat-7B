## Summary
In a randomized clinical trial examining the efficacy of antibiotics in children with acute sinusitis, it was found that antibiotics may offer minimal clinical benefit, particularly in those without nasopharyngeal bacterial pathogens, and the color of nasal discharge was not indicative of bacterial infection. The study involved children aged 2 to 11 years with persistent or worsening sinusitis symptoms, treated with Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid or placebo. Results showed a modest improvement in symptom scores with antibiotics, but at the cost of increased diarrhea. The study emphasized the importance of antibiotic stewardship, suggesting that testing for specific bacterial pathogens could help reduce unnecessary antibiotic use. Despite statistical significance in symptom improvement, the clinical significance remains debatable, and shared decision-making with parents is recommended, weighing the modest benefits against potential side effects.

## Case
A 4-year-old girl presents to your emergency department (ED) with fever and nasal drainage. Her vaccinations are all up to date. Symptoms have been present for the past 12 days. She initially had some cough and congestion which was diagnosed as a viral upper respiratory infection by her primary care doctor. Her symptoms have persisted and yesterday she developed fever (temperature of 38.3°C) and nasal drainage. On physical examination, she has nasal congestion with yellow-colored nasal discharge. The family says to you, "She’s been sick for almost 2 weeks and the color of her nasal drainage changed to yellow. Does this mean she has a bacterial infection that needs antibiotics?"

## Background
Acute bacterial sinusitis in children is a condition characterized by persistent or severe symptoms following a viral upper respiratory infection (URI), with clinical guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics suggesting diagnosis when symptoms such as nasal discharge and daytime cough persist beyond 10 days, worsen after initial improvement, or present with severe onset including high fever and purulent nasal discharge. The evidence supporting these guidelines is rated as quality B. Common bacterial pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Hemophilus influenzae, and Moraxella catarrhalis. While sinus aspiration is not routinely performed in children, untreated sinusitis can lead to serious complications such as preseptal and orbital cellulitis, and in severe cases, intracranial issues like cavernous venous thrombosis, osteomyelitis, meningitis, or abscesses. The challenge remains in distinguishing bacterial sinusitis from viral infections to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.

## Clinical Point
What are the potential benefits and harms of antibiotic treatment for children diagnosed with acute sinusitis and does it depend on bacterial pathogen colonization or color of nasal discharge?

- **Population:** Children aged 2 to 11 years with persistent or worsening acute sinusitis (as per AAP practice guideline) and symptom score of => 9 on Pediatric Rhinosinusitis Scale (PRSS). This scale ranges from 0-40 with higher scores representing more severe symptoms. Persistent = nasal symptoms, cough or both for 11 – 30 days without improvement. Worsening = period of improvement followed by worsening nasal symptoms or daytime cough, or new onset fever on days 6 – 10.
  - **Exclusion:** Severe presentation (presence of both colored nasal discharge and fever ≥39°C for 3 or more consecutive days, history of asthma, active wheezing, solely cough, history of allergic rhinitis, immotile cilia syndrome, cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, allergy to study medications, concurrent infections, systemic antibiotic use within the previous 15 days, prior sinus surgery, families did not have access to phone or were not English/Spanish speaking
  - **Intervention:** 10 days of Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid
- **Comparison:** Matching placebo
- **Outcome:** 
  - **Primary Outcome:** Symptom burden (PRSS) during first 10 days after diagnosis
  - **Secondary Outcome:** Treatment failure defined as (1) an increase in PRSS score of greater than 20% from enrollment at any time, (2) a decrease of less than 2 points in the PRSS score from enrollment to day 3, (3) a decrease in PRSS score of less than 20% from enrollment to day 4, (4) a decrease in PRSS score of less than 20% from enrollment on 2 consecutive occasions on days 5 to 11, or (5) a decrease in PRSS score by less than 50% from enrollment to the end-of-study follow-up visit. Acute otitis media (AOM). Receipt of another systemic antibiotic
  - **Safety:** Non-susceptible pathogen at follow up (S pneumonia or H influenzae). Clinically significant diarrhea (3 or more watery stools for 1 day or 2 watery stools on each of 2 consecutive days). Rash. Other resource utilization. Missed work days.
- **Type of Study:** A multi-center, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial.

## SGEM Bottom Line
Antibiotics *may* offer some benefit for mild to moderate sinusitis in children but at the cost of a potential increase in diarrhea.

## Case Resolution
You tell the family that their child likely has a viral infection or sinusitis and that the color of her nasal discharge does not necessarily tell you whether this is a bacterial infection. You engage in shared decision making and talk with the families about the potential harms and benefits of initiating antibiotic therapy. The family expresses that they would like a prescription for antibiotics, and you discharge them home.

## Clinical Application
This is reassuring that most children with sinusitis will recover with or without antibiotics, even when the history has been prolonged and moderately severe. At present we generally avoid prescribing antibiotics for those with uncomplicated sinusitis in the first instance, and will continue to recommend this. For those with moderate to severe sinusitis, or with a more prolonged history, use this evidence to support shared decision making with parents and children and young people; in particular considering the modest benefit against the relatively common side effects.

**What Do I Tell the Parent?**
I’m sorry your child is still sick. It’s hard to know whether her symptoms are due to a virus or bacteria. Giving her fever medications and fluids can help. Another option is to give her some antibiotics. Antibiotics don’t work on viruses. Antibiotics may decrease how sick she is and how long she will be sick. However, it can cause a runny bum (diarrhea) and there is a risk of allergic reaction. Which would you prefer, a running nose or a runny bum?
