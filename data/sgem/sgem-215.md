## Summary
Aortic dissection is a rare but potentially fatal condition that presents a diagnostic challenge in the emergency department, with up to 38% of cases initially missed. The primary symptom is severe, sudden-onset pain, often in the chest or back, but this is not specific enough to confirm the diagnosis. Clinical decision tools like the AHA ADD risk score and the Von Kodolitsch score can aid in assessment, though they are not definitive; the Von Kodolitsch score, for instance, has a low negative likelihood ratio that may help rule out the condition. Despite the availability of these tools, the American College of Emergency Physicians advises against relying solely on them, recommending that clinical judgment and a combination of history, physical examination, and imaging guide the decision to pursue further testing. In the case presented, a CT angiogram was ultimately necessary to confirm the diagnosis of descending aortic dissection, underscoring the importance of a comprehensive approach in managing suspected cases.

## Case
You are in the emergency department caring for a 65-year-old man with sharp chest pain radiating to the back. Blood pressure is elevated, and his pain was sudden in onset. His chest x-ray is normal, and there is no sign of asymmetric pulses. The EKG and laboratory tests are normal. You are wondering if you need to order a CT to rule out an aortic dissection.

## Background
Aortic dissection is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by a tear in the aorta's inner layer, leading to high mortality rates of up to 40% in the acute phase and 70% within two weeks. The primary symptom is pain in the chest, back, or abdomen, which complicates diagnosis due to its commonality in other conditions. Diagnostic challenges are exacerbated by the lack of data on the predictive value of clinical features, as most studies focus on already diagnosed cases. Two clinical prediction tools exist: the American Heart Association's aortic dissection detection (ADD) risk score and the Von Kodolitsch score, which considers immediate-onset tearing pain, mediastinal/aortic widening on chest x-ray, and pulse/BP differential. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) advises against relying solely on these tools, recommending that the decision to pursue further diagnostic workup should be at the treating physician's discretion.

## SGEM Bottom Line
Diagnosing aortic dissection in an undifferentiated population presenting to the emergency department continues to be a diagnostic challenge.

## Case Resolution
The patient’s pain persists, and in fact worsens despite medical treatment in the emergency department. You elect to perform a CT angiogram, and diagnose him with a descending aortic dissection.

## Clinical Application
We do not have strong evidence to guide our care in these patients. Various clinical data can increase and decrease the likelihood of an aortic dissection but not rule it in or out. It will take a combination of a good history, followed by a directed physical exam, basic investigations, clinical gestalt and shared decision making on how best to proceed. These decisions will all take place in a context that will depend on many factors (patient, physician and resources, medical-legal environment, etc).

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Well, prior to the CT, I tell my patient that his studies looking at acute coronary abnormalities are normal, but his pain is concerning for aortic dissection, or a tear in the big blood vessel coming from his heart. I would like to perform a CT scan to rule out this life-threatening condition.
