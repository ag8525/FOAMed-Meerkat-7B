## Summary
The document discusses the use of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) for acute ischemic stroke, focusing on the NINDS study, which demonstrated that administering tPA within three hours of stroke onset improved clinical outcomes at three months despite an increased risk of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. The study's findings have significantly influenced stroke management protocols, leading to the widespread adoption of tPA, although the decision remains controversial due to baseline differences in patient groups and the potential influence of financial interests. The case scenario highlights the importance of considering individual patient factors, such as blood pressure, age, and symptom improvement, when deciding on tPA administration. The document underscores the need for careful patient selection and informed discussions about the risks and benefits of tPA treatment in acute ischemic stroke.

## Case Scenario
An 85-year-old woman presents to your emergency department with left leg and arm weakness and slurred speech for the last two hours. She has a history of hypertension and diabetes. Her vitals are unremarkable except for a blood pressure of 202/115. A non-contrast CT head is performed that shows no acute intracranial pathology. Upon return from CT scan, the patient is speaking clearly but still has weakness (4/5) in the left arm and leg. Your stroke team asks you to administer alteplase and admit.

## Point
Is alteplase an effective treatment in patients who present with signs of an ischemic stroke of less than three hours duration?

## Background
Ischemic stroke, accounting for 85% of the 22 million strokes occurring annually worldwide, is a leading cause of disability and the third most common cause of death. Historically, there were no effective acute treatments, resulting in significant morbidity. The introduction of thrombolytic agents, initially used for myocardial infarction (MI), offered a potential treatment for ischemic stroke by dissolving clots to improve outcomes. In MI, lytic agents demonstrated a 1-2% mortality improvement, particularly when administered early, leading to the concept of "time is heart." This principle was adapted to stroke treatment as "time is brain," despite initial trials like MAST-Italy and ECASS-I showing no statistical benefit within a 6-hour window and increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage. These trials were based on the hypothesis that thrombolytics would be effective in the brain as they were in the heart, but the results highlighted the need for further research to establish efficacy and safety in stroke management.

## SGEM Bottom Line
I’m skeptical that thrombolysis has benefit for acute stroke

## Case Resolution
Based on your in depth knowledge of the NINDS study, you point out to the neurologist that the patient may be a poor candidate for tPA based on her presenting blood pressure, her age and the fact that her symptoms have been improving while in the ED. The neurologist and you have a discussion with the patient and her family about the risks and benefits. Given her improving symptoms, they elect to decline tPA treatment. The patient is admitted to the stroke unit. A week later, she has 4+/5 strength in her upper and lower extremities and she is speaking clearly. She is discharged to the rehabilitation center for further care.
