## Summary
In the context of managing acute pain in the emergency department, the document discusses the potential for continued opioid use following an initial prescription, particularly in patients with conditions like musculoskeletal pain and fractures. A study by Daoust et al. found that 9% of patients continued opioid use three months after an ED visit, primarily for the initial painful complaint, with a small percentage using it for new pain or misuse. The study highlights that patients who consumed opioids within the first two weeks were significantly more likely to continue use at three months. The clinical guidance suggests that while the risk of long-term use and misuse is low, it is not negligible, and opioids should be prescribed judiciously, considering alternatives and individualizing treatment to minimize potential dependency.

## Case
You are taking care of a 56-year-old woman who presented to the emergency department with a Jones fracture. During your discharge discussion, you offer her a prescription for oxycodone/acetaminophen and she gets a worried look on her face and says: *“I try to stay away from those medications…what if I get hooked?”* You realize you are unsure what to tell her about the chances of continued opiate use after an initial prescription.

## Background
Opioid use has significantly increased over the past 15 years, yet opioids remain essential for managing acute pain. The 2001 Joint Commission's designation of pain as the fifth vital sign is partly blamed for the rise in opioid misuse. Emergency Department (ED) physicians are among the top prescribers of opioids, raising concerns about the potential harm of aiming to completely eliminate pain, which may compromise patient awareness and understanding of their condition. Studies indicate that more educated patients prefer to endure some pain rather than risk opioid dependency, unlike less educated patients. Research on opioid use post-prescription often includes individuals with prior substance abuse, complicating the assessment of misuse. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) issued a 2012 clinical policy recommending that opioid prescriptions for adult ED patients be individualized, time-limited, reserved for severe or refractory acute pain, and not used for chronic pain exacerbations.

## SGEM Bottom Line
Only a small percent of patients who received an opioid prescription in the ED will still be using opioids months later and even less will be misusing opioids.

## Case Resolution
Your patient agrees to have a prescription written and tells you that she may only fill it if her pain is severe.

## Clinical Application
When discharging patients with painful complaints, be aware that the risks of future use and misuse is small but not zero and consider whether opioids are the most appropriate treatment for their pain.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
There does seem to be a correlation with opioid use for a painful complaint and opioid use/misuse three months later. The majority is for the same painful complaint. It seems more likely if you use the opioids in the first two weeks of getting the prescription. If this concerns you, you can try to avoid use in the short term and use alternatives to opioids.
