## Summary
In the context of a rural emergency department, the case of a 63-year-old woman with chest pain highlights the diagnostic challenge of acute aortic syndromes (AAS), including aortic dissection. Despite the absence of specific risk factors or alarming symptoms, the possibility of AAS was considered. The use of D-dimer testing, known for its high sensitivity (96.5%) but moderate specificity (56.2%), was debated as a potential tool to rule out AAS and avoid unnecessary imaging, given the logistical constraints of accessing CTA. However, the American College of Emergency Physicians advises against relying solely on D-dimer to exclude aortic dissection due to its variable performance and potential for false positives. The case resolution involved shared decision-making, emphasizing clinical gestalt and patient reassurance, with a decision against further testing based on the low pre-test probability of dissection. This underscores the need for further research on the real-world application of D-dimer in AAS risk stratification.

## Case
You are working a busy shift in a rural emergency department (ED) and your excellent Family Medicine trainee presents a case of a 63-year-old woman with chest pain and some intermittent radiation into the inter-scapular region. The patient has no specific risk factors for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or dissection. The history is not overly concerning for dissection – the adjectives “severe”, and “ripping” or “tearing” are not used.
Your trainee has done a great work-up and the possibility of an acute aortic dissection (AAD) is included in her differential diagnosis. Because this is a rural ED and the closest Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is a three-hour drive away, we must decide if this will benefit this patient.
You accompany your trainee back to the bedside and clarify the nature of the chest pain, re-examine the patient and review all the available clinical data. There are no abnormalities in the blood work, ECG, or chest x-ray. Her pain seems to have settled. In this woman with no specific features of an aortic dissection, can we use a D-dimer assay to rule out this serious diagnosis and avoid a long and costly transfer?

## Background
Acute aortic syndromes (AAS), including aortic dissection, intramural hematoma, and penetrating ulcers, are critical conditions characterized by nonspecific symptoms such as chest, back, or abdominal pain, and are associated with high morbidity and mortality if not promptly identified. Aortic dissections, in particular, require rapid diagnosis and intervention due to their swift progression. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is the standard imaging modality for detecting AAS due to its high sensitivity and specificity, though its use involves risks like ionizing radiation exposure and increased costs, especially in low-prevalence populations. The D-dimer test is used to potentially rule out AAS and reduce unnecessary imaging, but its diagnostic effectiveness is debated due to inconsistent performance across different patient groups. The American College of Emergency Physicians advises against relying solely on a negative D-dimer test to exclude thoracic aortic dissection in suspected cases.

## Clinical Point
What is the diagnostic accuracy of D-dimer for diagnosing acute aortic syndrome (AAS)?

## SGEM Bottom Line
It remains unclear as to the role of D-Dimer testing in patients with suspected acute aortic syndrome. Whilst this paper provides good data on the test characteristics of D-dimer for AAS – it cannot inform our application in the clinical setting.

## Case Resolution
Clinically your gestalt says that this is not a dissection. You enter a shared-decision-making discussion with the woman and her husband. You let her know that her acute coronary syndrome and pulmonary embolism risks are low and that she has a small but NON-ZERO risk of aortic disease. A blood test called a D-dimer may further lower this risk, however there is a good chance it will return a false positive and muddy the waters. She feels reassured after the counseling and decides not to pursue further investigation and is happy to go home with appropriate instructions about returning if her symptoms return or change.

## Clinical Application
D-dimer testing is a sensitive but non-specific marker of acute aortic syndromes. It may have a role in the risk stratification of patients with intermediate risk of aortic dissection on clinical assessment. Further study of the real-world outcomes of algorithms using D-dimer is required before we use it as a one-way tool in ED practice.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
We are happy to hear that your pain has resolved and at this time we feel that further investigation will not benefit you. Please do not hesitate to return if your symptoms return or if you have other concerns.
