## Summary
The document discusses the management of bronchiolitis in community hospitals, highlighting that despite the high incidence of bronchiolitis leading to hospitalizations, there is a lack of evidence-based treatments. The study by Plint et al. reveals that many infants receive medications and investigations with little proven benefit, such as bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and antibiotics, both in emergency and inpatient settings. The study found that 30% of patients were admitted, with a significant number receiving unnecessary interventions. The authors emphasize the need for knowledge translation strategies to improve management practices in community hospitals, advocating for supportive care as the primary treatment approach. The clinical application suggests avoiding unnecessary tests and treatments, focusing instead on supportive care, such as nasal toileting, and providing clear discharge instructions to parents.

## Case
Parents present to your community emergency department with their 6-month-old daughter. She has had a cough, fever, and *“noisy breathing”* for the past 24 hours. She is otherwise healthy, having had a previously uncomplicated prenatal, delivery, and post-natal course. Her immunizations are up to date. There is no family history of atopy or asthma.
On exam, she is febrile at 38.4 Celsius, pulse 150bpm, respiratory rate 50bpm, and an oxygen saturation of 93% on room air. You even manage to get a blood pressure, which is 78/48. She has a lot of clear nasal discharge and mildly increased work of breathing, with subcostal indrawing. Her cardiac exam is unremarkable, but there is diffuse wheezing throughout the lungs bilaterally, which the parents say has never happened before. The remainder of her exam is reassuring.

## Background
Bronchiolitis is a common respiratory condition in infants, characterized by inflammation of the small airways, and is the leading cause of hospitalization in this age group due to its high incidence. It is diagnosed clinically, as no definitive test exists to confirm or exclude it. Despite various guidelines from organizations like NICE, AAP, and CPS, no universally effective treatment has been identified. Supportive care remains the primary management approach, although inhaled hypertonic saline has shown some benefit, and research into combining nebulized epinephrine with systemic steroids is ongoing. Concerns persist regarding the use of unproven therapies such as beta-agonists, steroids alone, and antibiotics. Studies have documented the continued use of bronchodilators and steroids in Canadian pediatric emergency departments, highlighting the need for further research into practice patterns, especially in community hospital settings.

## Case Resolution
After a thorough history and careful physical exam, you reassure the parents that their child has bronchiolitis without any ancillary tests or treatments. The child is feeding well in the emergency department and is discharged home with advice around supportive care, including nasal toileting, and appropriate return to emergency department instructions.

## Clinical Application
In children with bronchiolitis, clinicians should not be tempted to perform investigations and prescribe treatments that have little benefit or are unproven. Bronchiolitis is a clinical diagnosis and does not require any specific investigations or treatment.
Although it was not discussed in this article, there has been some benefit shown with the use of hypertonic saline. However, conservative treatment with supportive therapy alone is the mainstay.

## What Do I Tell the Patient (in this case the parents)
Your child has a very common virus infection in their chest that is making them congested and wheezy. I wish there was a medicine that I would know could make this better but there doesn’t seem to be much that works for children like yours. The good news is that almost all children with this infection get fine at home. We are going to teach you how to keep your child’s nose clear. If your child gets worse, starts to have more trouble breathing, isn’t drinking, is becoming tired or unresponsive, or if you’re just worried they look sicker we want you to return to the emergency department.
