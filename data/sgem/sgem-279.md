## Summary
The document discusses the management of migraine headaches, focusing on a new treatment option, ubrogepant, a calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) antagonist. The study referenced evaluates ubrogepant's efficacy compared to placebo, showing it provides superior pain relief and symptom resolution at two hours post-dose, with a Number Needed to Treat for Benefit (NNTB) of 13 for pain freedom and 10 for symptom absence. However, the study's design, using a placebo control rather than comparing to standard treatments, raises ethical concerns and questions about its applicability to emergency department settings. The study's limitations include potential selection bias, industry funding, and the use of a modified intention-to-treat analysis, which may overestimate efficacy. The document concludes that while ubrogepant shows promise, it is not yet ready for widespread use in emergency settings without further comparative studies against established migraine therapies.

## Case
A 23-year-old man with a history of migraines presents with two days of headache, nausea, and photophobia typical of his prior migraines. He’s tried a number of medications at home including ibuprofen, acetaminophen, aspirin, and sumatriptan without any considerable improvement in symptoms. You start to offer him your standard medications like metoclopramide and haloperidol when he asks about a new drug he heard about called ubrogepant.

## Background
Migraine headaches are a chronic neurologic condition characterized by unilateral, throbbing pain often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia, significantly impacting patients' quality of life. While headaches are a frequent emergency department presentation, they can range from benign to life-threatening conditions such as subarachnoid hemorrhage and meningitis. The International Headache Society classifies headaches into primary and secondary categories, aiding in the differentiation of serious from benign cases. Although emergency departments seldom diagnose migraines initially, they frequently manage symptoms using treatments like NSAIDs, acetaminophen, aspirin, neuroleptics, triptans, and propofol. Recent advancements include calcitonin gene-related peptide antagonists (CGRPs), with studies on drugs like rimegepant and ubrogepant published in the New England Journal of Medicine, highlighting their potential in migraine management.

## Clinical Point
Does ubrogepant increase the percentage of patients who were free from pain and absent of the most bothersome migraine-associated symptom at two hours from initial dose in comparison to placebo?

## Case Resolution
You discuss with the patient that the novel CGRP receptor antagonists have potential to benefit patients with moderate to severe migraines but, that there’s limited evidence for their use in the emergency department and that we have no idea if these drugs are more effective or safer than our standard medications.
You decide instead to treat the patient with 10 mg of metoclopramide which has a modest reduction in pain. You follow this with 2.5 mg of haloperidol which results in the patient’s pain reaching a level of 2 out of 10 and resolution of his nausea and photophobia. He is improved enough to go home and states he will follow up with his neurologist in the next week or so.

**Clinical Application**: We do not know if ubrogepant is better, worse, or the same efficacy as existing treatments for migraine headaches. Until data on patients presenting to the ED with migraine is reported that includes a comparison to standard active treatment and safety data, this drug doesn’t belong in our armamentarium.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
There is a new class of drug that has been approved to treat migraine headaches. The research was not done on patients like you in the emergency department. The studies also did not compare the new drugs to our existing treatments. We do not know if it is more or less effective than what we already use. The good news is the treatments we do have are very effective. We can also offer you a dose of steroids to prevent you from suffering a rebound headache (NNT 9).
