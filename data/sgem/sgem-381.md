## Summary
A systematic review and network meta-analysis by Hayashi et al. evaluated the efficacy and safety of intravenous sedation (IVS), intra-articular anesthesia (IAA), and peripheral nerve blocks (PNB) for reducing anterior shoulder dislocations. The study included 16 RCTs with 957 patients and found no significant differences in immediate success rates or patient satisfaction among the three methods. However, IAA was associated with a shorter emergency department length of stay compared to IVS, and both IAA and PNB had fewer adverse respiratory events than IVS. The study highlighted the uncertainty in the evidence, emphasizing the need for high-quality trials and the importance of clinical judgment and patient preferences in choosing the appropriate analgesia method.

## Case
A 19-year-old man presents to the emergency department (ED) with his first time anterior shoulder dislocation after trying to recreate one of his favourite scenes in the movie Lethal Weapon. He is in significant pain, but your charge nurse informs us that, like most days since the pandemic started, the department is completely full of admitted patients, and there is nowhere safe to perform a procedural sedation, let alone have the staff to do it. The patient asks, through clenched teeth, whether there are any other options to get his shoulder back in.

## Background
The shoulder joint, with its extensive range of motion, is highly susceptible to dislocation, predominantly anterior dislocations, particularly affecting young active men. Diagnosis can be achieved through clinical evaluation, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), or x-ray, and numerous reduction techniques exist. Analgesia and sedation options vary, including peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs), intra-articular anesthesia (IAA), and procedural sedation (PS). PS is a significant advancement in emergency medicine, enabling pain-free procedures despite minor adverse events, though it poses logistical challenges. PNBs, increasingly popular due to ultrasound guidance, require practitioner skill, with limited definitive evidence from randomized control trials. IAA injections, theoretically straightforward due to joint accessibility during dislocation, have shown mixed results, with studies indicating frequent inaccuracies in joint space targeting. Consequently, the optimal analgesia or sedation technique for anterior shoulder dislocation reduction remains uncertain.

## Clinical Point
What is the safety and efficacy of intravenous sedation, intra-articular injection, and peripheral nerve block for the reduction of anterior shoulder dislocations.

## SGEM Bottom Line
It is important to embrace the full definition of EBM. Although there isn’t a clear winner based on the literature, clinicians will have to use their clinical judgement to determine which approach might be best in their healthcare setting, and for their specific patients. Furthermore, it will be important to determine patient values, and involve them in shared decision making.

## Case Resolution
Although the patient is afraid of needles, he decides that an intra-articular injection is preferable to waiting for a sedation bed. The procedure goes smoothly, and the shoulder slips back in before you even start your reduction technique.

## Clinical Application
There is significant uncertainty with regards to the best approach for sedation and analgesia for the reduction of anterior shoulder dislocations. Intra-articular anesthesia, peripheral nerve blocks, and intravenous sedation are all reasonable approaches.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
You have a shoulder dislocation, which means you need my help to get your arm back into your shoulder joint. We have many options. For some people, there are techniques that cause almost no pain, and therefore we can try to reduce your shoulder without any pain medication at all. However, there are also many options to control your pain, including an injection into the joint itself, an injection that will numb the nerves to the shoulder, and intravenous medication that will put you to sleep so you don’t feel or remember anything.
