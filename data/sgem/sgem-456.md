## Summary
A national survey conducted by Ma K et al. explored children's experiences and needs during visits to Canadian pediatric emergency departments (EDs), focusing on emotional, practical, and communication aspects. The study included children aged 7-17 and their caregivers, revealing that while nearly all children felt well cared for, about one-third did not fully understand their diagnosis or treatment. The survey highlighted the importance of direct communication with children, as it was associated with better understanding and reduced fear. Despite high satisfaction rates, the study emphasized that satisfaction does not equate to comprehension, suggesting a need for improved communication strategies tailored to different age groups. The findings underscore the necessity of involving children in discussions about their health and ensuring their understanding, rather than relying solely on caregiver satisfaction as a proxy for the child's experience.

## Case
You are working with a medical trainee on her first clinical rotation through the emergency department (ED). One of the first patients she sees is a 12-year-old boy presenting with lower abdominal pain. He is accompanied by his worried parents. Before entering the room, she says to you, *“This is the first time I’ve seen a pediatric patient. Do you have any tips for getting the history or performing the physical exam?”*

## Background
In the emergency department (ED), interactions with pediatric patients involve comprehensive processes including history taking, examination, assessment, planning, and communication of results, which are crucial for determining final disposition. Pediatric care in the ED is complex due to the need to effectively communicate with both children and their caregivers, as these experiences can significantly influence children's future healthcare perceptions and behaviors. Effective pain management is particularly important, as inadequate pain control can lead to future avoidance of medical care and vaccine hesitancy. Engaging directly with children to understand their needs and perspectives is essential for optimizing their care and ensuring positive healthcare experiences.

## Clinical Point
What are children’s perspectives on their needs and experiences during visits to Canadian pediatric EDs?

## SGEM Bottom Line
There are many opportunities to improve the ED experience for children and help them understand their diagnosis and treatment.

## Case Resolution
You applaud the medical trainee for her awareness of the differing needs of children. You encourage her to introduce herself to the boy and family and sit during the examination. Because the boy is twelve years old, you encourage her to start by first engaging the boy and then asking the parents for any additional information. If there are any sensitive questions (sexual health), you encourage her to ask the parents to leave the room briefly to ask those questions directly to the patient.

## Clinical Application
There are simple actions we can take to improve our clinical interactions. One basic thing we try to do is to sit when talking with our patients. When seeing children in the emergency department, we encourage everyone to speak directly with the child if they are age-appropriate and respect their privacy when asking sensitive questions. Additional work needs to be done to determine best strategies for explaining diagnoses, testing, and treatment to children of various ages.

**What Do I Tell My Student?**
Thank you for asking that Point. When seeing pediatric patients, we often can get history from the patient and the family. I try to start the conversation with the patient first and ask the family to fill in the parts that they might not know. This patient is presenting with lower abdominal pain. You may want to consider asking him some questions related to his sexual health. These questions are better asked in private, so it is appropriate to request the parents step out briefly. We also need to try and explain what we think is going on and what we’re going to do in language that the patient can understand.
