## Summary
The document discusses the use of intravenous lidocaine for pain management in the emergency department, particularly for conditions like renal colic and critical limb ischemia. It highlights that while lidocaine is a local anesthetic with potential analgesic benefits, current evidence from a systematic review is limited and heterogeneous, showing mixed efficacy compared to opioids and NSAIDs. The review included six randomized controlled trials and two case-series, indicating that lidocaine was as effective as or better than active controls in some studies, but not effective for migraine headaches. Safety data showed a low incidence of serious adverse events. The authors conclude that the evidence does not support routine use of IV lidocaine for pain management in the ED, and more robust research is needed. In practice, IV lidocaine may be considered when opioids and NSAIDs are contraindicated, but its routine use is not recommended based on current evidence.

## Case
A 42-year-old man presents to the emergency department (ED) with a chief complaint of severe right flank pain that radiates to his groin of three hours duration. The patient also reports nausea, an increase in frequency of urination, and blood in his urine. He is writhing in pain and on physical examination, he was noted to have severe right flank tenderness to palpation and tenderness at the right costo-vertebral angle. You entertain the clinical diagnosis of renal colic and proceed with ordering 10mg intravenous ketorolac. On re-assessment at 20 minutes post-analgesia, the patient is still complaining of a moderate degree of pain and states that morphine worked well in the past, but he is wondering if you can take away his pain without an opioid. While contemplating your next analgesic choice you suddenly remember listening to a podcast that discussed the role of intravenous lidocaine in managing pain of renal colic origin. You reassured the patient that you will check on the feasibility of other non-opioid analgesics, but at the same time you decided to quickly review available data on IV Lidocaine utility for acute pain in the ED.

## Background
Pain is a prevalent reason for emergency department visits, necessitating effective management through various pharmacological and non-pharmacological methods. Lidocaine, a local anesthetic targeting sodium channels, is commonly used for topical and local anesthesia due to its analgesic, anti-hyperalgesic, and anti-inflammatory properties. It has a short half-life of 60 to 120 minutes and generally predictable adverse effects. Intravenous lidocaine is proposed as an alternative for pain control, particularly when opioids or NSAIDs are ineffective or cause adverse effects.

## Clinical Point
Is administration of intravenous lidocaine safe and effective in managing patients presenting to the emergency department with acute or chronic pain?

## SGEM Bottom Line
The currently available evidence is not strong enough to support the routine use of IV lidocaine for analgesia in the emergency department.

## Case Resolution
You proceed with ordering IV morphine with nearly complete resolution of pain while in the ED and subsequent discharge home with urological follow-up.

## Clinical Application
The clinical utility of IV lidocaine for managing acute pain in the ED is yet to be determined by conducting more robust research with broader inclusion criteria (elderly and those with pre-existing cardiac disease) and pain syndromes. However, on a case-by-case basis in situations when opioids and NSAIDs are contraindicated and/or intolerable, intravenous lidocaine might be considered for pain control.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
One of the non-opioid analgesics that I considered ordering for you does not have good evidential support for its pain relief and safety in the ED. Therefore, I am going to proceed with IV morphine.
