## Summary
In the case of a 21-year-old male with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) and increased intracranial pressure (ICP), the clinical decision was made to administer mannitol, a hyperosmolar solution, en route to a CT scan. This decision aligns with current clinical practice guidelines that recommend mannitol as the gold standard for managing elevated ICP in TBI. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, including 1,820 patients, found no mortality benefit or improved ICP control with hypertonic saline compared to other solutions like mannitol. Despite hypertonic saline's theoretical benefits, such as volume expansion and osmotic effects, the evidence does not support its use as a first-line treatment for severe TBI. The patient was subsequently found to have a large epidural hemorrhage and was taken for emergency surgical intervention. The systematic review highlighted the lack of significant differences in primary and secondary outcomes between hypertonic saline and other treatments, as well as the potential underreporting of adverse events.

## Case
A 21-year-old male is standing on the corner, minding his own business (SOCMOB) when he is hit in the head with a bat and suffers a severe traumatic brain injury. He is brought into the trauma room and appears to have an isolated head injury. He does not open his eyes to pain, does not speak, and he withdraws to painful stimuli. His Glasgow Coma Score is 6 and after intubating him, his left pupil is sluggish and 5 mm while his right is 3 mm and reactive. You decide to give a hyperosmolar solution for his suspected increased intracranial pressure (ICP) while he is being transported to the CT scanner. You ask for a bag of mannitol, but someone asks whether hypertonic saline will be more effective for this patient?

## Background
Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a prevalent condition in Canada, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates, often leading to increased intracranial pressure (ICP) which can cause secondary cerebral injuries due to decreased cerebral perfusion pressure and ischemia. Increased ICP is closely linked to mortality and poor neurological outcomes. Standard interventions for managing ICP include cerebrospinal fluid drainage via external ventricular drains, decompressive craniectomy to allow brain expansion, and barbiturate coma as a last resort when other treatments fail. Hyperosmolar therapy, particularly with mannitol, is the recommended approach for treating increased ICP, with mannitol being the gold standard. Hypertonic saline solutions are also gaining support due to their volume expansion and osmotic effects.

## Clinical Point
What are the clinical benefits and harms associated with the use of hypertonic saline when compared to any alternative solution in patients with severe traumatic brain injury?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Hypertonic saline as a first line treatment for patients with severe traumatic brain injury cannot be recommended at this time.

## Case Resolution
You give the patient mannitol en route to the CT scanner and his pupil remains sluggish but does not dilate further. He has a large epidural hemorrhage and is taken directly to the operating room for drainage.

## Clinical Application
Chris will continue to use mannitol for the management of elevated ICP in traumatic brain injury patients

**What do I tell my patient?**
In this case we’d likely be speaking to the family members. I would tell them the patient had sustained a serious life threatening brain injury. We are doing everything we can to help. The CT scan shows a serious bleed in the brain. The neurosurgeons are taking him for emergency surgery now. They will be able to explain more once he is out of surgery.
