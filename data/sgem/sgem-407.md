## Summary
The document discusses a case of a 27-year-old patient with a forearm abscess related to opioid injection use, highlighting the importance of harm reduction strategies in the emergency department (ED) for individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD). The focus is on the distribution of fentanyl test strips (FTS) as a harm reduction tool to prevent opioid overdoses, particularly in the context of fentanyl contamination in illicit drugs. A qualitative study by Reed et al. evaluated ED staff perceptions of FTS distribution, revealing positive experiences but also identifying barriers to integration into ED workflows. The study suggests that FTS distribution can improve patient rapport and provide critical overdose prevention tools, though challenges remain in routine implementation. The case resolution includes prescribing antibiotics for cellulitis and discussing harm reduction techniques, including the use of FTS, with the patient.

## Case
A 27-year-old right hand dominant patient presents to the emergency department (ED) with a 2.5 cm left forearm abscess. They have no fever, chills, or signs of compartment syndrome. You perform an incision and drainage of the abscess with significant improvement in pain. The patient reports injection use of opioids, last use was a few hours ago. The patient currently has no signs of withdrawal and is interested in potentially starting on methadone; however, the patient is not ready to start the medication right now.

## Background
Opioid overdose deaths in the United States have been increasingly linked to fentanyl contamination in illicit opioids. Emergency Departments (EDs) serve as critical intervention points for individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD), offering opportunities for initiating treatments such as buprenorphine or methadone. However, for patients not ready for these medications, harm reduction strategies are essential. Fentanyl test strips (FTS) are a harm reduction tool that can help reduce overdose deaths by allowing people who use drugs to test for the presence of fentanyl before consumption, thereby informing safer usage decisions. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) supports enhanced harm reduction education for emergency physicians to better address this public health issue.

## SGEM Bottom Line
Although the clinical impact of fentanyl test strips is uncertain, staff led distribution in the ED appears feasible.

## Case Resolution
You prescribe antibiotics for the mild cellulitis around the abscess. You provide take home naloxone and discuss harm reduction techniques including rotating injection sites, where to access new needles, use of alcohol pads, and potential for fentanyl test strips.

## Clinical Application
Although the clinical utility of fentanyl test strips in an undifferentiated population of people who use drugs in the ED is unknown, there are several barriers to implementing this intervention in the ED.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
Until you are ready to stop using drugs, there are several things you can do to reduce your risk of getting very sick, a bad infection, or dying. One of the things you may be interested in are test strips, which can detect fentanyl. If the drugs test positive for fentanyl, you are at increased risk of overdose.
