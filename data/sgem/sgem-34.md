## Summary
A 66-year-old man with fever, lethargy, and headache underwent a lumbar puncture (LP) to evaluate for meningitis, highlighting the procedure's diagnostic utility in assessing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for infections. The document reviews techniques to minimize post-LP complications, particularly headaches, suggesting the use of small-gauge atraumatic needles and reinsertion of the stylet before needle withdrawal. It emphasizes that routine bed rest post-LP is not beneficial, and mobilization is recommended. The necessity of pre-LP CT scans remains debated, with no universal guideline, relying instead on clinical judgment. The summary underscores the importance of optimizing LP techniques and training to improve procedural success and patient outcomes.

## Case Scenario
A 66-year-old man presents with a 48-hour history of fever, lethargy, and headache. He has no significant past medical history. On physical examination, he has a temperature of 38.8C, GCS 15, stiff neck on flexion, and no rash. Urinalysis and CXR are normal. Laboratory testing reports an elevated WBC with a left shift. You decide he needs a lumbar puncture (LP) to check for meningitis.

## Point
How to perform the lumbar puncture

## Background
Lumbar puncture (LP), first described by Quincke in 1891, is a diagnostic procedure used to sample cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for conditions such as infection and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Despite its utility, LP can lead to complications, with post-LP headache being common and more severe adverse events including cerebral herniation, intracranial subdural hemorrhage, spinal epidural hemorrhage, and infection. A debated issue is the necessity of pre-LP computed tomography (CT) scans, with no evidence supporting universal neuroimaging prior to LP. Clinical judgment is recommended, though not clearly defined. Studies by Gopal et al. (1999) and Hasbun et al. (2001) explored this issue, involving patients with a median age of 42 and 40, respectively, with a significant portion being immunocompromised. However, these studies have not been validated in independent populations.

## BEEM Bottom Line
The following procedures may decrease the risk of post-LP headache:
- Small-gauge atraumatic needles
- Reinsertion of the stylet prior to the removal of the spinal needle
- Mobilization of patients after completing the LP

## Case Resolution
You perform a successful LP and send off the CSF to the lab for analysis to rule out meningitis.
