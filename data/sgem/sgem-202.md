## Summary
A 45-year-old male presented with classic symptoms of renal colic, including severe right-sided flank pain radiating to the groin, nausea, vomiting, and difficulty urinating. The document discusses the diagnostic dilemma between using CT and ultrasound, with ultrasound being favored for lower radiation exposure. Pain management typically involves narcotics and NSAIDs, with recent evidence suggesting a ceiling effect for ketorolac at 10mg IV. The study by Soleimanpour et al. compared IV lidocaine to IV morphine for pain control in renal colic, finding lidocaine more effective, though the study's methodological flaws limit the strength of this conclusion. The SGEM review advises against recommending lidocaine based on current evidence, highlighting the need for more rigorous trials. The patient in the case was successfully managed with ketorolac and discharged with instructions for expectant management and follow-up.

## Case
A 45-year-old male presents to the emergency department with sudden onset severe right-sided flank pain radiating to his groin that began one hour ago. He has also had nausea, vomiting, and difficulty urinating. He has never had anything like this before and is writhing around in pain, unable to get comfortable.

## Background
Renal colic, affecting 1-5% of the US population, is characterized by sudden flank pain radiating to the lower abdomen, often with microscopic hematuria, nausea, and vomiting. Diagnostic approaches include ultrasound, which reduces radiation exposure compared to CT without compromising diagnostic accuracy or patient outcomes. Pain management typically involves narcotics and intravenous NSAIDs, with recent findings indicating that 10mg IV ketorolac is as effective as higher doses. Although alpha blockers have been explored for treatment, they lack high-quality evidence of efficacy. Lidocaine presents a potential non-narcotic alternative for managing pain, aligning with efforts to reduce opioid use amid the US opioid epidemic.

## Clinical Point
In emergency department patients with renal colic, is IV lidocaine as or more effective than IV narcotics for pain control?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Based on this study, lidocaine cannot be recommended for the treatment of renal colic.

## Case Resolution
The patient has a bedside ultrasound performed by the emergency department physician. It demonstrates mild right hydronephrosis. He is provided with ketorolac 10mg IV and his pain is resolved. The patient is discharged home with pain control, expectant management and good instructions on when to return to the emergency department.

## Clinical Application
This article did not provide convincing evidence that lidocaine works. I am not even sure that this treatment would be defensible if it was given for renal colic and someone had a bad outcome. People have reported cases of it working beautifully. There has been a smaller RCT by Firouzian et al in Am J Emerg Medicine. It looked at IV morphine +/- IV lidocaine and found no statistically significant differences were observed between groups for the primary outcome of pain on the VAS. It too had some methodological issues. It would be interesting to perform a methodologically rigorous multicenter randomized control trial to tease this thing out.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
You have a kidney stone that is causing some mild swelling in your kidney. There does not appear to be any infection. Most kidney stones will pass on their own in about 1-2 weeks and it can be painful. We can treat your pain with some anti-inflammatory drugs. If that does not work you can also use some opioid pain pills as a back-up plan. We will also give you some anti-nausea medications. If your pain is not controlled, you are vomiting, develop a fever or are otherwise worried please come back to the emergency department. Otherwise, we will set up an outpatient appointment with a urologist for follow-up.
