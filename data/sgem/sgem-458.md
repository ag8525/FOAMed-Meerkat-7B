## Summary
In the emergency department setting, managing acute pain, particularly in patients with chronic opioid use, presents significant challenges. A recent study by Galili et al. explored the efficacy of low-dose ketamine (LDK) as an adjunct to morphine for acute pain control. This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that LDK, when added to morphine, significantly improved pain reduction at 10, 20, and 30 minutes post-administration compared to morphine alone, although this effect diminished by 45 minutes. Despite increased adverse effects such as nausea and dissociation, patient and provider satisfaction did not differ significantly between groups. The study suggests that LDK may enhance short-term pain relief in the ED, but further research is needed to solidify its role, especially in patients with chronic opioid use. The case resolution highlighted the practical application of this approach, providing effective analgesia for a patient with a complex forearm fracture.

## Case
You are hitting the zone in your shift, a veritable disposition machine meeting the constant flow of patients through the emergency department (ED). As you finish a note on a complex critically ill patient, you hear howls of pain coming from the EMS triage desk. A 38-year-old male patient in cycling gear is being brought in after colliding with a lamp post with an obvious upper extremity deformity. He explains loudly to the triage nurse that he has chronic back pain from multiple prior cycling accidents, and regularly takes significant doses of extended-release oxycodone. As you walk over to assess the patient, you take note of how distressed he is and consider how to get him comfortable enough to work him up in the ED.

## Background
Acute pain management in emergency departments is crucial yet challenging, particularly due to the limitations and risks associated with opioid and non-opioid analgesics. The overuse of opioids has led to a shift towards more cautious prescribing practices, emphasizing the need for effective pain relief strategies that minimize opioid use. A multi-modal approach, incorporating adjuvant non-opioid medications, is recommended to enhance pain control while reducing opioid doses. Ketamine, a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist traditionally used as a dissociative anesthetic, has gained popularity in emergency settings for its ability to provide sedation, analgesia, and amnesia without compromising hemodynamic stability or airway reflexes. Recently, sub-dissociative doses of ketamine have been effectively employed for acute pain management, offering a promising alternative to traditional opioid therapies.

## Clinical Point
Is ketamine an effective adjunct agent along with morphine for acute pain control in the ED?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Adjunct use of ketamine may be a useful part of the ED physician’s analgesia toolbox for acute pain, but further study is needed to establish its role and support protocols for its use.

## Case Resolution
Our patient received weight-based doses of morphine and ketamine with reasonably good effect, providing enough analgesia to obtain imaging and diagnose his complex forearm fracture. He is appreciative of better analgesia than he has experienced with his prior accidents and asks you when he might be able to get another “slug of the good stuff.”

## Clinical Application
Expanding the toolset of analgesia in acute pain is always a boon to ED physicians. We can consider adding LDK to our multi-modal analgesia strategy in the emergency department.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
I understand you are in a great deal of pain, and we are going to do our best to help you feel better. We can try a combination of medications to work in different ways to get you as much out of pain as possible – but rest assured we will come back and check on you and change course if our plan is not working.
