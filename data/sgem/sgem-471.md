## Summary
The document discusses the challenges and accuracy of the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) version 4 in pediatric triage within emergency departments. It highlights that pediatric triage is complex due to physiological and developmental differences, leading to high mistriage rates, with only about 34% of children correctly triaged. The study, conducted across 21 EDs, found that both undertriage and overtriage are common, influenced by factors such as age, comorbidities, and mode of arrival. The authors suggest that while ESI is widely used, it is not always accurate, and there is a need for improved methods to better identify critically ill children and accurately assess low-acuity cases. The study emphasizes focusing on clinically significant mistriage to enhance patient outcomes and resource allocation.

## Case
You are approached by the medical director of your emergency department (ED). She has noticed that recently, there has been an increasing number of pediatric cases presenting to your facility. In some of these cases, the children ended up being more sick than initially triaged. As the institution’s evidence-based medicine enthusiast, she asks you, "What do you think of the triage system we’re using now? How accurate is it for children?"

## Background
Pediatric triage is a critical aspect of emergency medicine, focusing on the initial assessment and management of acutely ill or injured children in the emergency department. It differs from adult triage due to the unique physiological, developmental, and communication challenges presented by younger patients. The Emergency Severity Index (ESI) is the predominant triage system in the United States, categorizing patients from Level 1, requiring immediate life-saving intervention, to Level 5, needing no resources beyond physician evaluation. Pediatric triage is particularly challenging due to age-related vital sign variations, difficulties in obtaining accurate histories, and non-specific presentations of critical illnesses. ESI Levels 1 and 2 assess acuity and risk of instability, while Levels 3, 4, and 5 are based on expected resource needs. Despite its widespread use, the ESI system has a high mistriage rate, with studies indicating up to 50% of pediatric cases being inaccurately triaged. Undertriage can delay care for critically ill children, while overtriage can lead to unnecessary resource use, increased costs, and ED crowding. Both errors are common, especially in younger children and those with atypical symptoms, posing significant risks in environments with prolonged wait times and staffing shortages.

## Clinical Point
How accurate is ESI version 4 in predicting acuity and resource needs among pediatric ED patients?

## SGEM Bottom Line
We need better and more accurate methods for triaging children in the emergency department.

## Case Resolution
You tell the medical director that ESI levels are not always accurate for the triage of pediatric patients. However, not all mistriage has clinically significant consequences. Rates of mistriage may be highly variable based on the triage nurse’s experience and training with pediatric patients. At this time, you recommend directing efforts towards identifying factors in previous instances when children were mistriaged that resulted in harm and focusing efforts to address those factors.

## Clinical Application
ESI levels are the most popular method of ED triage in the United States. They are not always accurate, but that may also be due to the nature of triage. Accurate triage helps with prioritizing patients and allocating resources. When evaluating triage, we should focus on capturing cases of clinically significant mistriage.

**What Do I Tell My Medical Director?**
The ESI system is one of the most popular in the United States. You’re right that it isn’t always accurate for the triage of pediatric patients. I don’t know of any specific triage system to recommend over the ESI system currently as the triage process itself is challenging because we’re making decisions based on little information in a short period. I think we should look at the cases of mistriage that resulted in clinically significant harm and see what factors may have impacted those. In the meantime, I will keep an eye out for any further studies that may suggest that there’s a better way to triage kids.
