## Summary
In this clinical scenario, a 40-year-old male with a shoulder dislocation requires procedural sedation, and the use of ketofol (a combination of ketamine and propofol) is considered. A study by Miner et al. compared the efficacy and safety of propofol alone versus ketofol in 1:1 and 4:1 ratios for procedural sedation in adults. The primary outcome was the incidence of airway or respiratory adverse events requiring intervention, with secondary outcomes including oxygen saturation, procedural success, recovery agitation, pain scale, memory, and patient satisfaction. The study found no significant difference in adverse respiratory events between propofol alone and either ketofol ratio, though recovery agitation was more common with ketofol. The authors concluded that ketofol does not offer a greater than 20% benefit over propofol alone in reducing adverse events. In practice, propofol was chosen for its simplicity and reduced risk of medication errors, with successful shoulder reduction achieved without respiratory complications.

## Case
A 40-year-old male comes to the Emergency Department with a dislocated shoulder while playing football. He’s a weekend warrior and has never had any shoulder dislocations in the past. It took him about an hour to get to the Emergency Department. After some pain control, you try the Cunningham and scapular manipulation to get the shoulder back in but they are not working. You decide that he needs procedural sedation. One of your residents asks you, “I’ve heard about this ketofol thing, can we try it in this patient?”

## Background
Ketofol is a combination of ketamine and propofol increasingly used in emergency medicine, particularly in pediatric settings, due to its complementary pharmacological effects. Ketamine, known for its analgesic properties at sub-dissociative doses, can elevate blood pressure and maintain respiratory drive, counteracting propofol's potential to induce hypotension and apnea. Conversely, propofol's antiemetic properties can mitigate the emetic effects associated with ketamine. This combination is typically administered in a single syringe, although separate administration is also practiced, aiming to balance the adverse effects of each drug and enhance procedural outcomes.

## Clinical Point
How does propofol compare to ketofol mixed at a 1:1 ratio compared to ketofol mixed at 4:1 (propofol to ketamine) for adults requiring procedural sedation in the Emergency Department?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Ketofol in a 1:1 or 4:1 ratio does not appear to have a greater than 20% benefit (less adverse respiratory/airway events) compared to propofol alone.

## Case Resolution
You and the resident both take a look at this article and decide to proceed with just propofol for the sedation since it will require less nursing time and less potential for medication errors in mixing as compared to propofol. As always, you do a good pre-procedure assessment of the airway as well as doing a good history and physical. You get all of your airway equipment to the bedside, check your equipment and size it appropriately. You also have the RSI kit at the bedside just in case. The patient receives the propofol and you successfully reduce the patient’s shoulder on the first attempt without any respiratory issues.

## Clinical Application
I am generally going to use IV opioids for pain control followed by propofol for procedural sedations.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We are going to use propofol to sedate you to help put your shoulder back in. Propofol is a very safe drug that we have a lot of experience using in our Emergency Department. Side effects are rare and may include having to breathe for you with a mask or put a tube down your throat but we will watch you closely and be prepared for any possible problem ahead of time.
