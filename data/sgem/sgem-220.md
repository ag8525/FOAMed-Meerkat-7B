## Summary
A randomized controlled trial compared acupuncture to titrated morphine for pain control in patients with renal colic, finding no significant difference in success rates but a faster resolution time favoring acupuncture. Despite fewer adverse events in the acupuncture group, the study's lack of blinding and potential biases, such as cultural acceptance and placebo effects, limit its validity. The trial's design, including the absence of a sham acupuncture group, raises questions about the placebo effect's role. The study's conclusions are challenged due to methodological flaws and lack of biological plausibility, suggesting acupuncture is not superior to morphine for acute pain management in the emergency department.

## Case
A 51-year-old man presents to the emergency department complaining of right flank pain radiating to his groin like his previous episodes of renal colic. He states the pain comes in “waves,” is associated with nausea but no vomiting. On exam, he is afebrile and appears very uncomfortable while grabbing his right flank. He has used ketorolac, acetaminophen, and tamsulosin in the past. He really wants to avoid taking any opioids and is wondering if acupuncture could work?

## Background
Ureteric stones smaller than 5mm typically do not require expulsive therapy, while tamsulosin may aid in the passage of larger stones between 5 to 10mm, though evidence is weak. Increased fluid intake or diuretics are not necessary for kidney stone passage. Bedside emergency department ultrasound is a safe and advantageous diagnostic tool for kidney stones compared to CT scans. Currently, lidocaine is not recommended for treating renal colic, and there is no high-quality evidence supporting acupuncture for emergency department presentations of back pain, ankle sprains, or migraines. Additionally, acupuncture has not been proven effective in preventing migraine recurrence in patients without aura, although some claims suggest its efficacy in treating renal colic pain.

## Clinical Point
In adult patients presenting to the emergency department with renal colic, is acupuncture superior to morphine for pain control?

## SGEM Bottom Line
This trial does not support the claim that acupuncture works or is superior to morphine for adult patients presenting to the emergency department.

## Case Resolution
You give 10mg IV ketorolac for the pain and 8mg IV of ondansetron for the nausea. He is re-assessed in 30 minutes without much relief. You discuss using sub-dissociated doses of ketamine to minimize the amount of opioid. He agrees and ends up having his pain resolved.

## Clinical Application
Acupuncture is based on meridians that have never been proven to exist. This seriously calls into Point the biological plausibility of manipulating meridians to improve qi (chi). The Bayesian pre-test probability of acupuncture working is very low and would require a large effect size to demonstrate efficacy. This is another low-quality study that does not demonstrate acupuncture is useful for acute pain. It is wrong to use the serious opioid problem to justify a therapy which has not been proven to work.

**What Do I Tell the Patient?**
It would be great if acupuncture had been shown to work in renal colic. Unfortunately, there is no good evidence to support it being effective. We will try to get your pain down using medications proven to work and even have some new options available that can minimize opioid use.
