## Summary
The document discusses the association between early intravenous (IV) fluid administration by paramedics and in-hospital mortality among patients with sepsis. It highlights a study that found IV fluids provided by paramedics were associated with reduced in-hospital mortality for patients with sepsis and hypotension, but not for those with higher initial systolic blood pressure. The study involved nearly 150,000 patients, identifying 1,871 with sepsis, and found a 28% overall mortality rate. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends rapid administration of 30ml/kg of crystalloid for hypotension, though this is based on low-quality evidence. The study's observational nature and missing data are noted limitations, and a randomized control trial is suggested for more definitive conclusions. The document concludes that while there is an association, the patient-oriented benefit of pre-hospital IV fluids in sepsis remains uncertain.

## Case
A 77-year-old man’s partner calls 911 because he has a fever, cough, shortness of breath, and lethargy. The patient is known to have hypertension and dyslipidemia. Paramedics arrive quickly and find a man in bed with a temperature of 39.5C, heart rate of 111 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation of 91%, and a blood pressure of 98/56. They suspect a respiratory infection, provided supplemental oxygen with a target of 94-96%, establish IV access, and begin a 500cc normal saline bolus.

## Background
Sepsis is a critical medical condition characterized by a systemic inflammatory response to infection, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates. A study by Alam et al investigated the efficacy of administering pre-hospital antibiotics, specifically ceftriaxone 2g IV, to patients with suspected infections, finding no significant mortality benefit at 28 days in an optimized EMS system. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) provides guidelines for sepsis management, including a strong recommendation for the rapid administration of 30ml/kg of crystalloid for hypotension, despite being based on low-quality evidence. The SSC's 2018 update, which combined the 3-hour and 6-hour treatment bundles into a 1-hour bundle, has been controversial, prompting a petition for retraction of these guidelines.

## Clinical Point
Is IV fluid administration by Paramedics for patients with suspected sepsis associated with reduced in-hospital mortality rates?

## SGEM Bottom Line
In patients suspected of sepsis, we don’t know if pre-hospital IV fluids will result in a patient-oriented benefit.

## Case Resolution
The patient arrives at the ED with a temperature of 37.9C, HR 98bpm, RR 20bmp, SpO2 95% with 2L nasal prongs, and a blood pressure of 106/70. You relay your suspicions of sepsis secondary to pneumonia and the ED staff start working up the patient.

## Clinical Application
Paramedics have different medical directives in different jurisdictions. In Ontario, there is no standing medical direction for IV fluid administration for septic patients. This represents a barrier for implementing such a protocol in our EMS system. This patient was hypotensive and there are protocols for providing IV fluid boluses in these cases.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
We think you have an infection in your lungs causing a fever and your blood pressure to be low. We are going to start an IV and give you some fluids. I will call ahead to the hospital to let them know we are coming, and by this IV already being in place, it will speed up your care.
