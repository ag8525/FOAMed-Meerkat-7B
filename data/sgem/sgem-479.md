## Summary
The document discusses the application of the Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) clinical decision rule for assessing intra-abdominal injuries (IAI) in children following blunt trauma. The PECARN rule, which includes seven clinical variables, aims to reduce unnecessary CT scans by identifying children at low risk for clinically significant IAIs. The study highlights that children with one or two positive PECARN variables, particularly those with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score below 14, are at higher risk for IAI requiring intervention. However, isolated symptoms such as abdominal pain, vomiting, or tenderness were not strongly associated with significant IAI. The document emphasizes the importance of using the PECARN tool alongside clinical judgment, as not all positive variables necessitate CT imaging, and highlights the potential for observation and serial examinations as alternative management strategies.

## Case
A ten-year-old boy presents to your emergency department (ED) after being involved in a motor vehicle collision at high speed. Emergency Medical Service (EMS) tells you that he was properly restrained. His parents were also in the vehicle and are currently being brought to the ED as well. He appeared a bit dazed initially, but he has had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15 throughout transport. Your primary survey is unremarkable. He complains of some abdominal pain, although you note a soft abdomen on exam and no seatbelt sign. As you complete your secondary survey, he vomits once, which is non-bloody. A medical trainee working with says to you, “He says his stomach hurts and threw up. Do you think we need to CT scan his abdomen?”

## Background
Intra-abdominal injury (IAI) in children, particularly from blunt trauma, is a critical concern due to its potential life-threatening nature, despite being less common than in adults. The spleen, liver, and kidneys are the most frequently injured organs, but any abdominal organ can be affected. Diagnosing IAIs in children is challenging due to often subtle clinical presentations and unreliable physical examinations. Computed tomography (CT) is the diagnostic gold standard, but its use must be judicious due to the risks of ionizing radiation, which poses a small but significant lifetime cancer risk. To enhance diagnostic accuracy and reduce unnecessary CT scans, clinical decision rules (CDRs) like the Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) rule have been developed. This rule uses seven clinical variables to identify children at very low risk of clinically significant IAIs, thereby potentially avoiding CT scans when all variables are negative. However, the application of these tools in cases where only one or two criteria are positive remains a clinical challenge, highlighting the need for further refinement in pediatric trauma care decision-making.

## Clinical Point
What is the risk for intraabdominal injuries requiring acute intervention (IAIAI) in children with one or two positive PECARN intraabdominal injury rule variables?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Consider using the PECARN clinical decision tool in conjunction with your clinical judgement when evaluating children with blunt abdominal injury. Children with one or two positive variables in the CDR **do not** necessarily require CT imaging.

## Case Resolution
During the boy’s stay in the ED, you and the trainee assessed him multiple times. His reported abdominal pain seems to improve, and he does not have any further episodes of emesis. Your repeat examinations reassure you that the chances of him having a clinically significant intra-abdominal injury are low. His aunt arrives at the ED. You fill her in on the patient’s ED course, and she takes the patient home.

## Clinical Application
The PECARN clinical decision tool can be used to risk-stratify children presenting with blunt intra-abdominal injury. If the child is negative for all seven variables, there is a very low chance of them having an intraabdominal injury that requires urgent intervention. Even if one or two of the variables are positive, this does not necessarily mean that the child needs to undergo a CT scan. Use this decision tool in conjunction with your clinical judgement.

**What Do I Tell My Trainee?**
I’m glad you asked that Point. There are several ways a child presenting with blunt intra-abdominal injury can be evaluated. Those include lab tests, ultrasound, CT scans, serial exams and observation. The PECARN clinical decision tool risk stratifies these children based on seven variables from the history and physical examination. Even if one or two of these variables are positive, this doesn’t mean we automatically need to put the child through the scanner. Let’s continue to re-evaluate him while he is under our care in the emergency department.
