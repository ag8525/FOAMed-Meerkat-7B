## Summary
The ENDAO Trial, a randomized controlled study, evaluated the efficacy of apneic oxygenation during rapid sequence intubation (RSI) in the emergency department setting. The trial included 200 patients and compared apneic oxygenation to standard pre-oxygenation methods, finding no significant difference in the primary outcome of mean lowest oxygen saturation (92% vs. 93%, p=0.08) or secondary outcomes such as desaturation rates below 90% or 80%, first pass success, and mortality. Despite physiological rationale and previous observational studies suggesting benefits, this trial did not demonstrate a reduction in desaturation or peri-intubation complications with apneic oxygenation. The study's limitations include its single-center design, potential statistical underpowering, and the high competency of the intubation team, which may not reflect broader clinical settings. The authors concluded that while apneic oxygenation may have a role, it likely adds little benefit when effective pre-oxygenation strategies are employed.

## Case
A 68-year-old female presents with shortness of breath. She has experienced fever, chills, and a productive cough worsening over the past five days. Chest X-ray demonstrated bilateral infiltrates consistent with multifocal pneumonia.

Over her emergency department course, the patient starts to decompensate despite high-flow nasal cannula and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The decision is made to intubate the patient. The Point arises whether apneic oxygenation should be used during the rapid sequence intubation.

## Background
Apneic oxygenation is a technique used to maintain oxygenation during periods of apnea, particularly during intubation, by providing continuous oxygen flow. It is widely adopted in emergency medicine and critical care due to its minimal cost and sound physiological basis. While some studies in various clinical settings, including the emergency department, have shown benefits such as increased first pass success and reduced hypoxemia during rapid sequence intubation, particularly in patients with intracranial hemorrhage, the overall evidence is mixed. The FELLOW Trial, a randomized controlled study conducted in the intensive care unit, found no significant difference in desaturation rates between apneic oxygenation and usual care, highlighting the need for further research to confirm its efficacy in different clinical environments.

## Clinical Point
Does the use of apneic oxygenation in the emergency department during rapid sequence intubation decrease the rate of clinically important desaturation, leading to a decrease in peri-intubation complications and death?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Apneic oxygenation may still have a role during rapid sequence intubation of emergency department patients, but it likely adds little when proper pre-oxygenation strategies are used.

## Clinical Application
If we were guaranteed successful endotracheal intubation with minimal delay in all our patients, then the majority of our preparation would be rendered superfluous. However, we have an undifferentiated cohort presenting to the emergency department. As with much of our preparation, apneic oxygenation is employed universally in the event of the rare case of unexpected difficulty. And while its utility in these patients is unknown, an underpowered trial, in which so few patients experienced the outcome in Point, does not disprove the potential benefits of apneic oxygenation.

## Case Resolution (Rory)
The patient was pre-oxygenated using a nasal cannula underneath a BiPAP circuit, and the cannula at 15 L/min was maintained during the apneic period. The patient was intubated on the first pass without hypoxia and was resuscitated and transferred to the intensive care unit.

## Case Resolution (Ken)
The patient was pre-oxygenated using a bag valve mask and was successfully intubated on the first pass without hypoxia and was resuscitated and transferred to the intensive care unit.
