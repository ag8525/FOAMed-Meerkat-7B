## Summary
The document discusses the use of therapeutic hypothermia in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) patients, specifically evaluating the efficacy of rapid cooling with cold saline by EMS during CPR. Despite previous studies suggesting neuroprotective benefits of hypothermia post-cardiac arrest, the RINSE trial found no significant improvement in survival to hospital discharge or neurologic outcomes with pre-hospital cooling. The trial, which included 1,198 patients, showed no difference in primary outcomes between the cooling group and standard care, and even suggested a decreased rate of return of spontaneous circulation in patients with shockable rhythms who received cooling. The study was stopped early due to the publication of the TTM trial, which also questioned the benefit of targeted temperature management. The conclusion is that pre-hospital cooling does not provide a patient-oriented benefit and is not recommended, reinforcing the need for further research to establish effective interventions for OHCA.

## Case
A 71-year-old woman has a witnessed arrest while watching her granddaughter’s soccer game. Bystander CPR is started and EMS is called. They arrive quickly and take over the resuscitation. She is not in a shockable rhythm. They continue CPR, get intravenous access, give a round of epinephrine and then wonder if they should start rapid cooling en-route to the hospital with some cold saline.

## Background
Therapeutic hypothermia, also known as targeted temperature management, is a medical intervention used post-cardiac arrest to provide neuroprotection. Initially gaining attention in 2002, it was supported by two randomized control trials indicating improved neurological outcomes. The Cochrane Collaboration's 2012 review endorsed mild therapeutic hypothermia for enhancing survival and neurological outcomes after cardiac arrest, aligning with International Resuscitation Guidelines. However, skepticism arose regarding prehospital cooling for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), as studies showed no patient-oriented benefits. The 2013 Targeted Temperature Management (TTM) Trial further indicated no significant survival advantage between maintaining a body temperature of 33°C versus 36°C in OHCA patients, challenging the previously assumed benefits of more aggressive cooling strategies.

## Clinical Point
Does rapid cooling by EMS with large volumes of 4C normal saline improve outcome in patients with OHCA receiving CPR?

## SGEM Bottom Line
We do not have good evidence that providing pre-hospital cooling to patients with OHCA receiving CPR has a patient-oriented benefit and therefore cannot be recommended at this time.

## Case Resolution
The 71-year-old woman with the out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is not cooled in the field. You get ROSC, transport her to hospital and hope she survives to hospital discharge neurologically intact.

## Clinical Application
This is another study reinforcing that cooling pre-hospital by EMS for OHCA should not be performed.

**What do I tell my patient?**
I will tell the patient’s partner that her wife had a cardiac arrest. We were able to bring her back but she is still unconscious. The prognosis is poor but the emergency department staff will do what they can.
