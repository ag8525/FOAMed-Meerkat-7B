## Summary
The study by James et al. investigates the utilization of emergency department (ED) services by deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) patients, specifically comparing American Sign Language (ASL) users and DHH English speakers to non-DHH English speakers. The retrospective chart review found no significant differences in Emergency Severity Index (ESI), triage pain scores, or acute ED revisits among the groups. However, DHH ASL users experienced a longer ED length of stay (LOS) by approximately 9% compared to non-DHH English speakers, highlighting potential care discrepancies due to interpreter availability. The study underscores the importance of efficient interpreter services to ensure equitable care for DHH patients in the ED.

## Case
One night you grab the next patient on the board, and upon entering the room introduce yourself as you normally would. The patient waves at you and gestures to a friend in the room, who explains that the patient is deaf and needs a sign language interpreter. You know from your brief look at the triage report that the patient’s vitals are stable and their chief complaint isn’t likely to be immediately life threatening, so you politely explain you’ll be back, and go find the charge nurse to obtain interpreter services.

## Background
Deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) individuals face significant health inequities and social disparities, often attributed to audism, which privileges non-DHH individuals. This population is more likely to utilize emergency department (ED) services compared to non-DHH individuals, yet research on ED outcomes for DHH patients is limited. DHH patients are diverse, with those experiencing adult-onset hearing loss often lacking proficiency in American Sign Language (ASL). DHH ASL users may encounter delays in care due to the availability of interpreters, potentially leading to discrepancies in the quality of care received.

## Clinical Point
How do deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) American Sign Language speakers and DHH English speakers utilize the ED, specifically regarding acuity of complaints and pain, what is their ED length of stay and what is the prevalence of acute revisits?

## SGEM Bottom Line
Deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) patients present to the ED with similar acuity levels, triage pain scores and had no significant difference in acute ED revisits compared to non-DHH English speaking patients. However, there was an association observed between DHH-ASL patients and longer ED LOS compared to non-DHH English speaking patients.

## Case Resolution
Fortunately, your ED has on-site interpreter ASL services that can be accessed quickly and efficiently. This service is preferred to an online, remote interpreter system due to technical difficulties, lack of staff training, which can lead to poor patient-provider communication.

## Clinical Application
Deaf and hard of hearing patients should be triaged and treated with the same level of concern and care. Use of interpreter services is essential as with any non-English speaking patient.

Thank you for your patience while we obtained interpreter services. How may we help you today?
