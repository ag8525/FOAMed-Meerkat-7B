## Summary
The document discusses the overuse of CT scans in emergency medicine, particularly in cases of low-risk head trauma, and examines factors influencing patient preferences for imaging. It highlights a study by Iyengar et al., which found that financial incentives, along with information on potential risks and benefits, significantly affect patients' decisions to undergo CT scans. The study used a hypothetical scenario based on the Canadian CT Head Rule, showing that decreased perceived benefit, increased perceived risk, and financial incentives to forgo testing reduced the desire for CT imaging. The authors conclude that while financial incentives can influence decision-making, further research is needed to confirm these findings in real-world settings. The document emphasizes the importance of shared decision-making in cases where the necessity of imaging is unclear, although it suggests that in clear-cut cases, such as when the CCHR indicates no need for a CT, shared decision-making may not be necessary.

## Case
A 21-year-old comes into the emergency department after being knocked unconscious while playing rugby. The patient is now feeling great, or as they say in New Zealand "sweet as." He had no pain, nausea, or neurologic symptoms. His exam is normal. You aren’t worried, but his dad is the coach of the American national rugby team and says that his players always get a CT when this happens. You wonder what factors might influence a patient’s preference for imaging?

## Background
Computed tomography (CT) scans are a critical diagnostic tool in emergency medicine, enabling rapid identification of life-threatening conditions, but their overuse is a concern due to unnecessary radiation exposure and increased healthcare costs. A significant portion of head CTs, particularly in trauma cases, are deemed unnecessary when evaluated against the Canadian CT Head Rule (CCHR), which is a clinical decision instrument designed to guide imaging decisions. Recent studies suggest revising the CCHR age criteria from 65 to 75 years to potentially reduce unnecessary imaging, though further high-quality research is needed before clinical implementation. In emergency settings, uncertainty often leads to a grey zone where the benefits and harms of imaging are closely matched, necessitating shared decision-making with patients. Despite clear clinical guidelines, patient resistance and a zero-miss culture may drive the overuse of CT scans. Research by Iyengar et al. explores how financial incentives and perceived risks and benefits influence patient preferences for CT imaging in low-risk head injury scenarios.

## Clinical Point
Do financial incentives, together with potential risk and potential benefit information, influence patient preference for diagnostic testing?

## SGEM Bottom Line
The potential risk, the potential benefit, and money can influence people’s behavior in making healthcare decisions.

## Case Resolution
You explain to the patient that he is very low risk for a serious head injury based on the CCHR. After discussing the risks of CT and the negligible chance of benefit, the patient (and his dad) are happy to observe his symptoms and only get a CT if he gets worse.

## Clinical Application
I don’t think I will be offering my patients financial incentives as part of medical decision-making any time soon. However, I use shared decision-making every shift. For this patient, a patient that passes the CCHR, I wouldn’t actually perform shared decision-making, because I think the decision is clear. A CT isn’t needed. But if the choice was unclear, I would perform shared decision-making, presenting the risks and benefits in multiple formats, like the authors did here.

**What Do I Tell My Patient?**
I am not sure if you need a CT scan at this point. I think the chance that we are missing an important injury is about 1%. A CT would catch that injury, but it exposes you to radiation, and so your risk might be 0.1%. Another option would be to stay in the ED for a couple more hours so I can keep an eye on you and perform some repeat neurologic testing.
