## Summary
The study by Drennan et al. investigates the impact of high-rise living on the survival rates of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) patients, revealing that survival to hospital discharge is significantly lower for those residing on the third floor or higher. The study highlights critical delays in emergency response times due to access issues in high-rise buildings, with a notable two-minute longer response time for higher floors. Key findings include a survival rate of 4.2% for patients below the third floor compared to 2.6% for those above, with survival rates dropping to 0.9% above the 16th floor and zero above the 25th floor. Factors such as older age, male sex, and longer 911 response times were associated with lower survival, while initial shockable rhythms and witnessed arrests improved outcomes. The study underscores the need for interventions to reduce response times, such as improving bystander CPR rates, increasing AED accessibility, and facilitating first responder access to high-rise buildings.

## Case
A 43-year-old male patient calls with chest pain from the 14th floor of a downtown high-rise apartment building. After the 911 dispatcher gets the address and details of the chest pain, the patient stops responding, and the dispatcher can hear no voice. The paramedic response time is 4 minutes with lights and sirens to the apartment. The paramedics enter the controlled access area and buzz the apartment number. Of course, the patient doesn’t Point because he is vital signs absent. The paramedics look on the control panel for a building superintendent and attempt to buzz them. Of course, he isn’t in his apartment at the time, so it goes to voicemail. The paramedics start picking random names on the list to buzz. "Hi, this is the paramedics, could you please let us in?" Click, they hang up. They try another name, and another name until someone buzzes them in. Upon entering the lobby, the first elevator that opens won’t fit the stretcher, so they have to send it up and wait for the second one. After it comes down, they take it to the 14th floor, where of course the apartment door is locked. One medic goes back down to the lobby to get the superintendent’s number and after a few minutes, they arrive at the 14th floor to open the patient’s door and allow us access to the building. Ten to fifteen minutes after the arrival of the Paramedics, they gain access and meet their patient, a 43-year-old male, vital signs absent, with the phone in his hand.

## Background
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a critical medical condition characterized by the sudden cessation of cardiac activity, leading to the absence of circulation outside a hospital setting. In the United States, approximately 350,000 EMS-assessed OHCAs occur annually, predominantly affecting individuals with a median age of 65 years. These events are clinically significant due to their high mortality rate, with only about 6% of adults surviving to hospital discharge. The majority of OHCAs occur at home, with half being witnessed by bystanders or EMS providers. Of those treated by EMS, approximately 25% present with an initial shockable rhythm. The American Heart Association's 2015 CPR and ECC Guidelines emphasize a "Chain-of-Survival" approach for OHCA, which includes five critical steps: recognition and activation of the emergency response system, immediate high-quality CPR, rapid defibrillation, basic and advanced emergency medical services, and advanced life support with post-arrest care.

## Clinical Point
Is there an association between the floor of patient contact and survival of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest?

## SGEM Bottom Line
We need to find ways to maximize bystander CPR, improve access to AEDs, and eliminate barriers for first responders.

## Case Resolution
Paramedics arrive at the patient, CPR is started, defibrillator pads are applied, and the first rhythm analysis shows fine ventricular fibrillation, which is shocked once at 200 joules. An intravenous line is started, 1mg of epinephrine is given, and the next rhythm check is asystole. The patient is intubated, more epinephrine is given, and the patient has an end tidal CO2 that has dropped to 10. A call to the physician is made, a consult takes place, and the patient is pronounced dead at the scene.
